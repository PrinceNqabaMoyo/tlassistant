## Trigonometric identities

\-An identity is a mathematical statement that equates one quantity with another.

\-Trigonometric identities allow us to simplify a given expression so that it contains sine and cosine ratios only.

\-This enables us to solve equations and also to prove other identities.

### Square identity

>DIAGRAM\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\\[

\-Cartesian axes, first quadrant

\-Point (x; y)

\-Line drawn from origin O to the point

\-Angle between the line and the positive x-axis is labelled α

\-Using the theorem of Pythagoras, we can write the sine and cosine functions in terms of x, y and r:



sin^2 θ + cos^2 θ = (y/r)^2 + (x/r)^2

= y^2/r^2 + x^2/r^2

= (y^2 + x^2)/r^2

= r^2/r^2

= 1



This is the square identity:

sin^2 θ + cos^2 θ = 1



#### Other forms of the square identity

>Here are some useful tips for proving identities:

* Change all trigonometric ratios to sine and cosine.
* Choose one side of the equation to simplify and show that it is equal to the other side.
* Usually it is better to choose the more complicated side to simplify.
* Sometimes we need to simplify both sides of the equation to show that they are equal.
* A square root sign often indicates that we need to use the square identity.
* We can also add to the expression to make simplifying easier:

– replace 1 with sin^2 θ +cos^2 θ.

– multiply by 1 in the form of a suitable fraction, for example 1 + sin θ / 1 + sinθ .

### Function values of 90° ± θ and θ − 90°

\-Sine and cosine are known as co-functions. Two functions are called co-functions if f(A) = g(B) whenever A + B = 90° (that is, A and B are complementary angles).

\-The function value of an angle is equal to the co-function of its complement.

\-Thus for sine and cosine we have:

sin (90° − θ) = cos θ

cos (90° − θ) = sin θ

\-The sine and cosine graphs illustrate this clearly: the two graphs are identical except that they have a 90° phase difference.

= −sin (90° − θ)

= −cos θ

similarly, we can show that cos(θ − 90°) = sin θ

\-Therefore, sin (θ − 90°) = −cos θ and cos (θ − 90°) = sin θ.

## Area, sine, and cosine rules

\-There are three identities relating to the trigonometric functions that make working with triangles easier:

1\. the area rule

2\. the sine rule

3\. the cosine rule



### The area rule

\-For any △ABC with AB = c, BC = a and AC = b, we can construct a perpendicular height (h) from vertex A to the line BC:



>DIAGRAM\\\[

\-Triangle ABC

\-side opposite vertex A is a

\-side opposite vertex B is b

\-side opposite vertex C is c

\-Line labelled h drawn from angle A  perpendicular to BC

]



\-In △ABC:



sin Bˆ = h/c

∴ h = c sin Bˆ



\-And we know that

Area △ABC = 1/2 × a × h

= 1/2 × a × c sin Bˆ

∴ Area △ABC = 1/2 ac sin Bˆ



\-Alternatively, we could write that

sin Cˆ = h/b

∴ h = b sin Cˆ



\-And then we would have that

Area △ABC = 1/2× a × h

= 1/2 ab sin Cˆ



\-Similarly, by constructing a perpendicular height from vertex B to the line AC, we can also show that area △ABC = 1/2 bc sin Aˆ.



#### The area rule

The area rule states that the area of any triangle is equal to half the product of the lengths of the two sides of the triangle multiplied by the sine of the angle included by the two sides.



In any △ABC:

Area △ABC = 1/2 bc sin Aˆ

= 1/2 ac sin Bˆ

= 1/2 ab sin Cˆ

### The sine rule

\-So far we have only applied the trigonometric ratios to right-angled triangles.

\-We now expand the application of the trigonometric ratios to triangles that do not have a right angle:



\-For any triangle ABC with AB = c, BC =a and AC = b, we can construct a perpendicular height (h) at F:



>DIAGRAM\[

\-Triangle ABC

\-perpendicular height, h, constructed from A to F

\-F lies on BC

]



#### Method 1: using the sine ratio

In △ABF:

sin Bˆ = h/c

∴ h = c sin Bˆ



In △ACF:

sin Cˆ = h/b

∴ h = b sin Cˆ



We can equate the two equations

c sin Bˆ = b sin Cˆ

∴ sin Bˆ/b = sin Cˆ/c

or b/sin Bˆ = c/sin Cˆ



\-Similarly, by constructing a perpendicular height from vertex B to the line AC, we can also show that:

sin Aˆ/a = sin Cˆ/c



or a/sin Aˆ = c/sin Cˆ



\-Similarly, by constructing a perpendicular height from vertex B to the line AC, we can also show that area △ABC = 1/2 bc sin Aˆ.





#### Method 2: using the area rule

\-In △ABC:



Area △ABC = 1/2 ac sin Bˆ

= 1/2 ab sin Cˆ

∴ 1/2 ac sin Bˆ = 1/2 ab sin Cˆ

c sin Bˆ = b sin Cˆ

sin Bˆ /b = sin Cˆ /c

or b/sin ˆB = c/sin Cˆ



#### In a nutshell:

In any △ABC :

\-sin Aˆ/ a = sin Bˆ/ b = sin Cˆ /c

\-a/sin Aˆ = b/sin Bˆ= c/sin Cˆ

### The cosine rule

\-The cosine rule relates the length of a side of a triangle to the angle opposite it and the lengths of the other two sides.



>DIAGRAM\[

\-Triangle ABC

\-Side opposite A is a

\-Side opposite B  is b

\-Side opposite C is c

\-D lies on BC such that CD is perpendicular to AB

\-CD = h

\-AD = d

\-DB = c - d

]



\-Consider △ABC with CD ⊥ AB:

\-In △DCB: a^2 = (c−d)^2 + h^2 from the theorem of Pythagoras.

\-In △ACD: b^2 = d^2 + h^2 from the theorem of Pythagoras.

\-Since h^2 is common to both equations we can write:

a^2 = (c−d)^2 + h^2

∴ h^2 = a^2 − (c−d)^2

And b^2 = d^2 + h^2

∴ h^2 = b^2 − d^2

∴ b^2 − d^2 = a^2 − (c−d)^2

a^2 = b^2 +(c^2 − 2cd + d^2) − d^2

= b^2 + c^2 − 2cd



\-In order to eliminate d we look at △ACD, where we have: cos Aˆ = d/b. So, d = b cos Aˆ.

\-Substituting back we get: a^2 = b^2 + c^2 − 2 bc cos Aˆ.



The cosine rule:

In any ABC:

a^2 = b^2 + c^2 − 2bc cos Aˆ

b^2 = a^2 + c^2 − 2ac cos Bˆ

c^2 = a^2 + b^2 − 2ab cos Cˆ
