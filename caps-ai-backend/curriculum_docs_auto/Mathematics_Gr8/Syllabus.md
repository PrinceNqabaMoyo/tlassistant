#### 2. Content & Cognitive Scope

**Core Concepts & Skills:**
*   **Mental Calculations:** Revise multiplication of whole numbers up to $12 \times 12$.
*   **Ordering and Comparing:** Revise prime numbers up to at least $100$.
*   **Properties of Whole Numbers:**
    *   Commutative, associative, and distributive properties.
    *   Additive property (identity element for addition).
    *   Multiplicative property (identity element for multiplication).
    *   Division property of $0$ (division by $0$ is undefined).
    *   Inverse operations (addition/subtraction and multiplication/division).

**Teaching Guidelines & Depth of Treatment:**
*   **Consolidation:** Grade 8 learners must consolidate number knowledge and calculation techniques developed in the Intermediate Phase and Grade 7.
*   **Conceptual Progression:** Revisit properties of whole numbers as a foundation for understanding operations.
*   **Algebraic Readiness:** Introduce new number sets (e.g., integers) by exploring how properties of numbers function. Learners must apply these properties to variables in algebraic expressions.

**Mathematical Properties & Formulas:**
*   **Commutative Property:**
    *   $a + b = b + a$
    *   $a \times b = b \times a$
*   **Associative Property:**
    *   $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$
    *   $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$
*   **Distributive Property:**
    *   $a(b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$
    *   $a(b - c) = (a \times b) - (a \times c)$
*   **Identity Elements:**
    *   Additive identity: $t + 0 = t$
    *   Multiplicative identity: $t \times 1 = t$
*   **Undefined Operations:**
    *   Division by $0$ is undefined.

#### 2. Content & Cognitive Scope

**Core Concepts:**
*   **Properties of Whole Numbers:** Application of associative, commutative, and distributive properties.
*   **Calculation Techniques:** Estimation, mental math, written methods (columns), long division, rounding/compensating, and calculator usage.
*   **Multiples and Factors:** Prime factorization, Lowest Common Multiple (LCM), and Highest Common Factor (HCF).

**Cognitive Depth & Instructional Requirements:**
*   **Knowledge:** Recall of prime numbers and definitions of factors/multiples.
*   **Routine Procedures:** Performing addition, subtraction, multiplication, and long division with 3-digit numbers.
*   **Complex Procedures:** Applying prime factorization to find LCM and HCF; using properties of numbers to simplify calculations.
*   **Problem Solving:** Judging the reasonableness of solutions via estimation and selecting appropriate calculation strategies (mental vs. written vs. calculator).

**Mathematical Illustrations & Properties:**
*   Commutative/Associative properties: $33 + 99 = 99 + 33 = 132$
*   Grouping: $51 + (19 + 46) = (51 + 19) + 46 = 116$
*   Distributive property: $4(12 + 9) = (4 \times 12) + (4 \times 9) = 48 + 36 = 84$
*   Distributive property: $(9 \times 64) + (9 \times 36) = 9(64 + 36) = 9 \times 100 = 900$
*   Inverse operations: If $33 + 99 = 132$, then $132 - 99 = 33$
*   Inverse operations: If $20 \times 5 = 110$, then $110 \div 5 = 20$

---

#### 3. Teaching Guidelines & Clarifications

*   **Calculation Strategy:** Learners should progress toward context-free calculations while maintaining the ability to check solutions using inverse operations.
*   **Estimation:** Encourage the use of doubling, halving, and rounding to verify the reasonableness of calculated results.
*   **Long Division:** Should be practiced with familiar and smaller number ranges before moving to larger, more complex values.
*   **Multiples/Factors:** 
    *   Emphasis on prime factorization as a foundational skill for future algebraic factorization.
    *   Reinforce that $1$ is not classified as a prime number.
    *   Use LCM/HCF knowledge to assist in future work with equivalent fractions.

### 3. Mathematical Notation & Formulas
The curriculum specifies the use of prime factorization for HCF and LCM calculations:

*   **Prime Factorization Example:**
    $$120 = 5^1 \times 3^1 \times 2^3$$
    $$300 = 5^2 \times 3^1 \times 2^2$$
    $$900 = 5^2 \times 3^2 \times 2^2$$

*   **HCF Calculation:**
    $$HCF = 5^1 \times 3^1 \times 2^2 = 60$$

*   **General Rule:**
    To find the HCF of multiple numbers, identify the common prime factors and multiply them using the lowest exponent found in the prime factorizations.

# Mathematics Revision: Integers

## 1. Overview
*   **Topic:** Integers
*   **Duration:** 9 hours
*   **Core Focus:** Consolidating number knowledge, performing all four operations, and working with squares and cube roots.

---

## 2. Counting, Ordering, and Comparing Integers
Learners must be able to count forwards and backwards across any interval. Key techniques include:
*   Using structured or semi-structured number lines.
*   Using chain diagrams for counting.
*   Arranging sets of integers in ascending (smallest to biggest) or descending (biggest to smallest) order.
*   Identifying missing numbers in sequences, grids, or on number lines.
*   Comparing integers using inequality symbols:
    *   $<$ (less than)
    *   $>$ (greater than)
    *   Example: $-425 < -450$ is **false**; $-450 < -425$ is **true**.

---

## 3. Calculations Using Integers

### Core Principles
*   **Additive Inverse:** Subtracting an integer is equivalent to adding its additive inverse (e.g., subtracting $-4$ is the same as adding $+4$).
*   **Operations:** Mastery of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division involving positive and negative numbers.
*   **Powers and Roots:** Calculations involving squares ($x^2$) and cube roots ($\sqrt[3]{x}$).

### Worked Examples
When performing calculations, use brackets to clarify the signs of the integers:

| Operation | Calculation | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Addition | $7 + (-4)$ | $3$ |
| Subtraction | $-7 - (-4)$ | $-7 + 4 = -3$ |
| Addition | $-7 + (+4)$ | $-3$ |

### Strategy for Multiplication
Use repeated addition and number patterns to verify the reasonableness of results when multiplying integers.

---

## 4. Practice Activities
To master these concepts, learners should engage in the following exercises:

| Activity Type | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Ordering** | Given a list of integers, arrange them from smallest to largest. |
| **Number Lines** | Plot integers on a number line to visualize distance from zero. |
| **Missing Values** | Fill in missing integers in a sequence (e.g., $-10, -5, 0, \dots, 10$). |
| **Mixed Operations** | Solve expressions involving all four operations, including squares and cubes. |
| **Comparison** | Insert the correct symbol ($<, >, =$) between two integers. |

---

#### 2. Teaching Guidelines & Misconception Management

| Topic | Clarification Notes & Teaching Guidelines |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1.2 Exponents** | **Common Misconceptions to Address:** |
| | • **Multiplication of unlike bases:** Learners often incorrectly assume $x^m \cdot y^n = (xy)^{m+n}$. |
| | • **Multiplication of like bases:** Learners often incorrectly assume $2^5 \cdot 2^7 = 2^{12}$ (adding exponents is correct, but ensure base consistency). |
| | • **Binomial squaring:** Learners often forget the middle term: $(x + y)^m \neq x^m + y^m$. |
| | • **Addition of exponents:** Learners often confuse $x^m + x^n$ with $x^{m+n}$ or $x^{mn}$. |
| | **Calculation Rules:** |
| | • Emphasize order of operations: $(7 - 4)^3 = 3^3$ (Correct) vs $7^3 - 4^3$ (Incorrect). |
| | • Emphasize root properties: $\sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5$ (Correct) vs $\sqrt{16} + \sqrt{9} = 4 + 3 = 7$ (Incorrect). |
| | • Fraction/Decimal application: $\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^3 = \frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2}{3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3} = \frac{8}{27}$. |

---

#### 3. Mathematical Examples & Formulas

**Key Laws and Operations:**

*   **Product Rule:** $x^m \cdot x^n = x^{m+n}$
*   **Power of a Power:** $(x^m)^n = x^{mn}$
*   **Root of a Quotient:** $\sqrt{\frac{9}{25}} = \frac{\sqrt{9}}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{3}{5}$

**Decimal and Square/Cube Examples:**

*   $(0,7)^2 = 0,49$
*   $(0,1)^3 = 0,001$
*   $\sqrt{0,09} = 0,3$
*   $\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2 = \frac{3^2}{4^2} = \frac{9}{16}$

## 2. Mathematical Procedures

### Solving Equations
To solve for an unknown variable, we use inverse operations to isolate the variable on one side of the equation.

| Operation | Inverse Operation |
| :--- | :--- |
| Addition ($+$) | Subtraction ($-$) |
| Subtraction ($-$) | Addition ($+$) |
| Multiplication ($\times$) | Division ($\div$) |
| Division ($\div$) | Multiplication ($\times$) |

**Example: Using Additive and Multiplicative Inverses**
Solve for $x$: $3x + 5 = 14$

1.  **Subtract 5 from both sides (Additive Inverse):**
    $3x + 5 - 5 = 14 - 5$
    $3x = 9$
2.  **Divide by 3 (Multiplicative Inverse):**
    $\frac{3x}{3} = \frac{9}{3}$
    $x = 3$

---

## 3. Working with Exponents
When equations involve exponents, ensure you apply the laws of exponents correctly before solving.

**Example: Solving with Exponents**
Solve for $x$: $x^2 = 16$

*   To isolate $x$, take the square root of both sides:
    $\sqrt{x^2} = \sqrt{16}$
    $x = 4$ (or $-4$)

# Mathematics Revision: Percentages and Equivalent Forms

## 1. Core Concepts
Understanding the relationship between common fractions, decimals, and percentages is essential for solving real-world financial and mathematical problems.

### Equivalent Forms
To work effectively with percentages, you must be able to convert between different representations of the same value:
*   **Common Fractions:** Fractions where the denominator is a multiple of the other.
*   **Decimal Fractions:** The decimal representation of a fraction.
*   **Percentages:** A fraction with a denominator of $100$.

**Key Equivalencies:**
*   $25\% = \frac{25}{100} = \frac{1}{4} = 0,25$
*   $50\% = \frac{50}{100} = \frac{1}{2} = 0,5$
*   $60\% = \frac{60}{100} = \frac{3}{5} = 0,6$

---

## 2. Procedures and Worked Examples

### Calculating a Percentage of a Whole
To find a percentage of a value, multiply the value by the percentage expressed as a fraction or decimal.

**Example: Calculate $60\%$ of R105**
$$\text{Amount} = \frac{60}{100} \times 105 = 63$$
**Result:** R63

### Calculating What Percentage One Number is of Another
To find what percentage part $A$ is of whole $B$:
$$\text{Percentage} = \left( \frac{A}{B} \right) \times 100$$

**Example: What percentage is 40c of R3,20?**
*Note: Convert units to be the same (R3,20 = 320c)*
$$\text{Percentage} = \frac{40}{320} \times 100 = 12,5\%$$

### Calculating Percentage Increase or Decrease
To find the percentage change:
$$\text{Percentage Change} = \left( \frac{\text{Difference}}{\text{Original Value}} \right) \times 100$$

**Example: Percentage increase if a bus ticket increases from R60 to R84**
*   Difference: $84 - 60 = 24$
*   Calculation: $\frac{24}{60} \times 100 = 40\%$

### Calculating Amounts from Percentage Changes
When applying a percentage increase or decrease to a total, you can calculate the new total directly.

**Example: Reduce R150 000 by 15%**
*   **Method 1 (Two-step):**
    *   Find 15%: $\frac{15}{100} \times 150\,000 = 22\,500$
    *   Subtract: $150\,000 - 22\,500 = 127\,500$
*   **Method 2 (Direct):**
    *   If you reduce by $15\%$, you are left with $85\%$ ($100\% - 15\% = 85\%$).
    *   Calculation: $\frac{85}{100} \times 150\,000 = 127\,500$

## 3. Mathematical Operations with Fractions
When working with fractions, apply standard algebraic rules:

| Operation | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Squaring a fraction** | $\left( \frac{2}{3} \right)^2 = \frac{2^2}{3^2} = \frac{4}{9}$ |
| **Square root of a fraction** | $\sqrt{\frac{16}{25}} = \frac{\sqrt{16}}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{4}{5}$ |

### 3. Mathematical Specifications & Examples

#### Coordinate Conventions
*   Points are represented as ordered pairs: $(x, y)$.
*   Translation example: If point $T(4, 3)$ is translated to $T'$, the new coordinates are determined by the vector shift.
*   Reflection example: Reflecting a point across the X-axis or Y-axis.

#### Geometric Scaling Formulas
*   **Perimeter:** If the side length of a square is doubled, the new perimeter $P_{new}$ relates to the original perimeter $P_{old}$ as:
    $$P_{new} = 2 \times P_{old}$$
*   **Area:** If the side length of a square is multiplied by a factor of $k$, the area $A$ changes by a factor of $k^2$:
    $$A_{new} = k^2 \times A_{old}$$
    *For an enlargement factor of $k=2$, the area becomes $2^2 = 4$ times the original area.*
