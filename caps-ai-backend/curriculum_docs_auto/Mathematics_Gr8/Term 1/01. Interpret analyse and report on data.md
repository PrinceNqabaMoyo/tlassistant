#### Worked Example: Calculating Population from Percentages

**Question:** If $150\,000$ Grade 6 learners took part in the survey, how many girls and how many boys were there in Grade 6?

**Solution:**
1. Identify the percentages for Grade 6 from the table:
   *   Male: $50,3\%$
   *   Female: $49,7\%$

2. Calculate the number of boys:
   $$\text{Boys} = 150\,000 \times \frac{50,3}{100} = 75\,450$$

3. Calculate the number of girls:
   $$\text{Girls} = 150\,000 \times \frac{49,7}{100} = 74\,550$$

### Data Interpretation: Pie Charts

Pie charts represent parts of a whole. The total area of the circle represents $100\%$ of the data.

**Key Concepts for Pie Charts:**
1.  **Largest/Smallest:** The largest slice represents the highest value; the smallest slice represents the lowest value.
2.  **Population vs. Area:** A pie chart showing land area **cannot** be used to determine population. Land area and population density are independent variables; a large province may have a small population, and a small province may have a large population.
3.  **Calculations:** To find the actual value of a slice, use the formula:
    $$\text{Value} = \text{Total} \times \frac{\text{Percentage of slice}}{100}$$

### 25.2 Identifying Bias and Misleading Data

Data presentation can sometimes be intentionally or unintentionally **biased** or **misleading**. When analyzing data, you must consider the following factors:

*   **Data representation:** Is the data shown accurately by the graph?
*   **Data collection:** When, how, and where was the data collected?
*   **Scale:** Are the scales used on the axes of the graphs appropriate?
*   **Summary Statistics:** Which measures (mean, median, or mode) are used to summarize the data?

#### Definition: Bias
**Bias** means that a person prefers a certain idea and possibly does not give equal chance to a different idea.

#### Summary of Data Representation Concepts

| Representation Type | Best Used For | Key Feature |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bar Graph** | Comparing categories | Heights of bars represent frequency/percentage. |
| **Pie Chart** | Showing parts of a whole | Total sectors must sum to $100\%$. |
| **Histogram** | Showing frequency distribution | Bars are adjacent; represents continuous data. |

**Note on Data Integrity:**
When interpreting graphs, always check:
1.  **The Scale:** Does the vertical axis start at $0$? If not, the visual representation of differences may be exaggerated.
2.  **The Sample:** Who was surveyed? (e.g., Adults vs. Children).
3.  **The Context:** Where and when was the data collected? (e.g., Supermarket vs. Home).

## Mathematical Background

In this chapter, the focus is on the interpretation, analysis, and reporting of data findings.

### 1. Key Definitions
*   **Interpret data:** The process of extracting information directly from data to determine if the information is understood.
*   **Analyse data:** Investigating given data to find patterns and trends.
    *   **Patterns:** Can be cyclic (e.g., data showing learners are consistently absent on Mondays).
    *   **Trends:** Increasing or decreasing patterns (e.g., the percentage of girls per grade increasing in higher grades).
*   **Report on data:** Summarising the most important findings from the data in a short paragraph.

### 2. Bias in Data
**Bias** refers to how far the information gained from a sample deviates from the true characteristics of the population.

*   **Biased sampling method:** A method that tends to give non-representative samples. These samples either under-represent or over-represent certain characteristics of the population.
*   **Unbiased sampling method:** A method that tends to give representative samples, providing a true reflection of the population's characteristics.

### 3. Misleading Data
**Misleading data** is data manipulated to favour a specific opinion. This is common in media and financial reports to present a positive or negative picture of a subject. Common techniques include:

*   **Altering the scale:** Starting the frequency axis at a point other than $0$.
*   **Visual distortion:** Using three-dimensional displays to exaggerate the impression of a specific category.
*   **Inconsistent scaling:** Using different scales on the frequency axes when comparing two different displays.

### 25.2 Identifying Bias and Misleading Data

#### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Bias:** Refers to how far the information gained from a sample deviates from the true information of the entire population. It implies that a person prefers a certain idea and may not give equal consideration to alternative ideas.
*   **Misleading Data:** Refers to the misuse of graphs or data to create a false impression or to favour a specific opinion.

#### Common Ways Data is Misrepresented
Graphs and data presentations can be misleading if:
1.  **Distorted Scales:** The vertical axis does not start at $0$, or it skips numbers, which exaggerates differences between values.
2.  **Improper Labelling:** The graph lacks clear labels, making it difficult to interpret the data accurately.
3.  **Omission:** Relevant data is left out to support a specific narrative.
4.  **3D Effects:** Graphs drawn in three dimensions can distort the visual perception of the actual values.

#### Critical Analysis Checklist
When evaluating data, always consider:
*   What data is missing or not shown?
*   When, how, and where was the data collected?
*   Are the scales on the axes consistent and appropriate?
*   Which summary statistics (mean, median, or mode) were chosen to represent the data?

### Interpreting and Analyzing Data: Identifying Misleading Graphs

When analyzing data, it is crucial to identify how graphs can be manipulated to create a false impression. This section covers common pitfalls in data representation.

---

#### 1. Misleading Graphs: Key Concepts

*   **3D Perspective:** Pie charts drawn in 3D can distort the size of slices, making those in the foreground appear larger than they actually are.
*   **Truncated Axes:** If the vertical axis (frequency axis) does not start at zero, the differences between bars are exaggerated, leading to false conclusions about the magnitude of the data.

#### 3. Interpreting Data Collection Methods
When analyzing data, it is important to consider how the collection process influences the results:
*   **Timing:** Collecting data earlier in the day may result in lower reported activity levels compared to later in the day.

#### 5. Statistical Analysis: Range
The **range** is a measure of spread in a data set. It is calculated as:
$$\text{Range} = \text{Maximum value} - \text{Minimum value}$$

**Data Table: Mathematics Marks**

| Class | Marks |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Class A** | 94, 42, 23, 67, 67, 68, 13, 53, 44, 34, 64, 69, 50, 31, 91, 40, 10, 30, 49, 61 |
| **Class B** | 74, 26, 65, 45, 71, 77, 58, 35, 39, 45, 68, 45, 57, 62, 29, 55, 23, 56, 38, 36, 50, 64, 58, 32, 42 |

**Worked Examples for Range:**
*   **Class A:**
    *   Max: $94$, Min: $10$
    *   Range: $94 - 10 = 84$
*   **Class B:**
    *   Max: $77$, Min: $23$
    *   Range: $77 - 23 = 54$

**Conclusion:**
A larger range indicates that the data is more spread out (greater variety in marks), while a smaller range indicates that the data is more clustered together. Class A has a wider range of marks than Class B.

### Data Analysis: Comparing Class Performance

This section focuses on interpreting and comparing two sets of data (Class A and Class B) using measures of central tendency: the **mean**, **median**, and **mode**.

---

#### 1. Calculating the Mean (Average)
The mean is calculated by dividing the total sum of marks by the number of marks.
$$\text{Mean} = \frac{\text{Total marks}}{\text{Number of marks}}$$

| Class | Total marks | Number of marks | Mean |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Class A | $1\,000$ | $20$ | $50$ |
| Class B | $1\,250$ | $25$ | $50$ |

**Observation (d):** Based on the means, both classes are performing at the same level.

---

#### 2. Calculating the Median
The median is the middle value of an ordered data set. If there are two middle values, the median is the average of those two values.

| Class | Marks from highest to lowest | Middle position | Median |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Class A | $94, 91, 69, 68, 67, 67, 64, 61, 53, 50, 49, 44, 42, 40, 34, 31, 30, 23, 13, 10$ | $10$ and $11$ | $\frac{50 + 49}{2} = 49,5$ |
| Class B | $77, 74, 71, 68, 65, 64, 62, 58, 58, 57, 56, 55, 50, 45, 45, 45, 42, 39, 38, 36, 35, 32, 29, 26, 23$ | $13$ | $50$ |

**Observation (f):** Based on the medians, the learners in the two classes are performing at more or less the same level.

---

#### 3. Calculating the Mode
The mode is the value that appears most frequently in a data set.

| Class | Highest frequency | Mode |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Class A | $2$ | $67$ |
| Class B | $3$ | $45$ |

**Observation (h):** Based on the modes, Class A appears to be performing better than Class B.

---

#### 4. Summary and Interpretation
**Observation (i):** The mode only describes the most frequent values and does not account for all the values in the data set. Because the mean and median take all values into account, they generally provide a more accurate representation of the data than the mode does.
