# 5 Work, energy and power

## 5.1 Introduction

We use the term ‘work’ in everyday conversation to mean many different things. We talk about going to work, doing homework, working in class. Physicists mean something very specific when they talk about work.

In Physics we use the term work to describe the process of transferring energy from object or system to another or converting energy from one form to another.

You will learn that work and energy are closely related to Newton’s laws of motion. You shall see that the energy of an object is its capacity to do work and doing work is the process of transferring energy from one object or form to another by means of a force. In other words,

* an object with lots of energy can do lots of work.
* when object A transfers energy to object B, the energy of object A decreases by the same amount as the energy of object B increases, we say that object A does work on object B.

Lifting objects or throwing them requires that you do work on them. Even making an electrical current flow requires that something do work. Objects or systems must have energy to be able to do work on other objects or systems by transferring some of their energy.

# Revision Notes: Work, Energy and Power

## 1. Core Concepts: Work

When a force acts in or against the direction of motion of an object, work is done on the object. Specifically, work is defined mathematically in terms of the force applied and the displacement of the object.

### Definition

> **Work:** When a force acts in or against the direction of motion of an object, work is done on the object.
> 
> $$W = F\Delta x \cos\theta$$

For work to be done, an object must have its position changed by an amount $\Delta\vec{x}$ while a force $\vec{F}$ is acting on it such that there is some non-zero component of the force in the direction of the displacement.

---

## 2. Mathematical Formula and Variables

Work is calculated using the formula:

$$W = F\Delta x \cos\theta$$

Where:
* $W$ = work in joules ($\text{J}$)
* $F$ = magnitude of force for which we are calculating work in newtons ($\text{N}$)
* $\Delta x$ = magnitude of displacement in metres ($\text{m}$)
* $\theta$ = angle between the force and the direction of displacement

### Key Notes on Variables:
* $F \cos\theta$ is the magnitude of the component of $\vec{F}$ in the direction of $\Delta\vec{x}$. If $\theta > 90^{\circ}$, then the component is parallel to the direction of displacement but points in the opposite direction, meaning the force is opposing the motion.
* We will deal with constant forces, not forces that are continuously changing in this chapter. All of the principles we have covered are applicable when forces are constant.
* $\cos\theta$ tells you the relative direction of the force and the displacement. If the component of the force along the direction of the displacement is opposite in direction to the displacement, then the sign of the work done will be negative, regardless of which direction was chosen as a positive direction.

## Work and Direction of Force

### Core Concepts

* **Condition for Work:** For work to be done on an object, there must be a component of the applied force acting in the direction of the motion (displacement).
* **Perpendicular Forces:** Forces acting perpendicular to the direction of motion do **no work**.
* **Effect of Angle ($\theta$):**
  * At $\theta = 90^\circ$, the magnitude of the component of the force in the direction of the displacement is zero, so **no work is done** as $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$.
  * At $\theta > 90^\circ$, the magnitude of the component of the force along the line of the displacement is opposite in direction to the displacement, so **negative work is done**, as $\cos\theta < 0$.

---

### Summary of Force Orientations and Work

| Condition | Angle ($\theta$) | Force Component ($F \cos\theta$) | Work Done |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Perpendicular** | $\theta = 90^\circ$ | $F \cos(90^\circ) = 0$ | No work done |
| **Opposite Direction** | $\theta > 90^\circ$ | Negative ($\cos\theta < 0$) | Negative work done |

---

### Everyday Misconceptions: Weightlifting

* **Common Use vs. Physics Definition:** In everyday language, holding or moving heavy objects is often associated with "hard work." From a physics perspective, work is strictly defined by force and displacement in the same direction.
* **Examples (Figure 5.3):**
  * **Deadlift (Left):** The powerlifter exerts an upward force on the weights while pulling them upwards. Since the force and the displacement are in the same direction, **positive work is done** on the weights.
  * **Bench Press (Right):** The powerlifter exerts an upward force on the weights while pushing them upwards. Again, force and displacement align, so **positive work is done** on the weights.

# Revision Notes: Chapter 5 - Work, Energy, and Power

## 1. Core Concepts: When is Work Done?

In physics, **work** is done on an object only when a force is applied to the object and the object undergoes a **displacement** in the direction of that force. 

### The Strongman Example
Consider a strongman carrying heavy sleds horizontally across a distance (as shown in Figure 5.4). From a biological and everyday perspective, the strongman is working very hard and using energy reserves to keep the sleds up. However, from a **physics perspective**, he is doing **zero work** on the sleds.

* **Reason:** The force the strongman exerts to hold and lift the sleds is directed **directly upwards** (to balance gravity). The displacement of the sleds is in the **horizontal direction**. 
* **Angle ($\theta$):** Because the upward force and horizontal displacement are perpendicular to each other, the angle between them is $\theta = 90^\circ$. There is no component of the force in the direction of displacement.
* Therefore, no work is done on the sleds in the physics sense, because no energy is transferred to the motion of the sleds.

---

## 2. Investigation: Is Work Done?

**Objective:** Decide whether work is done in various physical situations, identify interacting objects, action-reaction pairs, and analyze whether forces perform work.

### Activity Situations:
1. **Max pushes against a wall and becomes tired.**
   * *Analysis:* Force is applied, but there is **no displacement** ($\Delta\vec{x} = 0$). Therefore, **no work** is done.
2. **A book falls off a table and free falls to சுவா ground.**
   * *Analysis:* The gravitational force acts downwards, and the displacement of the book is downwards ($\theta = 0^\circ$). Therefore, **work is done** by gravity on the book.
3. **A rocket accelerates through space.**
   * *Analysis:* The rocket's engines exert a thrust force in the same direction as its displacement. Therefore, **work is done** by the thrust force.
4. **A waiter holds a tray full of plates above his head with one arm and carries it straight across the room at constant speed.**
   * *Analysis:* The force holding the tray is directed upwards, while the displacement is horizontal ($\theta = 90^\circ$). Therefore, **no work** is done on the tray by the waiter's upward lifting force.

---

## 3. Force and Displacement Alignment

When analyzing work graphically or vectorially:
* If the force vector $\vec{F}$ acts in the **same direction** as the displacement vector $\Delta\vec{x}$, the angle between them is $\theta = 0^\circ$.
* As illustrated by the diagrams of carts moving horizontally or along inclined planes, when $\vec{F}$ and $\Delta\vec{x}$ are parallel ($\theta = 0^\circ$), the maximum component of force contributes to the work done.

# Lesson Notes: Work

## Core Concepts

### Defining Work in Physics
* In physics, the everyday use of the word "work" differs from its scientific use. Only the component of the applied force that is **parallel to the motion** does work on an object. 
* *Example:* Holding up a heavy book does no work on the book because there is no displacement in the direction of the force.
* **Work** is defined as energy transfer. Because energy is a scalar quantity, work is also a scalar. The sign of the work indicates whether energy was increased or decreased:
  * If $\vec{F}_{\text{applied}}$ acts or has a component acting in the same direction as the motion, **positive work** is done. In this case, the object on which the force is applied gains energy.
  * If the direction of motion and $\vec{F}_{\text{applied}}$ are opposite, **negative work** is done. This means that energy is lost and the object exerting the force gains energy.

### Units of Work
* Work is measured in $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$.
* The S.I. unit for work is the **joule** (symbol $\text{J}$).

---

## Worked Example 1: Calculating work on a car when speeding up

### Question
A car is travelling along a straight horizontal road. A force of $500\text{ N}$ is applied to the car in the direction that it is travelling, speeding it up. While it is speeding up it covers a distance of $20\text{ m}$. Calculate the work done on the car.

---

### Solution

#### Step 1: Analyse the question to determine what information is provided
* The magnitude of the force applied is $F = 500\text{ N}$.
* The distance moved is $\Delta x = 20\text{ m}$.
* The applied force and distance moved are in the same direction. Therefore, the angle between the force and displacement is $\theta = 0^{\circ}$.
* These quantities are all in SI units, so no unit conversions are required.

#### Step 2: Analyse the question to determine what is being asked
* We are asked to find the work done on the car. We know from the definition that work done is $W = F\Delta x \cos\theta$.

#### Step 3: Substitute the values and calculate the work done
$$
\begin{aligned}
W &= F\Delta x \cos\theta \\
&= (500)(20)(\cos 0^{\circ}) \\
&= (500)(20)(1) \\
&= 10\,000\text{ J}
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Note:** The answer is positive because the applied force and the displacement are in the same direction. In this case, the car gains kinetic energy.

## Worked Example 2: Calculating work on the car while braking

### QUESTION
The same car now slows down when a force of $300\text{ N}$ is applied opposite to the direction of motion while it travels $25\text{ m}$ forward. Calculate the work done on the car.

### SOLUTION

#### Step 1: Analyse the question to determine what information is provided
* The magnitude of the force applied is $F = 300\text{ N}$.
* The distance moved is $\Delta x = 25\text{ m}$.
* The applied force and distance moved are in the opposite direction. Therefore, the angle between the force and displacement is $\theta = 180^\circ$.

These quantities are all in the correct units, so no unit conversions are required.

#### Step 2: Analyse the question to determine what is being asked
* We are asked to find the work done on the car. We know from the definition that work done is $W = F\Delta x \cos\theta$

#### Step 3: Next we substitute the values and calculate the work done
$$
\begin{aligned}
W &= F\Delta x \cos\theta \\
&= (300)(25)(\cos 180^\circ) \\
&= (300)(25)(-1) \\
&= -7500\text{ J}
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Note:** The answer must be *negative* as the applied force and the displacement are in opposite directions. This means that the energy is being lost by the car. This may be energy lost as heat to the environment. Energy conservation still holds, the energy has just been transferred to a larger system that contains the car.

---

What happens when the applied force and the motion are not parallel? By using the formula $W = F\Delta x \cos\theta$, we are actually calculating the *component* of the applied force in the direction of motion. Note that the component of the force perpendicular to the direction of motion does no work.

## Approaches to Finding Net Work Done

When multiple forces act on an object, the net work done is the work done by the net force, or the sum of the work done by each individual force. There are two equivalent approaches to find the net work done on an object:

*   **Approach 1:** Calculate the work done by **each force individually** and then sum them, **taking the signs into account**. If one force does positive work and another does the same amount of work but it is negative, then they cancel out.
*   **Approach 2:** Calculate the resultant force from all the forces acting and calculate the work done using the resultant force. This will be equivalent to Approach 1. If the resultant force parallel to the direction of motion is zero, no net work will be done.

Remember that work done tells you about the energy transfer to or from an object by means of a force. That is why we can have zero net work done even if multiple large forces are acting on an object. 

*   Forces that result in **positive work** increase the energy of the object.
*   Forces that result in **negative work** reduce the energy of the object. 

If as much energy is transferred to an object as is transferred away, then the final result is that the object gains no energy overall.

### Step 3: Calculation of Work Done by Each Force

$$W_{applied} = F_{applied} \Delta x \cos \theta$$
$$= (300)(20)(\cos 0^\circ)$$
$$= (300)(20)(1)$$
$$= 6000\text{ J}$$

$$W_{friction} = F_{friction} \Delta x \cos \theta$$
$$= (100)(20)(\cos 180^\circ)$$
$$= (100)(20)(-1)$$
$$= -2000\text{ J}$$

$$W_{net} = W_{applied} + W_{friction}$$
$$= (6000) + (-2000)$$
$$= 4000\text{ J}$$

> **Note:** The answer shown in this worked example shows that although energy has been lost by the car to friction, the total work done on the car has resulted in a net energy gain. This can be seen by the positive answer.

---

As mentioned before, there is an alternative method to solving the same problem, which is to determine the net force acting on the car and to use this to calculate the work. This means that the vector forces acting in the plane of motion must be added to get the net force $\vec{F}_{net}$. The net force is then applied over the displacement to get the net work $W_{net}$.

$$W_{net} = (200)(20)\cos(0)$$
$$W_{net} = 4000\text{ J}$$

### IMPORTANT!

The two different approaches give the same result, but it is very important to treat the signs correctly. The forces are vectors, but work is a scalar, so they shouldn't be interpreted in the same way.

## 5.3 Work-Energy Theorem (ESCMD)

### Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces (ESCMF)

In Grade 10, you saw that mechanical energy was conserved in the **absence** of non-conservative forces. It is important to know whether a force is a conservative force or a non-conservative force in the system, because this is related to whether the force can change an object's total mechanical energy when it does work on an object.

When the only forces doing work are conservative forces (for example, gravitational and spring forces), energy changes forms — from kinetic to potential (or vice versa); yet the total amount of mechanical energy ($E_K + E_P$) is conserved. For example, as an object falls in a gravitational field from a high elevation to a lower elevation, some of the object's potential energy is changed into kinetic energy. However, the sum of the kinetic and potential energies remain constant.

# Investigation: Non-conservative forces

We can investigate the effect of non-conservative forces on an object's total mechanical energy by rolling a ball along the floor from point A to point B.



![Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr12_Momentum-and-Impulse.pdf-0012-08.png](images/Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr12_Momentum-and-Impulse.pdf-0012-08.png)


Find a nice smooth surface (e.g., a highly polished floor), mark off two positions, A and B, and roll the ball between them.

The total mechanical energy of the ball, at each point, is the sum of its kinetic energy ($E_k$) and gravitational potential energy ($E_p$):

$$E_{\text{total},A} = E_{k,A} + E_{p,A}$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_A^2 + mgh_A$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_A^2 + mg(0)$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_A^2$$

$$E_{\text{total},B} = E_{k,B} + E_{p,B}$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2 + mgh_B$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2 + mg(0)$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2$$

In the absence of friction and other non-conservative forces, the ball should slide along the floor and its speed should be *the same* at positions A and B. Since there are no non-conservative forces acting on the ball, its total mechanical energy at points A and B are equal.

$$v_A = v_B$$
$$\frac{1}{2}mv_A^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2$$
$$E_{\text{total},A} = E_{\text{total},B}$$

Now, let's investigate what happens when there is friction (an *non-conservative force*) acting on the ball.

Roll the ball along a rough surface or a carpeted floor. What happens to the speed of the ball at point A compared to point B?

If the surface you are rolling the ball along is very rough and provides a large non-conservative frictional force, then the ball should be moving much slower at point B than at point A.

Let's compare the total mechanical energy of the ball at points A and B:

$$E_{\text{total},A} = EK_A + PE_A$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_A^2 + mgh_A$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_A^2 + mg(0)$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_A^2$$

$$E_{\text{total},B} = EK_B + PE_B$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2 + mgh_B$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2 + mg(0)$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2$$

In this case, the missing energy is the work done by the carpet through applying a frictional force on the ball. In this case the carpet is doing negative work on the ball.

When a non-conservative force (for example friction, air resistance, applied force) does work on an object, the total mechanical energy ($E_k + E_p$) of that object changes. If positive work is done, then the object will gain energy. If negative work is done, then the object will lose energy.

When a **net force does work** on an object, then there is **always a change in the kinetic energy** of the object. This is because the object experiences an acceleration and therefore a change in velocity.

This leads us to the work-energy theorem.

> **DEFINITION:** *Work-energy theorem*
>
> The work-energy theorem states that the work done on an object by the net force is equal to the change in its kinetic energy:
>
> $$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k = E_{k,f} - E_{k,i}$$

The work-energy theorem is another example of the conservation of energy which you saw in Grade 10.

> A force only does work on an object for the time that it is in contact with the object. ### Step 2: Determine how to approach the problem

We apply the work-energy theorem. We know that all the car's kinetic energy is lost to friction. Therefore, the change in the car's kinetic energy is equal to the work done by the frictional force of the car's brakes.

Therefore, we first need to determine the car's kinetic energy at the moment of braking using: 
$$E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

This energy is equal to the work done by the brakes. We have the force applied by the brakes, and we can use: 
$$W = F \Delta x \cos\theta$$

to determine the stopping distance.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_k &= \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{2} (1000) (16,7)^2 \\

\end{aligned}

Assume the stopping distance is $\Delta x_0$. Since the direction of the applied force and the displacement are in opposite directions, $\theta = 180^{\circ}$. Then the work done is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W &= F \Delta x \cos\theta \\
&= (8000) (\Delta x_0) \cos(180^{\circ}) \\
&= (8000) (\Delta x_0) (-1) \\
&= (-8000) (\Delta x_0)
\end{aligned}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E_k &= W_{\text{net}} \\
E_{k,f} - E_{k,i} &= (-8000) (\Delta x_0) \\
0 - 139\ 445 &= (-8000) (\Delta x_0) \\
\therefore \Delta x_0 &= \frac{139\ 445}{8000} \\
&= 17,4\ \text{m}
\end{aligned}

# Chapter 5: Work, Energy and Power

## Determining Net Work Done on a Round Trip

To determine the net work done, $W_{(round\ trip)}$, we need to sum the work done by each force acting during the period. We have calculated the work done by $\vec{F}$ already and we know that there is no work done by the normal force.

The total work done during a round trip by gravity is the sum of the work done during the upward motion (where the force is in the opposite direction to the motion) and the downward motion (where the force is in the same direction as the motion).

The distance over which the force acts is the same during the upward and downward motion and the magnitude of the force is the same. The only difference between the calculation for the work done during the upward and downward motion is the sign because of the change of direction of the motion. Therefore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{g(round\ trip)} &= W_{g(up)} + W_{g(down)} \\
&= (mg\sin\alpha \cdot \Delta x \cos 180^{\circ}) + (mg\sin\alpha \cdot \Delta x \cos 0^{\circ}) \\
&= (-mg\sin\alpha \cdot \Delta x) + (mg\sin\alpha \cdot \Delta x) \\
&= 0\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, the total work done during the round trip is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{(round\ trip)} &= W_F + W_g + W_N \\
&= W_F + W_{g(up)} + W_{N(up)} \\
&= (98) + (0) + (0) \\
&= 98\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now use this in the equation for the work-energy theorem:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{(round\ trip)} &= E_{k,f} \\
E_{k,f} &= 98\ \text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Step 6: Speed of the Block

We know the kinetic energy of the block, therefore we can determine its speed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{k,f} &= \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \\
\frac{1}{2}mv^2 &= E_{k,f} \\
v &= \pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}E_{k,f}} \\
&= \pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{(2)}(98)} \\
&= \pm 9,89949493661 \\
&= 9,90\ \text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 1. Concepts & Structure

### Potential Energy in Springs
When you wind up a toy, an egg timer, or an old-fashioned watch, you do work against its spring and store energy in it. We treat these springs as **ideal**, meaning we assume there is no friction and no production of thermal energy. This stored energy is recoverable as work, and it is useful to think of it as potential energy contained in the spring.

### Work and Conservative Forces
The total work done by a conservative force results in a change in potential energy, $\Delta E_p$. If the conservative force does positive work, then the change in potential energy is negative. Therefore:

$$W_{\text{conservative}} = -\Delta E_p$$

---

### DEFINITION: Non-conservative force
A non-conservative force is one for which the work done on the object depends on the path taken by the object.

---

### IMPORTANT!
Non-conservative forces do not imply that total energy is not conserved. Total energy is **always** conserved. Non-conservative forces mean that mechanical energy isn't conserved in a particular system, which implies that the energy has been transferred in a process that isn't reversible.

* **Friction** is a good example of a non-conservative force because it removes energy from the system, so the amount of mechanical energy is not conserved.
* Non-conservative forces can also do positive work, thereby increasing the total mechanical energy of the system.
* The energy transferred to overcome friction depends on the distance covered and is converted to thermal energy, which can't be recovered by the system.

---

## 2. Non-conservative Forces and Work-Energy Theorem (ESCMH)

We know that the net work done will be the sum of the work done by all of the individual forces:

$$F_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{conservative}} + F_{\text{non-conservative}}$$

(the work done by these forces yields)

$$W_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{conservative}} + W_{\text{non-conservative}}$$

(using the work-energy theorem)

$$\Delta E_k = W_{\text{conservative}} + W_{\text{non-conservative}}$$

(using $W_{\text{conservative}} = -\Delta E_p$)

$$\Delta E_k = -\Delta E_p + W_{\text{non-conservative}}$$

$$W_{\text{non-conservative}} = \Delta E_k + \Delta E_p$$

When the non-conservative forces oppose the motion, the work done by the non-conservative forces is negative, causing a decrease in the mechanical energy of the system. When the non-conservative forces do positive work, energy is added to the system. If the sum of the non-conservative forces is zero, then mechanical energy is conserved.

### Solution

Friction stops the player by converting his kinetic energy into other forms, including thermal energy. Thus $W_{\text{non-conservative}} = -F_f\Delta x$.

There is no change in potential energy.

#### Step 2: Next we calculate the distance using the conservation of energy
We begin with conservation of energy:

$$W_{\text{non-conservative}} = \Delta EK + \Delta PE$$

The equation expands to:

$$\begin{aligned}
W_{\text{non-conservative}} &= \Delta E_k + \Delta E_p \\
&= E_{k,f} - E_{k,i} + (0) \\
-F_f\Delta x &= E_{k,f} - E_{k,i} \\
-F_f\Delta x &= (0) - E_{k,i} \\
F_f\Delta x &= \frac{1}{2}mv_{i}^2 \\
\Delta x &= \frac{mv_{i}^2}{2F_f} \\
&= \frac{(65,0)(6,00)^2}{2(450)} \\
&= 2,60\text{ m}
\end{aligned}$$

#### Step 3: Quote the final answer
The footballer comes to a stop after sliding for $2,60\text{ m}$.

## 5.5 Power (ESCMJ)

Now that we understand the relationship between work and energy, we are ready to look at a quantity related the rate of energy transfer. For example, a mother pushing a trolley full of groceries can take $30\text{ s}$ or $60\text{ s}$ to push the trolley down an aisle. She does the same amount of work, but takes a different length of time. We use the idea of *power* to describe the rate at which work is done.

### DEFINITION: Power

Power is defined as the rate at which work is done or the rate at which energy is transfered to or from a system. The mathematical definition for power is:

$$P = \frac{W}{t}$$

---

Power is easily derived from the definition of work. We know that: $W = F \Delta x \cos \theta$

Power is defined as the rate at which work is done. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= \frac{W}{t} \\
&= \frac{F \Delta x \cos \theta}{t}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the case where $F$ and $\Delta x$ are in the same direction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&= \frac{F \Delta x}{t} \\
&= F \frac{\Delta x}{t} \\
&= Fv
\end{aligned}
$$

---

### IMPORTANT!

In the case where the force and the velocity are in opposite directions the power will be negative.

---

The unit of power is watt (symbol $\text{W}$).

---

### FACT

The unit watt is named after Scottish inventor and engineer James Watt ($19$ January $1736$ – $19$ August $1819$) whose improvements to the steam engine were fundamental to the Industrial Revolution. A key feature of it was that it brought the engine out of the remote coal fields into factories.

### FACT

Historically, the *horsepower* (symbol $\text{hp}$) was the unit used to describe the power delivered by a machine. One horsepower is equivalent to approximately $750\text{ W}$. The horsepower was derived by James Watt to give an indication of the power of his steam engine in terms of the power of a horse, which was what most people used to for example, turn a mill wheel.

P &= \frac{W}{t} \\
&= \frac{\frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 + mgh}{t} \\
&= \frac{\frac{1}{2}(60,0)(2,00)^2 + (60,0)(9,80)(3,00)}{3,50} \\
&= \frac{120 + 1764}{3,50} \\
&= 538,3\text{ W}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Step 3: Quote the final answer
The power generated is $538,0\text{ W}$.

The woman does $1764\text{ J}$ of work to move up the stairs compared with only $120\text{ J}$ to increase her kinetic energy; thus, most of her power output is required for climbing rather than accelerating.

## 5.6 Chapter summary

### Concepts & Definitions

* **Kinetic energy:** $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$
* **Conservation of Energy:** Energy is never created nor destroyed, but is only transformed from one form to another.
* **Conservation of Mechanical Energy:** In the absence of non-conservative forces, mechanical energy is conserved.
* **Work:** When a force acting on an object has a component along the line of motion, work is done. Work is the process of transferring energy from one object or system to another.
* **Energy:** Energy is the ability to do work.

### 1. Concepts & Structure

#### Worked Example: Work-Energy Theorem (Bullet Penetration Problem)

**Problem Statement:**
A bullet traveling at $100\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ just pierces a wooden plank of $5\text{ m}$. What should be the speed (in $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$) of the bullet to pierce a wooden plank of the same material, but having a thickness of $10\text{ m}$?

**Concept Notes & Principles:**
* The final speed and hence final kinetic energy are zero in both cases as the bullet comes to rest.
* According to the **Work-Energy Theorem**, the net work done on an object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy ($\Delta K = W_{\text{net}}$). Here, the initial kinetic energy is equal in magnitude to the work done by the resisting force of the material.
* Since the material is the same, the resisting force $F$ is assumed to be constant and identical in both cases.

---

### 2. Mathematics & Formulas

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

Let subscripts $1$ and $2$ denote the two cases (thickness of $5\text{ m}$ and $10\text{ m}$ respectively).

**Case 1: Thickness $\Delta x_1 = 5\text{ m}$**

Applying the work-energy theorem:
$$0 - \frac{1}{2}mv_{1}^2 = -Fx_1$$

Substitute the known values ($v_1 = 100\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ and $x_1 = 5\text{ m}$):
$$\frac{1}{2}m(100)^2 = F \times 5$$

**Case 2: Thickness $\Delta x_2 = 10\text{ m}$**

Applying the work-energy theorem:
$$0 - \frac{1}{2}mv_{2}^2 = -Fx_2$$

$$\frac{1}{2}mv_{2}^2 = F \times 10$$

**Ratio Method:**

Take the ratio of the two equations to eliminate the mass ($m$) and the constant force ($F$):

$$\frac{\frac{1}{2}mv_{2}^2}{\frac{1}{2}m(100)^2} = \frac{F \times 10}{F \times 5}$$

$$\frac{v_{2}^2}{(100)^2} = \frac{10}{5} = 2$$

$$v_{2}^2 = 2 \times (100)^2$$

$$v_{2}^2 = 2 \times 10000 = 20000$$

$$v_2 = \sqrt{20000} = 141,4\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

**Final Answer:**
$$141,4\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

3. Jack and Jill ran up the hill. Jack is twice as massive as Jill; yet Jill ascended the same distance in half the time. Who did the most work? Who delivered the most power? Explain your answers.

   With comparative problems we always write the values for one of the objects/people in terms of the values for the other. So:

   $$m_{Jack} = 2 \times m_{Jill}$$
   $$\frac{1}{2}t_{Jack} = t_{Jill}$$
   $$t_{Jack} = 2 \times t_{Jill}$$

   $$W_{Jill} = m_{Jill}gh$$
   $$W_{Jack} = m_{Jack}gh$$
   $$W_{Jack} = (2 \times m_{Jill})gh$$
   $$W_{Jack} = 2 \times (m_{Jill}gh)$$
   $$W_{Jack} = 2 \times W_{Jill}$$

   The power comparison is similar:

   $$P_{Jill} = \frac{W_{Jill}}{t_{Jill}}$$
   $$P_{Jack} = \frac{W_{Jack}}{t_{Jack}}$$
   $$P_{Jack} = \frac{2 \times W_{Jill}}{2 \times t_{Jill}}$$
   $$P_{Jack} = \frac{W_{Jill}}{t_{Jill}}$$
   $$P_{Jack} = P_{Jill}$$

   Jack did twice as much work as Jill but the same power.
