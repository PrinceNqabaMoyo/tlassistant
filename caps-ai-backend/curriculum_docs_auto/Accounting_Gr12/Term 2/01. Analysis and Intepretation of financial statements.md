## 1. Introduction to Concepts

The analysis and interpretation of financial statements is an evaluation process used to assess the current and previous financial positions and performance results of an enterprise.

A **ratio** is the mathematical relationship between two different figures, expressed in simple terms. To be useful, ratios must express meaningful relationships and be compared against benchmarks or comparative figures from previous periods.

### The Aims of Financial Analysis
The primary goals of analyzing financial statements are to determine:

| Concept | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1.1 Profitability** | A measure of business success by comparing profit made against the amount sold or invested. It also helps identify potential cost control problems. |
| **1.2 Return** | Determines if the investors (owners) are receiving a reasonable return on the capital they have invested in the business. |
| **1.3 Solvency** | The ability of the business to meet its financial obligations on time and to pay off long-term debts. |

# Analysis of Financial Statements

Financial analysis involves evaluating the performance and financial position of a business using various ratios. These ratios help stakeholders understand the liquidity, solvency, risk, and profitability of the entity.

---

## 1. Liquidity of the Business
Liquidity measures the ability of a business to meet its short-term financial obligations.

*   **Current ratio:** Measures the ability to pay current liabilities with current assets.
*   **Acid test ratio:** A stricter measure of liquidity, excluding inventory from current assets.
*   **Rate of inventory turnover:** Indicates how many times inventory is sold and replaced during a period.
*   **Number of months' inventory on hand:** Shows how long the current stock will last.
*   **Average debtors' collection period:** The average time taken to collect cash from credit customers.
*   **Average creditors' payment period:** The average time taken to pay credit suppliers.

## 2. Solvency of the Business
Solvency measures the ability of a business to meet its long-term financial obligations.
*   **Solvency ratio:** Total assets compared to total liabilities.

## 3. Financial Risk
*   **Debt/Equity ratio (Gearing ratio):** Measures the proportion of debt used to finance the business relative to the owners' equity.

## 4. Return on Investment
*   **Percentage return on average owners' equity:** Measures the profitability of the investment made by the owners.

## 5. Profitability: Gross Profit
Gross profit is the difference between the selling price of goods and the cost of producing or purchasing those goods.

### Core Concepts
*   **Gross Profit on Sales:** Expresses gross profit as a percentage of the total sales revenue.
*   **Gross Profit on Cost of Sales (Mark-up):** Expresses gross profit as a percentage of the cost price.

### Worked Example: Calculating Sales Price
If a business applies a fixed mark-up policy of $25\%$ on the cost price:

| Item | Amount (R) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Cost price of an article | $3\,000$ |
| $25\%$ mark-up on cost ($3\,000 \times 0.25$) | $750$ |
| **Sales price** | **$3\,750$** |

### Mathematical Formulas

**1. Gross Profit Calculation:**
$$\text{Gross Profit} = \text{Sales} - \text{Cost of Sales}$$

**2. Gross Profit on Sales Percentage:**
$$\frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Sales}} \times 100$$

**3. Gross Profit on Cost (Mark-up) Percentage:**
$$\frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Cost of Sales}} \times 100$$

## Calculating Gross Profit Percentages

Gross profit can be expressed as a percentage of either **Sales (Turnover)** or **Cost of Sales**.

### 1. Gross Profit as a Percentage of Sales
To calculate the gross profit percentage based on sales, use the following formula:

$$\frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Sales}} \times \frac{100}{1} = \text{Percentage}$$

**Example:**
If Gross Profit is R750 and Sales are R3 750:
$$\frac{750}{3 750} \times \frac{100}{1} = 20\%$$

### 2. Gross Profit as a Percentage of Cost of Sales
To calculate the gross profit percentage based on cost of sales, use the following formula:

$$\frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Cost of Sales}} \times \frac{100}{1} = \text{Percentage}$$

**Example:**
If Gross Profit is R750 and Cost of Sales is R3 000:
$$\frac{750}{3 000} \times \frac{100}{1} = 25\%$$

---

## Key Concept: Relationship between Mark-up and Margin
A profit mark-up of **20% on sales** is equivalent to a profit mark-up of **25% on cost of sales**. If a business maintains this mark-up consistently, these percentages will remain constant in the final Income Statement.

### Worked Example: Income Statement Analysis
Consider the following financial data for a business:

| Item | Amount (R) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Sales (240 articles) | 900 000 |
| Less: Cost of sales | (720 000) |
| **Gross profit** | **180 000** |

#### Analysis of the above data:

**Gross profit expressed as a percentage of sales:**
$$\frac{180 000}{900 000} \times \frac{100}{1} = 20\%$$

**Gross profit expressed as a percentage of cost of sales:**
$$\frac{180 000}{720 000} \times \frac{100}{1} = 25\%$$

## Financial Ratios

### a) Percentage Gross Profit on Turnover
This ratio expresses the gross profit of a financial year as a percentage of the net sales (turnover). If a business maintains a constant mark-up policy, this percentage should remain stable.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Percentage gross profit on turnover} = \frac{\text{Gross profit}}{\text{Turnover}} \times 100$$

**Reasons for variation in this ratio:**
*   Theft of inventory.
*   Incorrect sales calculations.
*   Granting of trade discounts.

---

### b) Percentage Gross Profit on Cost of Sales
This ratio determines whether the purchasing policy and inventory storage are being managed successfully. A decrease in this percentage compared to the previous year may indicate theft in the warehouse.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Percentage gross profit on cost of sales} = \frac{\text{Gross profit}}{\text{Cost of sales}} \times 100$$

---

### c) Percentage Operating Profit on Turnover
This ratio represents the operating profit as a percentage of turnover. It accounts for income and expenses before interest is considered. It is used to evaluate how effectively business expenses are being managed.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Percentage operating profit on turnover} = \frac{\text{Operating profit}}{\text{Turnover}} \times 100$$

*   **Note:** A lower percentage indicates that business expenses are being managed more effectively.

### d) Net profit on turnover
This ratio represents the net profit for a financial period as a percentage of sales.

## 3.2 Liquidity

Liquidity refers to the ability of a business to meet its short-term obligations (current liabilities) using available funds (current assets).

### a) Current ratio
The current ratio measures the extent to which current assets cover current liabilities.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Current ratio} = \text{Current assets} : \text{Current liabilities}$$

**Analysis Notes:**
*   The ratio is expressed as $x : 1$.
*   It is calculated by dividing current assets by current liabilities.
*   **Interpretation:** A higher ratio indicates a greater ability to meet short-term obligations.
*   **Example:** A ratio of $2 : 1$ means there are $\text{R}2$ of current assets available for every $\text{R}1$ of current liabilities.

---

## b) Acid Test Ratio
The acid test ratio is a measure of liquidity that excludes inventories, as these cannot always be immediately converted into cash (due to potential damage, obsolescence, or theft).

**Formula:**
$$\text{Acid test ratio} = (\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventories}) : \text{Current liabilities}$$

*   **Note:** Inventories include both trading stock and consumable stores on hand.
*   **Interpretation:** The ratio is expressed as $x : 1$. A business is considered liquid if the ratio is greater than $1$ (e.g., $1,2 : 1$ means for every $R1$ of current liability, there is $R1,20$ of liquid assets available).

---

## 3.3 Solvency of the Business
Solvency refers to the ability of a business to meet its long-term financial obligations.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Solvency ratio} = \text{Total assets} : \text{Total liabilities}$$

**Component Definitions:**
*   $$\text{Total assets} = \text{Tangible assets} + \text{Fixed deposit} + \text{Current assets}$$
*   $$\text{Total liabilities} = \text{Non-current liabilities} + \text{Current liabilities}$$

**Solvency Status:**
*   **Solvent:** $\text{Total assets} > \text{Total liabilities}$
*   **Insolvent:** $\text{Total assets} < \text{Total liabilities}$
*   **Interpretation:** The ratio is expressed as $x : 1$. A business is regarded as solvent if the ratio is not lower than $1 : 1$.

---

## 3.4 Risk
The debt/equity ratio indicates how a business is financed, specifically the proportion of borrowed money versus money provided by the owners.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Debt/equity ratio} = \text{Non-current liabilities} : \text{Owners' equity}$$

**Calculation Note:**
*   $$\text{Owners' equity} = \text{Capital balances of partners} + \text{Current account balances of partners}$$
*   If a partner's current account has a debit balance, that amount must be subtracted.

## Borrowed Capital and Financial Risk
When a business borrows money, it must pay interest. Relying heavily on borrowed capital increases financial risk. If profits decline, the business must still repay the loan and interest, which can cause significant financial strain.

### The Debt-to-Equity Ratio
This ratio indicates the extent to which a business is financed by borrowed capital versus owner's equity. It helps stakeholders assess the level of risk the business has taken on.

#### Interpretation of Debt-to-Equity Ratios
*   **$1 : 1$**: Regarded as acceptable. For every $R1$ invested by owners, there is $R1$ of borrowed funds.
*   **$0,7 : 1$**: Low-risk business (favourable gearing). The business relies more on equity than debt.
*   **$1,3 : 1$**: High-risk business (negative gearing). The business relies heavily on borrowed capital, often resulting in a poor credit rating.
*   **Ideal range for lenders**: Financial providers (banks) generally prefer a ratio between $0,5 : 1$ and $1 : 1$.

## 3.5 The Return on Investment for the Owners of the Business

This indicator measures how profitable the owners' investment is, allowing them to compare the business's performance against alternative investments (e.g., fixed deposits).

### Formula: Percentage Return on Average Owners' Equity
The formula for calculating the percentage return is:

$$\text{Percentage return} = \frac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Average owners' equity}} \times \frac{100}{1}$$

| Indicator | Calculation Component |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Numerator** | Net profit |
| **Denominator** | Average owners' equity |
| **Multiplier** | $100$ |

### Decision Making for Owners
Before deciding whether to continue investing in the business or to sell and invest elsewhere, owners must consider:
*   The future prospects and growth potential of the business.
*   The safety or risk associated with alternative investments.
*   Current rates of return available in the money market.
*   General economic conditions within the country.

## 1.5 Percentage Return on Average Owners' Equity

### Formula
To calculate the percentage return on average owners' equity, use the following formula:

$$\text{Return on Average Equity} = \frac{\text{Net Profit after Tax}}{\text{Average Owners' Equity}} \times 100$$

Where:
$$\text{Average Owners' Equity} = \frac{\text{Opening Balance} + \text{Closing Balance}}{2}$$

### Commenting on the Percentage
When commenting on this ratio, consider the following:
* **Comparison:** Compare the result against the previous year's performance or the industry average.
* **Investment Viability:** A higher percentage indicates that the business is generating a better return for the owners on the capital they have invested.
* **Risk:** If the return is lower than the interest rate on alternative investments (like a fixed deposit), the business may not be performing efficiently.

# Cost Accounting: Fixed and Variable Costs

## 1. Core Concepts

### Fixed Costs
Fixed costs are expenses that remain constant regardless of the level of production or the number of items produced. Even if production volume increases or decreases, these costs do not change.

### Variable Costs
Variable costs are expenses that change in direct proportion to the number of items produced. As production increases, total variable costs increase; as production decreases, total variable costs decrease.

---

## 2. Worked Example: Profit Calculation

To calculate the profit of a production run, we use the following formula:

$$\text{Profit} = \text{Selling Price (SP)} - \text{Variable Costs (VC)} - \text{Fixed Costs (FC)}$$

**Scenario:**
*   Number of units produced: $100$
*   Variable cost per unit: $R99$
*   Fixed costs: $R1\,750$
*   Selling price per unit: $R150$

**Calculation:**
1.  **Total Variable Cost:** $100 \text{ units} \times R99 = R9\,900$
2.  **Total Selling Price (Revenue):** $100 \text{ units} \times R150 = R15\,000$
3.  **Profit:**
    $$\text{Profit} = R15\,000 - R9\,900 - R1\,750$$
    $$\text{Profit} = R3\,350$$
