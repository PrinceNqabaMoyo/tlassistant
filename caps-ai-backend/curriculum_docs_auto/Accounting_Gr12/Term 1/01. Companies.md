# Chapter 2: Companies

## 2.1 Concepts relating to companies

### Persons in a company

| Concept | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Directors** | People who are appointed by the shareholders to run the company. |
| **Independent auditor (external)** | An auditor who expresses an opinion on the financial statements in an auditor’s report but does not work for the company. |
| **Internal auditor** | An auditor who supervises the preparation of the financial statements, is responsible for internal control and is employed by the company. |
| **Shareholders** | People who own the company. |
| **South African Revenue Services (SARS)** | The government department to whom the company must pay income tax on the profits and VAT when due. |

### Documents relating to companies

| Concept | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Auditor’s report** | An opinion given by a qualified person on whether the financial statements are reliable or not. |
| • Qualified auditor’s report | When the auditors find the financial statements acceptable EXCEPT for some aspects that need to be changed, fixed or investigated. |
| • Unqualified auditor’s report | When the auditors find the financial statements acceptable in ALL respects. |
| • Disclaimer | When the auditors are not prepared to express an opinion on the financial statements (because they are too unreliable). |
| **Balance Sheet** | This statement reflects the assets, liabilities and net worth (owners’ equity of the company). Another term for this is ‘Statement of Financial Position’. |
| **Cash Flow Statement** | This shows the flow of cash in a company (money coming in and money going out). |
| **Income Statement** | This statement shows the profit or loss made from business operations (income and expenses). Another term for this is ‘Statement of Comprehensive Income’. |
| **Tax assessment** | This is issued by SARS to confirm the amount of income tax which the company has to pay based on profits. |

# Chapter 2: Further Concepts Relating to Companies

## Core Company Concepts

Understanding the terminology used in corporate accounting is essential for financial reporting and analysis.

| Concept | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Authorised share capital** | The maximum number of shares a company is legally permitted to sell. |
| **Dividends** | The portion of profits (after tax) approved for distribution to shareholders. Total dividends = Interim + Final. |
| **Interim dividends** | Dividends paid to shareholders during the financial year. |
| **Final dividends** | Dividends recommended to shareholders at the end of the financial year. |
| **Income tax** | The tax paid by the company to SARS on its annual profits. |
| **Issued share capital** | The number of shares actually sold to shareholders. Use this figure to calculate dividends. |
| **Limited liability** | Shareholder liability is restricted to their investment; personal assets are protected. |
| **No par value** | Shares have no fixed monetary value until they are issued. |
| **Provisional tax** | Payments made to SARS twice a year based on estimated profits. |
| **Retained income** | Profits after tax that are not distributed as dividends but reinvested for company growth. |
| **Issue price** | The price at which shares are offered to the public. |
| **Shareholders earnings** | Net profit after tax. |
| **Shareholders for dividends** | The liability representing dividends declared but not yet paid. |
| **Buy back shares** | Issued shares repurchased by the company and subsequently retired or cancelled. |

## GAAP Concepts (Generally Accepted Accounting Practice)

These principles ensure consistency, transparency, and accuracy in financial reporting.

| Concept | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Business entity rule** | Company finances must be kept strictly separate from the personal finances of shareholders. |
| **Going concern** | Financial statements assume the company will continue to operate in the foreseeable future. |
| **Historical cost** | Assets are recorded at their original purchase price. |
| **Matching** | Income and expenses must be recorded in the same financial year to which they relate (e.g., sales and cost of sales). |
| **Materiality** | Significant items must be disclosed separately in financial statements (e.g., directors' fees). |
| **Prudence** | Financial figures should be realistic and conservative. Avoid overstating assets or income. For example, reporting 'net debtors' (Trade Debtors minus Provision for Bad Debts) reflects a realistic expectation of cash inflow. |

# 2.2 Company General Ledger Accounts

In company accounting, specific ledger accounts are used to track equity, tax obligations, and dividend distributions.

### Key Ledger Accounts
The following are the most commonly examined company ledger accounts:
*   **Ordinary share capital:** Represents the equity contributed by shareholders.
*   **SARS (Income tax):** Represents the liability or asset balance regarding corporate tax.
*   **Shareholders for dividends:** Represents the liability to pay declared dividends.
*   **Income tax:** The expense account for tax incurred during the year.
*   **Dividends on ordinary shares:** The distribution of profits to shareholders.
*   **Appropriation account:** The account used to close off net profit and distribute dividends.

> **NB:** SARS (Income tax) implies a liability owing to SARS.

### Notes on Company Financial Statements

1. **Opening Balances:** Balances for SARS (Income Tax) and Shareholders for Dividends represent amounts unpaid from the previous financial year.
2. **Share Issues:** Shares issued to the public are recorded at the issue price (e.g., R7,50 per share).
3. **Settlement of Liabilities:** Amounts owing to SARS and shareholders from the previous year are settled during the current year.
4. **Provisional Tax:** The first payment is made 6 months into the financial year; the second payment is made at the end of the financial year.
5. **Interim Dividends:** Paid during the financial year.
6. **Share Buy-backs:** Shares bought back at a specific price (e.g., R8,50). The new average price must be calculated.
   * Calculation: $\frac{\text{Value of Ordinary Share Capital}}{\text{Number of Shares}} = \text{Average Price}$
   * Example: $\frac{R1\,375\,000}{55\,000} = R2,50$ per share. The difference between the buy-back price and the average price is debited to Retained Income.
8. **Final Dividends:** Declared at the end of the financial year but not yet paid.
9. **Income Tax:** The tax figure for the year is calculated on the net profit. This is compared to provisional tax payments to determine if the company owes SARS (liability) or is owed a refund (asset). Net profit is transferred from the Profit and Loss Account to the Appropriation Account.
10. **Final Accounts:** These include the Trading Account, Profit and Loss Account, and the Appropriation Account.

# 2.3 Preparation of Financial Statements for Companies

## Income Statement
To prepare an Income Statement from a pre-adjustment trial balance, follow these systematic steps:

1. **Initial Entry:** Enter the pre-adjustment trial balance figures from the nominal section onto the answer sheet next to the corresponding detail.
2. **Process Adjustments:** Read each adjustment carefully:
    * **a)** Calculate the adjustment amount if necessary.
    * **b)** Determine the debit and credit accounts for the adjustment.
    * **c)** Reflect the adjustment on your answer sheet using a $(+)$ or $(-)$ sign next to the pre-adjustment figure:
        * **Outstanding/Accrued amounts:** Add $(+)$
        * **Prepaid/Received in advance amounts:** Subtract $(-)$
3. **Final Calculation:** Once all adjustments are processed, calculate the final figures and record them in the final column.

> **Note:** A pre-adjustment trial balance refers to a trial balance that is not final. It requires adjustments (entries) to be made to finalize the figures used in the preparation of the annual financial statements.

# Chapter 2: Revision of Financial Adjustments

The degree of difficulty of these adjustments lies in the calculation of the figure, but the entry format remains consistent. Ensure you memorize the standard formats for these adjustments.

## List of Adjustments

1.  **Prepaid expenses:** Insurance prepaid, $R200$.
2.  **Accrued expenses:** Water and electricity still due, $R2\,000$.
3.  **Income received in advance:** Rent income received in advance, $R1\,000$.
4.  **Accrued income:** Interest on fixed deposit still due, $R400$ (not capitalized).
5.  **Bank charges:** $R700$; interest on overdraft, $R200$ on late bank statement to be brought into account.
6.  **RD cheque:** Bank statement showed an RD cheque of $R1\,000$ (received from debtor B. Bam in settlement of his account of $R1\,100$).
7.  **Interest capitalized:** Loan balance brought down $R55\,000$ (1 July 2011). Total payments $R15\,000$. Closing balance of loan $R50\,000$.
8.  **Depreciation:** 
    *   Vehicles: $10\%$ p.a. at cost price.
    *   Equipment: $10\%$ p.a. at carrying value.
9.  **Bad debts:** J Jon's debt written off as bad debt, $R200$.
10. **Provision for bad debts:** Increase provision to $5\%$ of debtors. Calculation: $(17\,000 \times 5\% = \dots)$.

# Accounting Adjustments: Rent Income

When rent increases during a financial year, adjustments are required to ensure the correct amount is recorded in the Income Statement and the Balance Sheet.

---

## 3. Adjustment: Rent Received in Advance
**Scenario:** Total rent received is $R27\,200$. Rent increased by $10\%$ on $1$ October $2013$. The tenant paid for March $2014$ in advance.

### Calculation using a Time Line
| Month | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec | Jan | Feb |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rate** | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $110\%$ | $110\%$ | $110\%$ | $110\%$ | $110\%$ |

**Step 1: Determine total percentage**
$$(100\% \times 7) + (110\% \times 5) = 700\% + 550\% = 1250\%$$
*(Note: The total period including the prepaid month is $1360\%$)*

**Step 2: Apply Formula**
$$\text{Amount in advance} = \frac{\text{Unknown } \%}{\text{Known } \%} \times \text{Total Received}$$
$$\frac{110\%}{1360\%} \times R27\,200 = R2\,200$$

**Step 3: Final Rent Income**
$$R27\,200 - R2\,200 = R25\,000$$

| Statement | Account | Amount |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Income Statement** | Rent Income | $R25\,000$ |

### Adjustment: Rent Income (High Level)

When rent changes during the financial year and there is outstanding (accrued) rent, use a time line to determine the correct income for the year and the amount still owed by the tenant.

#### Example Scenario
*   **Total rent received:** $R22\,800$
*   **Adjustment:** Rent decreased by $10\%$ on $1$ October $2013$.
*   **Condition:** The tenant has not yet paid the rent for February $2014$.

#### Time Line Calculation
To find the rent for the year and the accrued income, map the percentages across the financial year:

| Month | Mar | Apr | May | June | July | Aug | Sept | Oct | Nov | Dec | Jan | Feb |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **%** | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $100\%$ | $90\%$ | $90\%$ | $90\%$ | $90\%$ | $90\%$ |

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Calculation of total percentage:**
$$(100\% \times 7 \text{ months}) + (90\% \times 4 \text{ months}) = 22\,800$$
$$700\% + 360\% = 1\,060\% = 22\,800$$

#### Formula for Accrued Income
Use the following formula to calculate the amount owed by the tenant:
$$\frac{\text{Unknown \%}}{\text{Known \%}} \times \text{Total rent received}$$

*   **Unknown %:** $90\%$ (The rent for February, which is outstanding)
*   **Known %:** $1\,060\%$ (The total percentage of rent received)

$$\frac{90\%}{1\,060\%} \times 22\,800 = R2\,200 \text{ (Accrued Income)}$$

**Total Rent Income for the year:**
$$22\,800 + 2\,200 = R25\,000$$

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

# 2.4 Cash Flow Statements

## Purpose of a Cash Flow Statement
While the **Income Statement** shows operational results (net profit) and the **Balance Sheet** shows the financial position at a specific date, neither explains the movement of cash. The Cash Flow Statement is prepared to show exactly where the business receives its funds and how those funds are utilized.

## Basic Concepts and Terminology
Cash Flow Statements focus exclusively on the movement of cash. Remember: **CASH IS KING**.

| Concept | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Cash inflow** | Money coming into the business (represented as a positive number). |
| **Cash outflow** | Money going out of the business (represented in brackets, e.g., $(5\,000)$). |

---

## Worked Example: Calculating Cash Flow
To prepare a Cash Flow Statement, you must calculate the difference between the current year's figures and the previous year's figures.

*   The company repurchased $3\,000$ shares at $820$ cents per share.

### Mathematical Calculation of Cash Flow
To find the cash flow for an item, use the formula:
$$\text{Cash Flow} = \text{Current Year} - \text{Previous Year}$$

**Example: Fixed Deposit**
$$\text{Cash Flow} = 28\,000 - 23\,000 = 5\,000$$
Since the asset increased, cash was used to purchase the deposit, resulting in an **outflow** of $(R5\,000)$.

**Example: Loan from Beta Bank**
$$\text{Cash Flow} = 74\,000 - 80\,000 = -6\,000$$
Since the liability decreased, the business paid back the loan, resulting in an **outflow** of $(R6\,000)$.

# Chapter 2: Financial Analysis and Interpretation

This chapter focuses on the analysis and interpretation of company financial statements. The following mind map outlines the key categories and ratios used to evaluate company performance.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

## 1. Financial Risk and Gearing
This category assesses the company's reliance on borrowed funds (loans) versus shareholders' equity.
*   **Debt/Equity Ratio:** Measures the proportion of debt to equity.
*   **% Return on Total Capital Employed:** Measures how effectively the company uses its total capital (equity + loans) to generate profit.

## 2. Return (Shareholders' Earning)
These indicators measure the returns generated for shareholders.
*   **Earnings per Share (EPS):** The portion of a company's profit allocated to each outstanding share of common stock.
*   **Dividends per Share:** The total dividends paid out per share.
*   **% Return on Average Shareholders' Equity:** Measures the profitability of the shareholders' investment.

## 3. Solvency
Solvency measures the company's long-term ability to meet its financial obligations.
*   **Solvency Ratio:** Indicates if the company has enough assets to cover all its debts.
*   **Net Asset Value (NAV):** The value of a company's assets minus its liabilities.

## 4. Liquidity
Liquidity measures the company's ability to pay its immediate (short-term) debts.
*   **Current Ratio:** Compares current assets to current liabilities.
*   **Acid Test Ratio:** A stricter measure of liquidity, excluding stock from current assets.
*   **Net Current Assets:** Working capital (Current Assets - Current Liabilities).
*   **Turnover Rate of Stock:** How quickly stock is sold and replaced.
*   **Debtors' Collection Period:** The average time taken to collect cash from debtors.
*   **Creditors' Payment Period:** The average time taken to pay creditors.
*   **Average Period of Stock on Hand:** The average number of days stock is held before being sold.

## 5. Profitability and Efficiency
These ratios measure how well the company manages its expenses and generates profit from sales.
*   **% Gross Profit on Sales:** Gross profit as a percentage of net sales.
*   **% Net Profit on Sales:** Net profit as a percentage of net sales.
*   **% Operating Expenses on Sales:** Operating expenses as a percentage of net sales.
*   **% Operating Profit on Sales:** Operating profit as a percentage of net sales.
*   **% Gross Profit on Cost of Sales:** Gross profit expressed as a percentage of the cost of goods sold.
*   **Mark-up:** The percentage added to the cost price to determine the selling price.

## Key Concepts

Assets are resources controlled by the entity as a result of past events and from which future economic benefits are expected to flow.

### 7. Solvency Ratio
The solvency ratio indicates the ability of a company to meet its long-term financial obligations.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Solvency ratio} = \text{Total assets} : \text{Total liabilities}$$

**Calculation:**
$$\text{Solvency ratio} = 7\,183\,000 \div (1\,980\,000 + 1\,080\,000)$$
$$\text{Solvency ratio} = 7\,183\,000 \div 3\,060\,000$$
$$\text{Solvency ratio} = 2,3 : 1$$

---

### 8. Net Asset Value (NAV) per Share
The Net Asset Value per share represents the value of the company's net assets attributable to each ordinary share.

**Formula:**
$$\text{NAV per share} = \frac{\text{Ordinary shareholders' equity}}{\text{Number of shares issued}} \times 100$$

**Calculation:**
$$\text{NAV per share} = \frac{4\,123\,000}{1\,100\,000} \times 100$$
$$\text{NAV per share} = 374,8 \text{ cents per share}$$

*Note: Multiply by 100 to convert the Rand value into cents per share.*

---

### 9. Earnings per Share (EPS)
Earnings per share indicates the portion of a company's profit allocated to each outstanding share of common stock.

**Formula:**
$$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Net profit after tax}}{\text{Number of shares issued}} \times 100$$

**Calculation:**
$$\text{EPS} = \frac{904\,000}{1\,100\,000} \times 100$$
$$\text{EPS} = 82,2 \text{ cents per share}$$

*Note: Multiply by 100 to convert the Rand value into cents per share.*

# 2.6 Comments on an Audit Report

Financial statements are essential documents because various stakeholders rely on them to assess the performance and health of a business.

## Stakeholders and Their Interests

| Group of people interested | Reasons for interest in financial results |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Owners (Shareholders)** | Interested in the overall health and performance of the business. |
| **Potential owners** | Interested in evaluating the business for potential investment. |
| **Management (Board of directors)** | Use reports for planning and maintaining good business practices. |
| **Banks** | Interested in asset coverage to ensure loans can be repaid. |
| **Employees and trade unions** | Interested in profitability to negotiate wage increases. |
| **SARS** | Interested in profit/loss to determine tax obligations. |
| **Auditor** | Reports to shareholders on whether financial statements are a fair reflection. |

---

## The Independent Auditor

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Function of the Independent Auditor
* **Assurance:** The auditor signs an Auditor's Report, which provides assurance to shareholders that the financial statements are reliable.
* **Opinion:** The auditor is not required to check every transaction or specifically hunt for fraud. Their primary function is to provide an **opinion** on whether the financial records are a "true and fair" representation of the company's operations.
* **Decision Making:** Shareholders rely on these "true and fair" statements to make informed business decisions.
* **Duty to Report:** If an auditor becomes aware of fraud, they have a legal and professional duty to report it to the shareholders.
* **Ethics:** Auditors are bound by strict ethical standards and face disciplinary action if found negligent.

### Quality of Auditors
External auditors must be registered professionals with bodies such as the **South African Institute of Chartered Accountants (SAICA)** or the **Independent Regulatory Board for Auditors (IRBA)**.

**Advantages of engaging registered auditors:**
* Auditors are guided by a professional code of ethics.
* Companies are assured of high-quality work from qualified professionals.
* High standards are maintained because auditors are accountable to their professional bodies.
* Negligent auditors face disciplinary measures.
* Auditors can be deregistered for acts of misconduct.

---

### Auditor Liability and Responsibilities

*   Auditors can be sued for producing a misleading report.
*   Auditors may lose future contracts due to the production of substandard work.

---

### The Independent Auditor and the Audit Report

An auditor is a person who expresses an **opinion** on financial information and accounting controls.

*   **Appointment:** Appointed at the Annual General Meeting (AGM) by shareholders, not by directors.
*   **Status:** Cannot be an employee of the company.
*   **Remuneration:** Charges fees based on the number of hours spent on the audit.

#### The Auditor's Opinion
The independent auditor must assess whether the financial statements:
1.  Provide a fair representation of the company’s activity.
2.  Are understandable and not confusing to the reader.
3.  Are prepared in accordance with GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Practice).
4.  Are prepared in accordance with the requirements of the Companies Act.

---

### The Role of Internal Auditors

*   **Function:** Test internal controls and identify fraud or mistakes (e.g., debtors, wages, or computer entries).
*   **Status:** Employees of the business organization who earn a salary.
*   **Relationship:** The independent (external) auditor considers the checks carried out by the internal auditor.

---

### Types of Audit Reports

Auditors issue a report to the shareholders expressing an opinion on their findings.

| Report Type | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Qualified** | A report indicating some irregularities; the auditor must specify the nature of these irregularities to the shareholders. |
| **Unqualified** | A "good" report with only minor irregularities, if any. |
| **Withheld/Disclaimer** | A "very bad" report where the auditor recommends further investigations into outstanding irregularities before issuing a final report. |

# Chapter 2: Audit Reports and Corporate Governance

## Key Concepts and Definitions

### The Role of External Auditors
External auditors are registered professionals who must belong to professional bodies, such as the **South African Institute of Chartered Accountants (SAICA)**. Their role is to provide an independent assessment of a company's financial statements to protect shareholders and ensure professional standards are maintained.

### Asset Management
All fixed (tangible) assets must be recorded in an **Asset Register**. Each asset has a unique entry from the date of purchase until the date of disposal, including the calculation of yearly depreciation.

### Corporate Governance Principles (STARDIF)
When evaluating ethical business practices and management conduct, consider the following principles:
*   **Transparency:** Openness in financial reporting.
*   **Accountability:** Being answerable for decisions and actions.
*   **Responsible management:** Acting in the best interest of the company.
*   **Discipline:** Adhering to company policies and legal requirements.
*   **Fairness:** Treating all stakeholders equitably.

---

## Memorandum: Audit and Governance Exercises

### 1. Shareholder Satisfaction
*   **Status:** Satisfied.
*   **Reasoning:** An "unqualified report" indicates that the financial statements are fairly presented and the auditors found no irregularities.

### 2. Scope of Audit
*   **Reason:** Auditors are only legally responsible for the specific pages stipulated within their audit report (e.g., pages 8 to 20).

### 3. Requirement for Independent Audits
*   **Reason:** Public companies must be audited to provide shareholders with confidence that their investments are being managed correctly and that financial reporting is accurate.

### 4. Consequences of Auditor Negligence
If auditors (e.g., Barlow and Bokwe) fail to perform their duties, they may face:
*   Legal action (being sued).
*   Termination of their contract (not being re-appointed).
*   Disciplinary action by their professional body.

### 5. Verifying Fixed/Tangible Assets
To verify the Fixed Assets figure in the Balance Sheet, auditors should:
*   Examine the financial records of the business.
*   Assess the internal control systems of the business.
*   Assess the accounting principles applied.
*   Inspect the physical Asset Register.

### 6. Ethical Advice regarding Unissued Shares
*   **Advice:** It is unethical for a managing director to purchase unissued shares without advertising the issue to the public.
*   **Explanation:** As a public company, shares must be offered fairly. Allowing one individual to purchase them privately disadvantages other shareholders, dilutes their dividend earnings, and prevents the company from potentially raising more capital through an open market sale.

# Chapter 2: Audit Reports

## 1. Concepts and Definitions

An audit report is an official document issued by an independent auditor after examining a company's financial statements. It provides shareholders and potential investors with an opinion on the accuracy and reliability of the financial information.

### Types of Audit Reports

| Report Type | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Unqualified Report** | A "good" report indicating that the financial statements are presented fairly in all material respects and comply with International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). |
| **Qualified Report** | A report indicating that there are specific irregularities or issues. The auditor must state the nature of these irregularities, effectively "qualifying" their opinion on the statements. |

---

## 2. Worked Example: Analysis of Audit Reports

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Extracts from Audit Reports

**Kwela Ltd (Unqualified):**
"In our opinion, the financial statements fairly present, in all material respects, the financial position of this company at 28 February 2012 and the results of their operations and cash flows for the year ended in accordance with International Financial Reporting Standards, and in the manner required by the Companies Act of South Africa."

**Pomi Ltd (Qualified):**
"In our opinion, except for the effects of the company's overvaluation of its fixed assets, the financial statements fairly present the financial position of the company on 29 February 2012 and the results of their operations and cash flows for the year ended in accordance with International Financial Reporting Standards, and in the manner required by the Companies Act of South Africa."

## 3. Memorandum: Decision-Making for Investors

When deciding which company to invest in, an investor (like James) must interpret the audit report as follows:

### Analysis of Kwela Ltd
*   **Reliability:** James can rely on the figures provided in the financial statements.
*   **Fair Presentation:** The report confirms that the statements are fair in all material respects.
*   **Conclusion:** This is a "good" report, indicating high reliability for potential shareholders.

### Analysis of Pomi Ltd
*   **Reliability:** James cannot fully rely on the figures provided in the financial statements.
*   **Shortcomings:** The report explicitly draws attention to specific shortcomings (e.g., the overvaluation of fixed assets).
*   **Investment Risk:** Because the fixed assets are overvalued, the true net asset value of the company is uncertain, making the investment riskier.

# Chapter 2: Audit Reports and Corporate Governance

## 1. Concept: Withheld / Disclaimer Audit Report
A **Withheld (Disclaimer) Audit Report** is a negative audit outcome. It occurs when auditors are unable to express an opinion on the financial statements, often because they require further investigation into outstanding irregularities before they can issue a formal report.

### Key Auditor Responsibilities
*   **Duty to Report:** If an auditor becomes aware of fraud, they have a legal and professional duty to report this to the shareholders.
*   **Ethical Standards:** Auditors are bound by high ethical standards. If they are found to be negligent in their work, they may face disciplinary proceedings.

## 2. Case Study: Woodview Ltd (JSE Suspension)
**Context:** Woodview Ltd failed to publish its annual report within three months of its financial year-end.

*   **Consequences:**
    *   The JSE Securities Exchange suspended the trading of the company's shares.
    *   The Annual General Meeting (AGM) was postponed without informing shareholders of the reason.
    *   The share price dropped by $30\%$ to $140$ cents per share.
