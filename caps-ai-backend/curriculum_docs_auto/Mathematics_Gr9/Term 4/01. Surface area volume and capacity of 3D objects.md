## Investigating the Surface Area of Cylinders

In order to calculate the surface area of a cylinder, you need to know what shape the surfaces of the cylinder are.

* The surfaces of the top and base of a cylinder are made up of **circles**.
* The curved surface between the top and base of a cylinder can be unrolled to create a **rectangle**.

Surface area of a cylinder = Area of all its surfaces
$$= \text{Area of top} + \text{Area of base} + \text{Area of curved surface}$$
$$= \pi r^2 + \pi r^2 + (l \times b)$$
$$= 2\pi r^2 + (2\pi r \times h)$$
$$= 2\pi r(r + h)$$

> **Note:** Can you explain why the length of the rectangle is equal to the circumference of the top or base of the cylinder?

## 19.2 Volume

The **volume** of an object is the amount of space it occupies. We usually measure volume in cubic units, such as $\text{mm}^3$, $\text{cm}^3$ and $\text{m}^3$.

> **Note:** To convert between cubic units, remember:
> - $1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\ 000\text{ mm}^3$
> - $1\text{ m}^3 = 1\ 000\ 000\text{ cm}^3$

### Formulae for Volume of Prisms

The general formula for the volume of a prism is:
$$\text{Volume of a prism} = \text{Area of base} \times \text{height}$$

> **Note:** In case of a triangular prism, do not confuse the height of the base of the triangle ($h_b$) with the height of the prism ($h_p$).

## 19.3 Capacity

Remember that the **capacity** of an object is the amount of space *inside* the object. You can think of the capacity of an object as the amount of liquid that the object can hold.

The **volume** of an object is the amount of space that the object itself takes up.

The volume of a solid block of wood is $10\text{ cm} \times 6\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} = 240\text{ cm}^3$.

The same block of wood is carved out to make a hollow container with inside measurements of $8\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm}$. (Its walls are $1\text{ cm}$ thick.) The amount of space inside the container must be calculated using the *inside* measurements. So, the capacity of the container is $8\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm} = 96\text{ cm}^3$.

* A. Solid block with outside measurements
* B. Hollowed block with inside measurements

#### Mathematical background

The surface area of an object is the sum of the areas of all the faces and is measured in square units. A formula that can be used to calculate the surface area of a prism or a cylinder is:

$$\text{Surface area} = 2 \times \text{area of the base} + \text{the perimeter} \times \text{the height of the prism}$$

The volume of an object is the amount of space it takes up. The formula to calculate the volume of a prism or a cylinder is:

$$\text{Volume} = \text{area of base} \times \text{height}$$

Three dimensions are involved (namely length, breadth and height), so volume is measured in cubic units, such as cubic centimetres ($\text{cm}^3$) and cubic metres ($\text{m}^3$).

* A cubic centimetre is a cube with sides that are $1\text{ cm}$ each.
* A cubic metre is a cube with sides that are $1\text{ m}$ each.

The difference between volume and capacity is:
* **volume** is the amount of space an object takes up
* **capacity** is the amount of content a container can hold if it is filled to capacity.

An object can have both volume and capacity.

If volume is measured in cubic centimetres ($\text{cm}^3$) the measure of capacity is millilitres. A millilitre is the content of a cube with sides $1\text{ cm}$.

If the dimensions of an object are doubled, the volume increases by a factor of eight.

## 19.1 Surface area

### SURFACE AREA OF PRISMS

The **surface area** of an object is the total area of all of its faces added together. 
You learnt the following formula in previous grades:

* $\text{Surface area of a prism} = \text{Sum of the areas of all its faces}$

Alternatively, learners can calculate the sum of the areas of all the faces or use the formula:
$$\text{Surface area } (SA) = 2 \times \text{area of the base} + \text{perimeter} \times \text{the height of the prism}$$

Learners should keep the following in mind:
* In the case of a cube, it is easy to simply multiply the area of a face by six.
* The formula to calculate the area of a triangle is $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}bh$.
* The hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle is calculated using the Theorem of Pythagoras.

### Answers and Worked Examples

1. **Cube** ($10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$)
   $$\begin{aligned}
   SA &= 6 \times \text{area of face} \\
   &= 6 \times 10^2\text{ cm}^2 \\
   &= 600\text{ cm}^2
   \end{aligned}$$

2. **Rectangular Prism** ($12\text{ cm} \times 8\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm}$)
   $$\begin{aligned}
   SA &= \text{Sum of area of all faces} \\
   &= 2(12 \times 8 + 8 \times 5 + 5 \times 12)\text{ cm}^2 \\
   &= 2(96 + 40 + 60) = 392\text{ cm}^2
   \end{aligned}$$

3. **Rectangular Prism** ($25\text{ mm} \times 25\text{ mm} \times 100\text{ mm}$)
   $$\begin{aligned}
   SA &= 2 \times 25^2 + (4 \times 25 \times 100) \\
   &= 1\,250 + 10\,000 \\
   &= 11\,250\text{ mm}^2
   \end{aligned}$$

4. **Triangular Prism** ($60\text{ mm} \times 40\text{ mm} \times 50\text{ mm} \times 70\text{ mm}$)
   $$\begin{aligned}
   SA &= 2\left(\frac{1}{2} \times 60 \times 40\right) + 2(50 \times 70) + (60 \times 70) \\
   &= 2\,400 + 7\,000 + 4\,200 \\
   &= 13\,600\text{ mm}^2
   \end{aligned}$$

5. **Square-based Pyramid** ($10\text{ m} \times 10\text{ m}$ base, $3\text{ m}$ height)
   $$\begin{aligned}
   x &= \sqrt{10^2 + 10^2} = \sqrt{200} = 14,14... \\
   \text{Area bases} &= 2 \times \frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 10 = 100\text{ m}^2 \\
   \text{Area lateral faces} &= 3 \times (14,14... + 10 + 10) \\
   &= 102,43\text{ m}^2 \\
   \text{Total } SA &= 100 + 102,43 = 202,43\text{ m}^2
   \end{aligned}$$

6. **Triangular Pyramid** ($2\text{ m} \times 1,5\text{ m} \times 4\text{ m}$)
   $$\begin{aligned}
   x &= \sqrt{2^2 + (1,5)^2} = \sqrt{6,25} = 2,5\text{ m} \\
   \text{Area bases} &= 2 \times \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 1,5 = 3\text{ m}^2 \\
   \text{Area lateral faces} &= 4 \times (1,5 + 2 + 2,5) \\
   &= 24\text{ m}^2 \\
   \text{Total } SA &= 3 + 24 = 27\text{ m}^2
   \end{aligned}$$

### 2. Investigating the Surface Area of Cylinders

#### Teaching Guidelines & Concepts
1. **Visualizing the net of a cylinder:**
   * A cylinder consists of two circular bases (top and bottom) and a curved lateral surface.
   * If you unroll the curved surface of a cylinder, it forms a rectangle.
   * The length ($l$) of this unrolled rectangle equals the circumference of the circular base ($2\pi r$), and its breadth ($b$) equals the height of the cylinder ($h$).

2. **Developing the Formula:**
   * The general surface area formula for a prism can be adapted for a cylinder by replacing the perimeter of the prism with the circumference of the cylinder's base.
   
   $$\text{Surface Area} = 2 \times (\text{area of the base}) + (\text{circumference} \times \text{height})$$
   
   $$\text{Surface Area} = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$$

3. **Factorized Form:**
   * Taking out the common factor $2\pi r$:
   
   $$\text{Surface Area} = 2\pi r(r + h)$$

* **Required Information:** To calculate the surface area of a cylinder, you only need two measurements:
  1. The **radius ($r$)** of the circular base.
  2. The **height ($h$)** of the cylinder.

# Calculating the Surface Area of Cylinders

## Core Concepts and Formulas

The surface area of a cylinder is the total area of all its surfaces, which includes the top base, the bottom base, and the curved surface area:

$$\text{Surface Area} = \text{Area of top} + \text{Area of base} + \text{Area of curved surface}$$

$$\text{Surface Area} = \pi r^2 + \pi r^2 + (l \times b)$$

$$\text{Surface Area} = 2\pi r^2 + (2\pi r \times h)$$

$$\text{Surface Area} = 2\pi r(r + h)$$

*Note: The length of the rectangle that wraps around the cylinder is equal to the circumference of the top or base ($2\pi r$).*

To work out the surface area of a cylinder, you need to know:
1. The radius ($r$)
2. The height ($h$)

If you are given the circumference instead of the radius, you can find $r$ by solving:
$$r = \text{circumference} \div 2\pi$$

*(Use either $3,14$ or $\frac{22}{7}$ for $\pi$)*

---

## Worked Examples and Activities

### Question 1
Calculate the surface area of the following objects. Use $\pi = 3,14$ and round off all answers to two decimal places.

* **A.** A cylinder with radius $r = 6\text{ cm}$ and height $h = 6\text{ cm}$.
  $$\text{SA} = 2\pi r(r + h)$$
  $$\text{SA} = 2 \times 3,14 \times 6 \times (6 + 6)$$
  $$\text{SA} = 452,16\text{ cm}^2$$

* **B.** A cylinder with radius $r = 4\text{ m}$ and height $h = 8\text{ m}$.
  $$\text{SA} = 2\pi r(r + h)$$
  $$\text{SA} = 2(3,14) \times 4 \times (4 + 8)$$
  $$\text{SA} = 301,44\text{ m}^2$$

### Question 2
Calculate the surface area of a cylinder if its height is $60\text{ cm}$ and the circumference of its base is $25,12\text{ cm}$.

* Find the radius first:
  $$r = 25,12 \div (2\pi) = 4\text{ cm}$$
* Calculate the surface area:
  $$\text{SA} = 2\pi r(r + h) = 25,12(4 + 60) = 1\,607,68\text{ cm}^2$$

### Question 3
Calculate the surface area of a cylinder if its height is $5\text{ m}$ and the circumference of its base is $12,56\text{ m}$.

* Find the radius first:
  $$r = 12,56 \div 2\pi = 12,56 \div (2 \times 3,14) = 2\text{ m}$$
* Calculate the surface area:
  $$\text{SA} = 2\pi r(r + h) = 12,56(2 + 5) = 87,92\text{ m}^2$$

### Question 4
The outside of a cylindrical structure at a factory must be painted. Its radius is $3,5\text{ m}$ and its height is $8\text{ m}$. How many litres of paint must be bought if $1\text{ litre}$ covers $10\text{ m}^2$? *(Note: The bottom of the structure will not be painted).*

* Since the bottom is not painted, modify the formula (only one circular base is included):
  $$\text{SA} = 2\pi r \times h + \pi r^2$$
  $$\text{SA} = 2 \times 3,14 \times 3,5 \times 8 + 3,14 \times (3,5)^2$$
  $$\text{SA} = 214,305\text{ m}^2$$
* Calculate paint required:
  $$214,305 \div 10 = 21,43\text{ litres}$$
* **Conclusion:** $21$ litres will not be enough. $22$ litres of paint will allow for enough paint including any wastage or spills.

# 19.2 Volume

## Formulae for Volume of Prisms

The **volume** of an object is the amount of space it occupies. We usually measure volume in cubic units, such as $\text{mm}^3$, $\text{cm}^3$, and $\text{m}^3$.

### Converting Between Cubic Units
* $1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\,000\text{ mm}^3$
* $1\text{ m}^3 = 1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3$

### General Formula
$$\text{Volume of a prism} = \text{Area of base} \times \text{height}$$

How the area of the base is calculated depends on the shape of the base:
* **Square base:** $A = s^2$
* **Rectangle base:** $A = lb$
* **Triangle base:** $A = \frac{1}{2}bh$ *(Note: In the case of a triangular prism, do not confuse the height of the base triangle ($h_b$) with the height of the prism ($h_p$).)*

In the same way, the area of any polygonal base must be calculated first and then multiplied by the height of the prism. Volume is measured in cubic units, for example, centimetres cubed ($\text{cm}^3$) and cubic metres ($\text{m}^3$).

# Calculating the Volume of Prisms

## Core Concepts & Formulas

The volume ($V$) of any standard prism is calculated by finding the area of its base and multiplying it by its height or length.

| Prism Type | Formula | Diagram Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rectangular Prism** | $$V = (l \times b) \times h$$ | Length ($l$), breadth ($b$), and height ($h$) |
| **Cube** | $$V = s \times s \times s = s^{3}$$ | Side length ($s$) |
| **Triangular Prism** | $$V = \left(\frac{1}{2} \text{ base} \times h_b\right) \times h_p$$ | Base of triangle, height of triangle ($h_b$), and height of prism ($h_p$) |

---

## Worked Examples & Teaching Guidelines

### Example 1: Solving for Edge Length from Volume
* **Problem:** The volume of a cube is $216\text{ m}^3$. What is the length of one of its edges?
* **Solution:** 
  Using the formula for the volume of a cube, $V = s^{3}$:
  $$216 = s^{3}$$
  To find the edge length ($s$), take the cube root of the volume:
  $$s = \sqrt[3]{216} = 6\text{ m}$$

### Example 2: Composite Prisms
* When working with complex prisms, you can split the shape into two or more simpler parts, calculate their individual volumes, and add them together. 
* Alternatively, treat the cross-section as a combined figure (e.g., a square combined with an isosceles triangle). 
* *Note:* For triangular bases, remember to use the Theorem of Pythagoras ($a^2 + b^2 = c^2$) if any side lengths of a right-angled triangle are missing (such as recognizing standard Pythagorean triples like $3, 4, 5$).

## 19.3 Capacity

### Teaching Guidelines

- **Volume** is the amount of space that an object takes up.
- **Capacity** is the amount of content that a container can hold if it is filled to the brim.
- An object can have both volume and capacity.
- Volume is measured in cubic centimetres ($\text{cm}^3$) and capacity is measured in millilitres. A millilitre is the content of a cube with sides that are $1\text{ cm}$ each. So:
  $$1\text{ ml} = 1\text{ cm}^3 \quad \text{and} \quad 1\,000\text{ ml} = 1\text{ }\ell$$

#### The following conversions apply:
$$1\text{ m}^3 = 100 \times 100 \times 100\text{ cm}^3$$
$$= 1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3$$
$$= 1\,000\,000\text{ ml}$$
$$= 1\,000\text{ }\ell$$
$$= 1\text{ kilolitre (kl)}$$

$$1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm}$$

$$1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ ml}$$
$$1\,000\text{ ml} = 1\text{ }\ell$$
$$1\,000\text{ }\ell = 1\text{ kl}$$

---

Remember that the **capacity** of an object is the amount of space *inside* the object. You can think of the capacity of an object as the amount of liquid that the object can hold.

> **Note:** The **volume** of an object is the amount of space that the object itself takes up.

The volume of a solid block of wood is $10\text{ cm} \times 6\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} = 240\text{ cm}^3$.

The same block of wood is carved out to make a hollow container with inside measurements of $8\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm}$. (Its walls are $1\text{ cm}$ thick.) The amount of space inside the container must be calculated using the *inside* measurements. So, the capacity of the container is $8\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm} = 96\text{ cm}^3$.

# 19.4 Doubling dimensions and the effect on volume

## DOUBLING THE DIMENSIONS OF A PRISM

### Teaching Guidelines

You can show learners how doubling dimensions influences the volume of a prism.

# Doubling the Dimensions of a Cylinder

## Teaching Guidelines

The standard formula for the volume of a cylinder with radius $r$ and height $h$ is:
$$V = \pi r^2 h$$

When scaling the dimensions of a cylinder, the volume changes by a factor dependent on which dimensions are scaled and how many times they are scaled.

1. **Doubling the height ($2h$):**
   $$V_1 = \pi r^2 \times 2h = 2\pi r^2 h = 2V$$
   *Conclusion:* The volume doubles ($2$ times).

2. **Doubling the radius ($2r$):**
   $$V_2 = \pi (2r)^2 \times h = \pi \cdot 4r^2 \cdot h = 4\pi r^2 h = 4V$$
   *Conclusion:* The volume increases by a factor of $4$ ($4$ times).

3. **Doubling both the height ($2h$) and the radius ($2r$):**
   $$V_3 = \pi (2r)^2 \times 2h = \pi \cdot 4r^2 \cdot 2h = 8\pi r^2 h = 8V$$
   *Conclusion:* The volume increases by a factor of $8$ ($8$ times).

---

## Common Misconceptions
* Learners often incorrectly assume that doubling *all* dimensions of a three-dimensional object simply doubles its volume. Because the radius is squared in the volume formula ($r^2$), doubling the radius quadruples the base area, thereby multiplying the total volume impact significantly.

## Lesson: Investigating Changes in the Volume of 3D Objects

### Core Concepts

When the dimensions of a three-dimensional geometric object are scaled, its volume changes in predictable ways based on the formulas governing its volume. 

1. **Rectangular Prism:**
   * **Volume Formula:** $V = l \times b \times h$
   * Where $l$ is length, $b$ is breadth, and $h$ is height.
   * If only the height ($h$) is doubled, the new volume becomes $V_{\text{new}} = l \times b \times (2h) = 2 \times (l \times b \times h) = 2V$. Thus, doubling the height **doubles** the volume.

2. **Cylinder:**
   * **Volume Formula:** $V = \pi r^2 h$
   * Where $r$ is the radius and $h$ is the height.
   * If only the radius ($r$) is doubled, the new volume becomes $V_{\text{new}} = \pi (2r)^2 h = \pi (4r^2) h = 4 \times (\pi r^2 h) = 4V$. Thus, doubling the radius **quadruples** (multiplies by 4) the volume.
   * If both the height and the radius are doubled, the new volume becomes $V_{\text{new}} = \pi (2r)^2 (2h) = \pi (4r^2)(2h) = 8 \times (\pi r^2 h) = 8V$. Thus, doubling both the height and the radius multiplies the volume by **eight**.
