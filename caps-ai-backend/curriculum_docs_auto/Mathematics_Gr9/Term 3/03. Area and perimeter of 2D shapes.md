## 14.3 Area and perimeter of circles

### Revising concepts from previous grades

The perimeter of a circle is called the **circumference** of a circle. You will remember the following about circles from previous grades:

* The distance across the circle through its centre is called the **diameter** ($d$) of the circle.
* The distance from the centre of the circle to any point on the circumference is called the **radius** ($r$).
* The circumference ($c$) of a circle divided by its diameter is equal to the irrational value we call **pi** ($\pi$). To simplify calculations, we often use the approximate values: $\pi \approx 3,14$ or $\pi \approx \frac{22}{7}$.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0003-00.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0003-00.png)

It illustrates the concept of area decomposition, showing how a complex shape can be partitioned into two simpler rectangles (yellow and red) to facilitate the calculation of total area.



The following are important formulae to remember:

* $d = 2r$ and $r = \frac{1}{2}d$
* Circumference of a circle ($c$) = $2\pi r$
* Area of a circle ($A$) = $\pi r^2$

## 14.5 Area of other quadrilaterals

### Parallelograms

As shown below, a parallelogram can be made into a rectangle if a right-angled triangle from one side is cut off and moved to its other side.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0005-03.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0005-03.png)

> This geometry figure displays two circles, one nested within the other, with radii labeled as $r = 12\text{ cm}$ for the larger circle and $r = 7\text{ cm}$ for the smaller circle. It is designed to help students practice calculating the area of composite shapes by subtracting the area of the inner circle from the area of the outer circle to find the shaded region.



So we can find the area of a parallelogram using the formula for the area of a rectangle:

$$\text{Area of rectangle} = l \times b$$
$$= (\text{base of parallelogram}) \times (\text{perpendicular height of parallelogram})$$

$$\text{Area of parallelogram} = \text{Area of rectangle}$$

$$\therefore \text{Area of parallelogram} = \text{base} \times \text{perp. height}$$

> **Note:** We can use any side of the parallelogram as the base, but we must use the perpendicular height on the side we have chosen.

## Kites

To calculate the area of a kite, you use one of its properties, namely that the diagonals of a kite are perpendicular.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0007-00.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0007-00.png)
> **[Diagram Context - Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0007-00.png]**
> This graphic displays four parallelograms labeled A through D, each featuring specific side lengths and perpendicular heights indicated by dashed lines and right-angle symbols. These figures illustrate the geometric components required to calculate the area of a parallelogram, reinforcing the CAPS curriculum requirement to identify and apply the correct base and corresponding perpendicular height in area calculations.



$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Area of kite DEFG} &= \text{Area of }\Delta\text{DEG} + \text{Area of }\Delta\text{EFG} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(b \times h) + \frac{1}{2}(b \times h) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(EG \times OD) + \frac{1}{2}(EG \times OF) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}EG(OD + OF) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}EG \times DF
\end{aligned}$$

Notice that $EG$ and $DF$ are the diagonals of the kite.

$$\therefore \text{Area of a kite} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{diagonal } 1 \times \text{diagonal } 2)$$

## Mathematical Background

### Perimeter of 2D Shapes

The following formulae are used to calculate the **perimeter of 2D shapes**:

| Shape | Formula |
| :--- | :--- |
| square | $P = 4s$ |
| rectangle | $P = 2(l + b)$ or $P = 2l + 2b$ |
| circumference of a circle | $c = 2\pi r$ or $\pi d$ |

### Area of 2D Shapes

The following formulae are used to calculate the **area of 2D shapes**:

| Shape | Formula |
| :--- | :--- |
| square | $A = l^2$ |
| rectangle | $A = l \times b$ |
| triangle | $A = \frac{1}{2}(b \times h)$ |
| circle | $A = \pi r^2$ |

The areas of more complex polygons can be worked out by breaking them up into known shapes such as squares, rectangles and triangles. The following formulae are used to calculate the **area of rhombi, kites, parallelograms and trapeziums**:

| Shape | Formula |
| :--- | :--- |
| rhombus | $A = l \times h$ |
| kite | $A = \frac{1}{2}(\text{diagonal}_1 \times \text{diagonal}_2)$ |
| parallelogram | $A = \text{base} \times h$ |
| trapezium | $A = \frac{1}{2}(\text{sum of parallel sides}) \times h$ |

### Important Note on Scaling Dimensions

If the dimensions of a shape are doubled, the perimeter of the new shape is doubled and the area is four times larger than the original shape.

# 14.3 Area and perimeter of circles

## REVISING CONCEPTS FROM PREVIOUS GRADES

The perimeter of a circle is called the **circumference** of a circle. You will remember the following about circles from previous grades:

* The distance across the circle through its centre is called the **diameter** ($d$) of the circle.
* The distance from the centre of the circle to any point on the circumference is called the **radius** ($r$).
* The circumference ($c$) of a circle divided by its diameter is equal to the irrational value we call **pi** ($\pi$). To simplify calculations, we often use the approximate values: $\pi \approx 3,14$ or $\frac{22}{7}$.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0016-03.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr9_Area-and-perimeter-of-2D-shapes.pdf-0016-03.png)

## 14.4 Converting Between Units

### Converting Between Units Used for Perimeter and Area

#### Teaching Guidelines
* To convert from a larger unit to a smaller unit for perimeter, **multiply** by the conversion factor.
* To convert from a smaller unit to a larger unit, **divide** by the conversion factor.
  * *Example:* To change centimetres (larger unit) to millimetres (smaller unit), multiply by $10$.
* **Pay particular attention to the conversion of square units.**

#### Common Misconceptions
Learners often become confused about the relative size of units and might divide centimetres by $10$ to get millimetres. Encourage learners to ask the question: *"If I change this unit, for example, from centimetres to metres, will the number be more or less than it is?"* In this case, the answer is less, so learners should divide centimetres by $100$ to get metres.

---

### Key Conversion Reference Table

| Unit Conversion (Length) | Unit Conversion (Area) |
| :--- | :--- |
| $1\text{ cm} = 10\text{ mm}$ | $1\text{ cm}^2 = 1\text{ cm} \times 1\text{ cm} = 0,01\text{ m} \times 0,01\text{ m} = 0,0001\text{ m}^2$ |
| $1\text{ mm} = 0,1\text{ cm}$ | $1\text{ cm}^2 = 0,0001\text{ m}^2$ |
| $1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm}$ | $1\text{ m}^2 = 10 000\text{ cm}^2$ |
| $1\text{ cm} = 0,01\text{ m}$ | |
| $1\text{ km} = 1 000\text{ m}$ | |
| $1\text{ m} = 0,001\text{ km}$ | |

---

### Worked Examples

#### Example 1: Converting Units of Length
* **(a)** $34\text{ cm} = 340\text{ mm}$ (since $34 \times 10 = 340$)
* **(b)** $501\text{ m} = 0,501\text{ km}$ (since $501 \div 1 000 = 0,501$)
* **(c)** $226\text{ m} = 22\,600\text{ cm}$ (since $226 \times 100 = 22\,600$)

* **(e)** $1,9\text{ cm} = 19\text{ mm}$ (since $1,9 \times 10 = 19$)
* **(f)** $73\text{ mm} = 7,3\text{ cm}$ (since $73 \div 10 = 7,3$)
* **(g)** $924\text{ mm} = 0,924\text{ m}$ (since $924 \div 1 000 = 0,924$)
* **(h)** $32,23\text{ km} = 32\,230\text{ m}$ (since $32,23 \times 1 000 = 32\,230$)

1\text{ cm}^2 &= 0,0001\text{ m}^2 \\
\therefore 50\text{ cm}^2 &= 50 \times 0,0001\text{ m}^2 \\
&= 0,005\text{ m}^2
\end{aligned}$$

## 14.5 Area of Other Quadrilaterals

### Parallelograms

#### Core Concepts and Derivation
* **Properties:** The opposite sides of a parallelogram are parallel and equal. An altitude (or height) of a parallelogram is a line drawn perpendicularly between two opposite, parallel sides.
* **Geometric Transformation:** A parallelogram can be transformed into a rectangle by cutting a right-angled triangle from one side and moving it to the opposite side. 
* **Formula Derivation:**
  * The area of a rectangle is given by:
    $$\text{Area} = l \times b$$
  * Since the base and perpendicular height of the resulting rectangle are equal to the base and perpendicular height of the original parallelogram:
    $$\text{Area of parallelogram} = \text{base} \times \text{perpendicular height}$$
    $$A = b \times h$$

# Kites and Trapeziums: Revision Notes

---

## Kites

### Core Concepts & Properties
* The diagonals of a kite bisect each other at right angles ($90^\circ$).
* If a rectangle is drawn around a kite, the diagonals of the kite divide the rectangle into four smaller rectangles. Each smaller rectangle has a diagonal which is a side of the kite, meaning the area of the kite is exactly **half** the area of the enclosing rectangle.

### Area Formula for a Kite
$$\text{Area of a kite} = \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{diagonal}_1 \times \text{diagonal}_2)$$

In words: **The area equals half of the product of the diagonals.**

---

### Derivation of the Area Formula
Considering a kite $DEFG$ with diagonals $EG$ and $DF$ intersecting at $O$:
$$\text{Area of kite } DEFG = \text{Area of } \triangle DEG + \text{Area of } \triangle EFG$$

$$\text{Area} = \left(\frac{1}{2} \times b \times h\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} \times b \times h\right)$$

$$\text{Area} = \left(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{EG} \times \text{OD}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{EG} \times \text{OF}\right)$$

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\text{EG}(\text{OD} + \text{OF})$$

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\text{EG} \times \text{DF}$$

Since $EG$ and $DF$ are the diagonals:
$$\therefore \text{Area of a kite} = \frac{1}{2} (\text{diagonal}_1 \times \text{diagonal}_2)$$

---

### Worked Examples (Kites)

#### Example 1: Calculate the area of kites with given diagonals (Answers in $\text{m}^2$)
**(a) Diagonals of $150\text{ mm}$ and $200\text{ mm}$**
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{diagonal}_1 \times \text{diagonal}_2)$$
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(150 \times 200)$$
$$\text{Area} = 15\,000\text{ mm}^2 = 0{,}015\text{ m}^2$$

**(b) Diagonals of $25\text{ cm}$ and $40\text{ cm}$**
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{diagonal}_1 \times \text{diagonal}_2)$$
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(25 \times 40)$$
$$\text{Area} = 500\text{ cm}^2 = 0{,}05\text{ m}^2$$

---

#### Example 2: Complex Kite Area Calculation
**Given:**
* $\text{BO} = \text{OD} = 6\text{ cm}$
* $\text{OC} = 15\text{ cm}$
* $\text{AD} = 10\text{ cm}$

**Step 1: Find length $\text{OA}$ using Pythagoras' theorem in $\triangle AOD$**
$$10^2 = 6^2 + \text{OA}^2 \quad \text{[Pythagoras]}$$
$$\text{OA}^2 = 100 - 36$$
$$\text{OA}^2 = 64$$
$$\text{OA} = 8\text{ cm}$$

**Step 2: Calculate the total area of the kite**
$$\text{Area of kite} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{diagonal}_1 \times \text{diagonal}_2)$$

## Trapeziums

### Core Concepts & Properties
* A trapezium is a quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides.
* If you tessellate (tile) two congruent trapeziums together, they form a parallelogram where the base of the parallelogram is equal to the sum of the parallel sides of the trapezium.

---

### Derivation of the Area Formula
We can divide trapezium $ABCD$ into two triangles sharing the same perpendicular height $h$ (denoted as $AE$), namely $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ADC$:

$$\text{Area of trapezium } ABCD = \text{Area } \triangle ABC + \text{Area } \triangle ADC$$

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{AB} \times \text{AE} + \frac{1}{2} \times \text{DC} \times \text{AE}$$

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\text{AE}(\text{AB} + \text{DC})$$

In words: **The area equals half of the sum of the parallel sides multiplied by the height.**

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} (\text{sum of parallel sides}) \times \text{height}$$

*(Note: Always remember that the area of a rectangle is given by $\text{length} \times \text{breadth}$.)*

# 14.6 Doubling dimensions of a 2D shape

## Teaching Guidelines

You can show learners how doubling dimensions influences the perimeter and the area. For example:

* A rectangle: length $l$ and breadth $b$; area $= lb$. Now double the dimensions to $2l$ and $2b$, then the area becomes $A = 2l \times 2b = 4lb$ which is four times the original area.
* A kite: let the diagonals be $a$ and $b$. The area $= \frac{1}{2}ab$. Double the dimensions, then the area becomes $A = \frac{1}{2} \times 2a \times 2b = \frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times ab = 4 \times \frac{1}{2}ab$; four times the original area.

# Lesson Notes: Effects of Scaling Dimensions on Perimeter and Area

## Core Concept
When the dimensions of a two-dimensional geometric shape are scaled (multiplied by a scale factor $k$), the perimeter and the area change in predictable ways:
* **Perimeter ($P$):** Scales linearly by the scale factor $k$. If dimensions are doubled ($k = 2$), the perimeter is multiplied by $2$.
* **Area ($A$):** Scales quadratically by the square of the scale factor ($k^2$). If dimensions are doubled ($k = 2$), the area is multiplied by $2^2 = 4$.
