## 4.1 Angles revision

When two lines point in different directions, we say they are **at an angle** to each other. If the directions are almost the same, we say the **angle** between them is small. If the directions are very different, we say the angle between them is big.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Words we use to describe angles:

* **Arms of the angle:** the two lines that are at an angle to each other
* **The vertex:** the point where the two arms meet
* **Vertices:** plural of "vertex"

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Symbols to describe angles:

Arrowheads on the lines mean that the lines keep on going. The length of an angle's arms does not change the size of the angle. Whether the arms are long or short, the angle size stays the same.

There are **two angles at a vertex** so it is important to show which one we are talking about.

### Naming Angles
You can name an angle in different ways:
* $\hat{A}\hat{B}C$
* $C\hat{B}A$
* Just $\hat{B}$

The "hat" symbol ($\,\hat{ }\,\text{}$) on the letter shows where the vertex of the angle is.

## 4.2 The degree: a unit for measuring angles

Imagine if we didn’t have units for measuring length. How would tailors make clothes to the right size without a tape measure? How could an architect design a safe and beautiful house without a ruler? How could we lay out a professional soccer field without being able to measure accurately in metres? 

We need units and measuring instruments in many situations. You know that we use metres, centimetres, kilometres, millimetres, etc. for measuring lengths. 

We should also have units for measuring angles. The units we use for measuring angles are very ancient. No one today is completely sure why, but our ancestors decided many thousands of years ago that a revolution should be divided into $360$ equal parts. We call these parts degrees. The symbol for a degree is $^\circ$.

Circles are special for many reasons. The most important reason is the following:

**The distance from the centre of a circle to the edge is the same in any direction.**

This distance is called the **radius**.
* We pronounce this: "ray-dee-us".
* The plural of **radius** is **radii**.
* We pronounce this: "ray-dee-eye".

* If your circles are turning into spirals it is because the arms of your compass have moved. Check their width again against a ruler.

## Mathematical Background

* An **angle** is formed when two lines, the sides of the angle, meet at a common point, the **vertex**.
* An angle measures the amount of turning about a point in degrees. For example, starting on BA and turning A about B to end at C, forms $\hat{\text{ABC}}$.
* The **label** of the vertex is always written in the middle, for example $\angle\text{ABC}$ or angle $\angle\text{CBA}$ means that B is the vertex. Another way of writing $\angle\text{ABC}$ is $\hat{\text{ABC}}$ or simply $\hat{\text{B}}$. When there is more than one angle at a vertex, the two endpoints will have to be included, for example $\text{A}\hat{\text{B}}\text{C}$ or $\text{C}\hat{\text{B}}\text{D}$, to avoid confusion.
* Angles could have their sides slanted and learners should also be shown angles with sides that are not horizontal and/or vertical.
* In order to measure the size of an angle, the sides sometimes have to be lengthened, this is also called **producing the lines**.
* Parallel lines and perpendicular lines could also be slanted and learners should work with such lines oriented in different ways.

## 4.2 The Degree: A Unit for Measuring Angles

### Core Concepts

Just as we need standard units to measure length (such as metres, centimetres, or millimetres) or area (such as square units), we need standard units to measure angles. 

* **The Degree ($^\circ$):** Historically, a full revolution (a complete circle) was divided into $360$ equal parts. Each of these parts is called a **degree**, denoted by the symbol $^\circ$. 
* Therefore, a complete revolution measures $360^\circ$.
* Angles can be thought of as fractions or wedges of a full revolution. For example:
  * A quarter turn is $\frac{1}{4}$ of $360^\circ$, which equals $90^\circ$.

## Revision Notes: Geometry of Angles

### Key Concepts

* **Straight Angle:** A straight line represents an angle of $180^\circ$. Angles on a straight line add up to $180^\circ$.
* **Revolution:** A full circle or complete turn around a point represents an angle of $360^\circ$. Angles around a point add up to $360^\circ$.
* **Reflex Angle:** An angle greater than $180^\circ$ and less than $360^\circ$. Because standard protractors only measure up to $180^\circ$, measuring a reflex angle requires a specific strategy:
  1. Measure the angle on the other side and subtract its size from $360^\circ$.
  2. Alternatively, extend one of the arms to split the angle into two parts (one part being a straight angle of $180^\circ$), measure the remaining smaller part, and add it to $180^\circ$.

# 4.7 Using the Compass

## Concepts & Structure

### Why We Use a Compass
Drawing circles using a string and pencils has drawbacks because string is stretchy, making it difficult to keep the radius constant. This can cause the drawing pencil to wander off course, resulting in a spiral or wobbly curve rather than a true circle. 

To draw accurate circles, we need a special instrument called a **pair of compasses**, or simply a **compass**.

### Key Definitions & Terminology
* **Radius:** The distance from the centre of a circle to any point on the circle.
  * Pronunciation: *"ray-dee-us"*
* **Radii:** The plural form of radius.
  * Pronunciation: *"ray-dee-eye"*

# 4.8 Using Circles to Draw Other Figures

## Background
Engineers, architects, designers, sign writers, production designers, and artists often use compasses and circles. Many logos and well-known symbols incorporate circles or parts of circles, such as the yin-yang logo or the Mercedes-Benz logo.

---

## Geometric Figures Hiding in the Circles

### Activity Instructions
1. Look at the set of seven intersecting circles (such as the flower-of-life pattern shown in the diagram).
2. Find hidden polygons by joining the points where the circles intersect each other.
3. The intersection points serve as the vertices of the polygons. 
4. Carefully rule lines with a pencil to discover various shapes:
   - Triangles
   - Quadrilaterals
   - Pentagons
   - Hexagons
5. Once identified, you can measure the interior angles at each vertex and the lengths of the sides to analyze the geometric properties of the constructed figures.

---

## Arcs of Circles
It is not always necessary to draw complete circles to construct geometric figures. Often, constructors are only interested in the specific intersection points where circles cross each other. Consequently, drawing only the necessary **arcs** at those crossing points is sufficient for clean geometric constructions.

---

## Arcs of circles

### Notes

* **Circumference:** The distance around a circle, a sphere, a cylinder, or any other curved shape.
* **Perimeter:** The distance around a shape that has vertices, such as a rectangle. 
* **Note on Perimeter and Circumference:** Both terms refer to the same type of measurement: the distance around the shape or around the base of an object.
* **Arc:** Part of the length of the circumference of a circle. It could be a small part or it could be all the way around, the full circle.
* Radii of the same circle are all equal; therefore, the straight distance between any point on the circle and the centre of the circle is the radius of the circle. Starting from any point on the circle and marking off six equal arcs, we should get the sides of a regular hexagon.
* In practice, learners sometimes find that their last arc does not go through the original point on the circumference. If this happens, help learners to find why and to improve the effort.

## 4.9 Parallel and perpendicular lines with circles

### PARALLEL AND PERPENDICULAR

#### Notes and Teaching Guidelines
* **Parallel lines** are always the same distance apart and will never meet.
* **Perpendicular lines** meet at right angles ($90^\circ$).
* **Intersection:** When two lines (or arcs) cross each other, we say they **intersect**. The **intersection point** is the exact place where they meet.

---

### Activities and Exercises

   *(Answer: are always the same distance apart and will never meet.)*

   *(Answer: meet at right angles.)*
