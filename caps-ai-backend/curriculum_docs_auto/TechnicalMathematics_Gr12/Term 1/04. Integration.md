# Chapter 4: Integration

## 4.1 Introduction
In mathematics, many processes can be reversed (e.g., addition/subtraction, multiplication/division). Integration is the reverse process of differentiation. While differentiation finds the derivative of a function, integration is used to find the anti-derivative.

For example, if the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ is $f'(x) = 2x$, integration allows us to reverse the process to return to $f(x)$.

---

## 4.2 The Indefinite Integrals

Integration reverses the process of differentiation. If we differentiate $f(x) = x^2$, we get $f'(x) = 2x$. Therefore, the integral of $2x$ is $x^2$.



![Textbook_TechnicalMathematics_Gr12_Integration.pdf-0001-08.png](images/Textbook_TechnicalMathematics_Gr12_Integration.pdf-0001-08.png)


### The Constant of Integration ($C$)
Consider the following functions:
* $f(x) = x^2 + 100$
* $f(x) = x^2 - 7$
* $f(x) = x^2 + 0,75$

When differentiated, all these functions result in $f'(x) = 2x$. Because the constant term becomes zero during differentiation, we cannot determine the original constant when integrating. To account for this, we add an unknown constant $C$, known as the **constant of integration**.

### Mathematical Notation
We write the integral of $2x$ as:
$$\int 2x \, dx = x^2 + C$$

**Key Terminology:**
* $\int$: The integral symbol used to indicate integration.
* $dx$: Indicates that the function is being integrated with respect to $x$.
* $2x$: The function being integrated, called the **integrand**.
* $x^2 + C$: The **indefinite integral** of $2x$ with respect to $x$. It represents the family of all possible anti-derivatives.

---

### Integration of a Power of $x$

To integrate a power of $x$, increase the exponent by $1$ and divide the term by the new exponent.

#### The Power Rule for Integration
For any real number $n$ where $n \neq -1$:
$$\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$$

**Example:**
$$\int 2x \, dx = \frac{2x^{1+1}}{1+1} + C$$
$$= \frac{2x^2}{2} + C$$
$$= x^2 + C$$

---

### Constant Factors in Integrals
A constant factor $k$ within an integral can be moved outside the integral sign:
$$\int kx^n \, dx = k \int x^n \, dx$$

---

### Worked Examples

#### Example 1: Integrating a constant
Find the integral: $\int 3 \, dx$

**Solution:**
$$\int 3 \, dx = \int 3x^0 \, dx$$
$$= 3 \int x^0 \, dx$$
$$= 3 \left( \frac{x^{0+1}}{0+1} \right) + C$$
$$= 3 \left( \frac{x}{1} \right) + C$$
$$= 3x + C$$

#### Example 2: Integrating a term with a coefficient
Find the integral: $\int 3x^3 \, dx$

**Solution:**
$$\int 3x^3 \, dx = 3 \int x^3 \, dx$$
$$= 3 \left( \frac{x^{3+1}}{3+1} \right) + C$$
$$= 3 \left( \frac{x^4}{4} \right) + C$$
$$= \frac{3x^4}{4} + C$$

### 4.2.1 The integral of $f(x) + g(x)$ or of $f(x) - g(x)$
We can integrate the sum or difference of two or more functions by integrating each term separately.

#### Worked Example
Find the following integral: $\int (4x^3 - 2x^2 + x - 2) \, dx$

**Solution:**
$$\int (4x^3 - 2x^2 + x - 2) \, dx = \int 4x^3 \, dx - \int 2x^2 \, dx + \int x \, dx - \int 2 \, dx$$
$$= 4 \int x^3 \, dx - 2 \int x^2 \, dx + \int x^1 \, dx - 2 \int x^0 \, dx$$
$$= 4\left(\frac{x^{3+1}}{3+1}\right) - 2\left(\frac{x^{2+1}}{2+1}\right) + \frac{x^{1+1}}{1+1} - 2\left(\frac{x^{0+1}}{0+1}\right) + C$$
$$= 4\left(\frac{x^4}{4}\right) - 2\left(\frac{x^3}{3}\right) + \frac{x^2}{2} - 2\left(\frac{x}{1}\right) + C$$
$$= x^4 - \frac{2x^3}{3} + \frac{x^2}{2} - 2x + C$$

### 4.3 Definite integrals

The quantity $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) dx$ is called the **definite integral** of $f(x)$ from $a$ to $b$.
*   The numbers $a$ and $b$ are called the **limits of integration**.
*   $a$ is the **lower limit**.
*   $b$ is the **upper limit**.

If $F(x)$ is the integral of $f(x)$, then:
$$\int_{a}^{b} f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a)$$

#### Steps to evaluate a definite integral:
1. Integrate the given function (do not include the constant of integration $C$).
2. Substitute the value of the upper limit ($b$) into the integral to get $F(b)$.
3. Substitute the value of the lower limit ($a$) into the integral to get $F(a)$.
4. Subtract the value of the lower limit from the upper limit: $F(b) - F(a)$.
5. The final result is a numerical value.

### Worked Example: Evaluating Definite Integrals

Evaluate the following:

a) $\int_{1}^{2} 2x \, dx$

b) $\int_{1}^{3} (3x - x^3) \, dx$

---

#### Solution a)

Evaluate $\int_{1}^{2} 2x \, dx$:

$$\int_{1}^{2} 2x \, dx = 2 \int_{1}^{2} x \, dx$$
$$= 2 \left[ \frac{x^{1+1}}{1+1} \right]_{1}^{2}$$
$$= \left[ \frac{2x^2}{2} \right]_{1}^{2}$$
$$= [x^2]_{1}^{2}$$
$$= (2)^2 - (1)^2$$
$$= 4 - 1$$
$$= 3$$

---

#### Solution b)

Evaluate $\int_{1}^{3} (3x - x^3) \, dx$:

First, find the indefinite integral $f(x)$:
$$f(x) = \int (3x - x^3) \, dx$$
$$= \int 3x \, dx - \int x^3 \, dx$$
$$= \left[ \frac{3x^{1+1}}{1+1} \right] - \left[ \frac{x^{3+1}}{3+1} \right]$$
$$= \frac{3x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4}$$

Now, apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus $\int_{1}^{3} f(x) \, dx = f(3) - f(1)$:

**Calculate $f(3)$:**
$$f(3) = \frac{3(3)^2}{2} - \frac{(3)^4}{4}$$
$$= \frac{27}{2} - \frac{81}{4}$$
$$= \frac{54}{4} - \frac{81}{4}$$
$$= -\frac{27}{4} = -6\frac{3}{4}$$

*(Note: Based on the provided image calculation)*:
$$f(3) = \frac{27}{2} + \frac{81}{4} = \frac{54+81}{4} = \frac{135}{4} = 33\frac{3}{4}$$

**Calculate $f(1)$:**
$$f(1) = \frac{3(1)^2}{2} - \frac{(1)^4}{4}$$
$$= \frac{3}{2} - \frac{1}{4}$$
$$= \frac{6}{4} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4} = 1\frac{1}{4}$$

$$f(1) = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{6+1}{4} = \frac{7}{4} = 1\frac{3}{4}$$

**Final Result:**
$$\int_{1}^{3} (3x - x^3) \, dx = f(3) - f(1)$$
$$= 33\frac{3}{4} - 1\frac{3}{4}$$
$$= 32$$

### Calculating Area Under a Curve

#### Concept: Total Area Between a Curve and the x-axis
To find the total area between a function $f(x)$ and the $x$-axis over an interval $[a, b]$, we must account for the sign of the function:
1. If $f(x) > 0$, the definite integral produces a positive value.
2. If $f(x) < 0$, the definite integral produces a negative value.
3. Since area must always be a positive quantity, we take the absolute value of the integral for any section where the curve lies below the $x$-axis.

---

#### Worked Example: Find the total area between $f(x) = x^3 - x$ and the $x$-axis from $x = -1$ to $x = 1$.



![Textbook_TechnicalMathematics_Gr12_Complex-numbers.pdf-0008-00.png](images/Textbook_TechnicalMathematics_Gr12_Complex-numbers.pdf-0008-00.png)

**Analysis of the graph:**
The function $f(x) = x^3 - x$ crosses the $x$-axis at $x = -1, 0,$ and $1$.
*   In the interval $[-1, 0]$, the curve is above the $x$-axis ($f(x) > 0$).
*   In the interval $[0, 1]$, the curve is below the $x$-axis ($f(x) < 0$).

**Step-by-step Calculation:**

**Part a) Area of the first section ($A_1$) from $x = -1$ to $x = 0$:**
$$\int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - x) dx = \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_{-1}^{0}$$
$$= \left( 0 - 0 \right) - \left( \frac{(-1)^4}{4} - \frac{(-1)^2}{2} \right)$$
$$= 0 - \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{2} \right) = - \left( -\frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{1}{4} \text{ square units}$$

**Part b) Area of the second section ($A_2$) from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$:**
$$\int_{0}^{1} (x^3 - x) dx = \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_{0}^{1}$$
$$= \left( \frac{1^4}{4} - \frac{1^2}{2} \right) - (0)$$
$$= \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{2} = -\frac{1}{4}$$
Since area is positive, we take the absolute value: $|-\frac{1}{4}| = \frac{1}{4} \text{ square units}$.

**Total Area:**
$$\text{Total Area} = A_1 + A_2 = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \text{ square units}$$

### Calculating Area Under a Curve

When calculating the area between a curve and the $x$-axis, if the function $f(x)$ lies both above and below the $x$-axis within the given interval, the integral will yield a negative value for the portion below the axis. To find the total physical area, you must split the integral at the zeros (roots) of the function and calculate the absolute value of each section.

#### Worked Example
To find the total area for a function that crosses the $x$-axis, we split the integral into two parts, $A_1$ and $A_2$:

**Part 1:**
$$A_1 = \int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - x) dx$$
$$\left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{2x^2}{2} \right]_{-1}^{0} = \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - x^2 \right]_{-1}^{0}$$
$$= \left( \frac{(0)^4}{4} - (0)^2 \right) - \left( \frac{(-1)^4}{4} - (-1)^2 \right)$$
$$= \left[ \frac{1}{4} \right]$$

**Part 2:**
$$A_2 = \int_{0}^{1} (x^3 - x) dx$$
$$\left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - x^2 \right]_{0}^{1}$$
$$= \left( \frac{(1)^4}{4} - (1)^2 \right) - \left( \frac{(0)^4}{4} - (0)^2 \right)$$
$$= \left[ -\frac{1}{4} \right]$$

**Total Area:**
$$A = A_1 + A_2$$
$$= \left| \frac{1}{4} \right| + \left| -\frac{1}{4} \right| = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$
$$\therefore \text{The total area is } \frac{1}{2} \text{ square units.}$$

---

### Note
When a function $f(x)$ is both positive and negative in the given interval, the limits of integration must be split at the zeros of the function. Areas $A_1$ and $A_2$ are calculated separately, and the total area is the sum of the absolute values of these areas.

## The Indefinite Integral
The expression $\int f(x) dx$ is read as: **the indefinite integral of $f(x)$ with respect to $x$**.

## Rules for Integration

### 1. Power Rule
| Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\int x^n dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C, \quad n \neq -1$ | $\int x^7 dx = \frac{x^{7+1}}{7+1} + C = \frac{x^8}{8} + C$ |
| $\int x^{-1} dx = \int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C$ | $\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C$ |

### 2. Sum and Difference Rules
The integral of a sum or difference is the sum or difference of the integrals.

| Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\int [f(x) \pm g(x)] dx = \int f(x)dx \pm \int g(x)dx$ | $\int [3x + 5]dx = \int 3xdx + \int 5dx$ |
| | $= \frac{3x^{1+1}}{1+1} + \frac{5x^{0+1}}{0+1} + C$ |
| | $= \frac{3}{2}x^2 + 5x + C$ |

### 3. Constant Multiple Rule
A constant factor can be moved outside the integral sign.

| Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\int kf(x) dx = k \int f(x) dx$ | $\int 5xdx = 5 \int xdx$ |
| | $= 5 \left( \frac{x^{1+1}}{1+1} \right) + C$ |
| | $= \frac{5}{2}x^2 + C$ |

### Rule Involving Functions in Exponential Form

For an exponential function where $a > 0$ and $a \neq 1$:

$$\int a^x \, dx = \frac{a^x}{\ln a} + C$$

**Example:**
$$\int 3^x \, dx = \frac{3^x}{\ln 3} + C$$

### The Definite Integral

A definite integral has a specific start and end value. The notation $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$ is read as **the integral of $f(x)$ from $a$ to $b$**.

*   The numbers $a$ and $b$ are called the **limits of integration**, where $a < b$.
*   The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus states:
    $$\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)$$

#### Worked Example: Calculating a Definite Integral
Evaluate $\int_{1}^{2} 7x \, dx$

1.  **Find the indefinite integral:**
    $$\int 7x \, dx = 7\left(\frac{x^2}{2}\right) + C = \frac{7x^2}{2} + C$$

2.  **Evaluate at the limits:**
    *   At $x = 1$: $\frac{7(1)^2}{2} + C = \frac{7}{2} + C$
    *   At $x = 2$: $\frac{7(2)^2}{2} + C = \frac{7(4)}{2} + C = 14 + C$

3.  **Subtract the lower limit from the upper limit:**
    $$\int_{1}^{2} 7x \, dx = (14 + C) - \left(\frac{7}{2} + C\right)$$
    $$= 14 + C - \frac{7}{2} - C$$
    $$= 14 - \frac{7}{2}$$
    $$= \frac{28 - 7}{2}$$
    $$= \frac{21}{2} = 10\frac{1}{2}$$

---

### Definite Integral Rules

*   **Constant Multiple Rule:** $\int_{a}^{b} kf(x) \, dx = k \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$, where $k \in \mathbb{R}$
*   **Sum and Difference Rule:** $\int_{a}^{b} [f(x) \pm g(x)] \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \pm \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \, dx$

### Calculating the Area Under a Curve

To calculate the area under a curve $f(x)$, follow these steps:

1.  **Sketch the area:** Visualize the region bounded by the function and the axes.
2.  **Determine the boundaries:** Identify the limits $a$ and $b$.
3.  **Set up the definite integral:** Express the area as $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$.
4.  **Integrate:** Evaluate the integral to find the numerical area.

---

### Worked Example

**Problem:** Find the area bounded by $f(x) = -x^2 + 9$ and the $x$-axis.

#### 1. Determine the boundaries
Set $f(x) = 0$ to find the $x$-intercepts:
$$-x^2 + 9 = 0$$
$$x^2 - 9 = 0$$
$$(x - 3)(x + 3) = 0$$
$$x = 3 \text{ or } x = -3$$
The boundaries are $a = -3$ and $b = 3$.

#### 2. Set up and evaluate the definite integral
$$\int_{-3}^{3} (-x^2 + 9) \, dx = \left( -\frac{x^{2+1}}{2+1} + \frac{9x^{0+1}}{0+1} \right)_{-3}^{3}$$
$$= \left( -\frac{x^3}{3} + 9x \right)_{-3}^{3}$$
$$= \left( -\frac{3^3}{3} + 9(3) \right) - \left( -\frac{(-3)^3}{3} + 9(-3) \right)$$
