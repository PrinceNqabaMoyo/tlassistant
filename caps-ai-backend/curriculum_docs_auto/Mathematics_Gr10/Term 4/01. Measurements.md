*   **Given:** Parallel sides $a = -3z - 2$ and $b = -z$; Height $= h$

# 13.2 Right Prisms and Cylinders

## Definition: Right Prism
A **right prism** is a geometric solid that has a polygon as its base and vertical faces perpendicular to the base. The base and top surface are the same shape and size. It is called a "right" prism because the angles between the base and faces are right angles.

*   **Triangular prism:** Has a triangle as its base.
*   **Rectangular prism:** Has a rectangle as its base.
*   **Cube:** A rectangular prism where all sides are of equal length.
*   **Cylinder:** Has a circle as its base.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

## Surface Area of Prisms and Cylinders

### Definition: Surface Area
**Surface area** is the total area of the exposed or outer surfaces of a prism.

### Understanding Surface Area via Nets
To calculate the surface area, imagine the prism as a cardboard box that can be unfolded. A solid unfolded in this way is called a **net**. By unfolding the prism, you can clearly see each of its individual faces. To find the total surface area, calculate the area of each face and add them all together.

#### Examples of Nets:
*   **Triangular Prism:** When unfolded, it consists of two triangular faces and three rectangular faces. The total surface area is the sum of the areas of these five shapes.
*   **Cylinder:** When unfolded, the top and bottom faces are circles. The curved surface flattens into a rectangle where the length of the rectangle is equal to the circumference of the circular base. The total surface area is the sum of the areas of the two circles and the area of the rectangle.

### Worked Example: Finding the Surface Area of a Rectangular Prism

**Question**
Find the surface area of a rectangular prism with dimensions $5\text{ cm}$ (length), $2\text{ cm}$ (height), and $10\text{ cm}$ (depth).

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

### Solution

#### Step 1: Sketch and label the net of the prism
To find the surface area, we unfold the 3D object into a 2D net.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

#### Step 2: Find the areas of the different shapes in the net
The surface area is the sum of the areas of all the faces. We can group these into the "large rectangle" (the four sides wrapped around) and the two "small rectangles" (the ends).

**1. Area of the large rectangle (lateral surface area):**
The length of this rectangle is equal to the perimeter of the prism's end face multiplied by the depth of the prism.
$$\text{Area} = \text{perimeter of small rectangle} \times \text{length}$$
$$\text{Area} = (2 + 5 + 2 + 5) \times 10$$
$$\text{Area} = 14 \times 10$$
$$\text{Area} = 140\text{ cm}^2$$

**2. Area of the two small rectangles (the ends):**
$$\text{Area} = 2 \times (5 \times 2)$$
$$\text{Area} = 2 \times (10)$$
$$\text{Area} = 20\text{ cm}^2$$

#### Step 3: Find the sum of the areas of the faces
$$\text{Total Surface Area} = \text{large rectangle} + 2 \times \text{small rectangle}$$
$$\text{Total Surface Area} = 140 + 20 = 160$$

#### Step 4: Final Answer
The surface area of the rectangular prism is $160\text{ cm}^2$.

### Worked Example 3: Finding the Surface Area of a Triangular Prism

#### Question
Find the surface area of the triangular prism with the following dimensions:
*   Base of triangle = $8\text{ cm}$
*   Height of triangle = $3\text{ cm}$
*   Length of prism = $12\text{ cm}$

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

#### Solution

**Step 1: Sketch and label the net of the prism**
To visualize the surface area, we break the prism down into its individual faces (two triangles and three rectangles).

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Step 2: Find the area of the different shapes in the net**
To find the area of the rectangular faces, we need to calculate the length of the prism, which is multiplied by the perimeter of the triangular bases.

To find the perimeter of the triangular base, we must first find the length of the slanted sides ($x$) using the **Theorem of Pythagoras**.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

Since the triangle is isosceles, the altitude bisects the base into two segments of $4\text{ cm}$ each.

Using Pythagoras:
$$x^2 = 3^2 + 4^2$$
$$x^2 = 9 + 16$$
$$x^2 = 25$$
$$x = \sqrt{25}$$
$$x = 5\text{ cm}$$

**Perimeter of the triangle:**
$$P = 8 + 5 + 5 = 18\text{ cm}$$

**Summary of areas:**

| Shape | Formula | Calculation | Area |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2 Triangles | $2 \times (\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height})$ | $2 \times (\frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 3)$ | $24\text{ cm}^2$ |
| 3 Rectangles | $\text{Perimeter} \times \text{Length}$ | $18 \times 12$ | $216\text{ cm}^2$ |
| **Total Surface Area** | | $24 + 216$ | **$240\text{ cm}^2$** |

---

### Calculation of Surface Area for a Triangular Prism

To find the surface area of the triangular prism, we follow these steps:

**1. Calculate the slant height ($x$) of the triangle:**
Using the Pythagorean theorem:
$$x^2 = 3^2 + \left(\frac{8}{2}\right)^2$$
$$x^2 = 3^2 + 4^2$$
$$x^2 = 25$$
$$\therefore x = 5 \text{ cm}$$

**2. Calculate the perimeter of the triangle:**
$$\text{Perimeter} = 5 + 5 + 8 = 18 \text{ cm}$$

**3. Calculate the area of the large rectangle (lateral surface):**
$$\text{Area of large rectangle} = \text{perimeter of triangle} \times \text{length}$$
$$\text{Area} = 18 \times 12 = 216 \text{ cm}^2$$

**4. Calculate the area of the triangular faces:**
$$\text{Area of triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times b \times h$$
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 3 = 12 \text{ cm}^2$$

**Step 3: Find the sum of the areas of the faces**
$$\text{Surface area} = \text{area large rectangle} + (2 \times \text{area of triangle})$$
$$\text{Surface area} = 216 + 2(12)$$
$$\text{Surface area} = 240 \text{ cm}^2$$

**Step 4: Final Answer**
The surface area of the triangular prism is $240 \text{ cm}^2$.

### Worked Example: Calculating the Surface Area of a Cylinder

To calculate the surface area of a cylinder, we break the shape down into its net (the flattened 2D representation).

#### Step 1: Sketch and label the net of the cylinder
<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

#### Step 2: Find the area of the different shapes in the net
The total surface area is the sum of the area of the rectangular body and the two circular bases.

**1. Area of the large rectangle (lateral surface area):**
The length of the rectangle is equal to the circumference of the circular base ($2\pi r$).
$$\text{area of large rectangle} = \text{circumference of circle} \times \text{length}$$
$$\text{area} = 2\pi r \times l$$
$$\text{area} = 2\pi (10) \times 30$$
$$\text{area} = 1884,9555... \text{ cm}^2$$

**2. Area of the circles:**
$$\text{area of circle} = \pi r^2$$
$$\text{area} = \pi (10)^2$$
$$\text{area} = 314,1592... \text{ cm}^2$$

**3. Total Surface Area:**
$$\text{surface area} = \text{area large rectangle} + (2 \times \text{area of circle})$$
$$\text{surface area} = 1884,9555... + 2(314,1592...)$$
$$\text{surface area} = 2513,3 \text{ cm}^2$$

#### Step 3: Write the final answer
The surface area of the cylinder is $2513,3 \text{ cm}^2$.

### Volume of Prisms and Cylinders

#### Definition: Volume
Volume is the three-dimensional space occupied by an object, or the contents of an object. It is measured in cubic units.

#### Calculating Volume
The volume of right prisms and cylinders is calculated by multiplying the area of the base of the solid by the height of the solid:
$$V = \text{Area of base} \times \text{height}$$

### 13.3 Right Pyramids, Right Cones, and Spheres

#### Definitions
*   **Pyramid:** A geometric solid that has a polygon as its base and faces that converge at a point called the apex. The faces are not perpendicular to the base.
*   **Right Pyramid:** A pyramid where the line segment connecting the apex to the centre of the base is perpendicular to the base.
*   **Cone:** Similar to a pyramid, but the base is a circle instead of a polygon.
*   **Sphere:** A solid that is perfectly round and looks the same from any direction.

# Surface Area of Pyramids, Cones, and Spheres

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

## Summary of Surface Area Formulas

The surface area of a 3D object is the total area of all its outer faces. For pyramids and cones, this is calculated by summing the area of the base and the area of the lateral sides (or walls).

| Shape | Diagram | Surface Area Formula |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Square pyramid** | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> | $\text{Surface area} = b^2 + 4(\frac{1}{2}bh_s) = b(b + 2h_s)$ |
| **Triangular pyramid** | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> | $\text{Surface area} = (\frac{1}{2}b \times h_b) + 3(\frac{1}{2}b \times h_s) = \frac{1}{2}b(h_b + 3h_s)$ |
| **Right cone** | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> | $\text{Surface area} = \pi r^2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 2\pi rh = \pi r(r + h)$ |
| **Sphere** | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> | $\text{Surface area} = 4\pi r^2$ |

### Key Variables
*   $b$: Base length
*   $h_s$: Slant height of the triangular face
*   $h_b$: Height of the triangular base
*   $r$: Radius of the circular base or sphere
*   $h$: Vertical height of the cone

---

### Worked Example: Finding the Surface Area of a Triangular Pyramid

#### Question
Find the surface area of the given triangular pyramid (correct to one decimal place).

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

#### Solution

**Step 1: Find the area of the base**
The area of the base triangle is given by the formula:
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}bh_b$$

To find the height of the base triangle ($h_b$), we use the Theorem of Pythagoras on the base triangle:

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

$$6^2 = 3^2 + h_b^2$$
$$\therefore h_b = \sqrt{6^2 - 3^2}$$
$$h_b = \sqrt{36 - 9}$$
$$h_b = \sqrt{27}$$
$$h_b = 3\sqrt{3}$$

Now, calculate the area of the base triangle:
$$\text{Area of base} = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 3\sqrt{3}$$
$$\text{Area of base} = 9\sqrt{3} \text{ cm}^2$$

**Step 2: Find the area of the sides**
The pyramid has three identical triangular faces. The area of these sides is calculated as:
$$\text{Area of sides} = 3 \left( \frac{1}{2} \times b \times h_s \right)$$
$$\text{Area of sides} = 3 \left( \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 10 \right)$$
$$\text{Area of sides} = 3 \times 30$$
$$\text{Area of sides} = 90 \text{ cm}^2$$

**Step 3: Calculate Total Surface Area**
$$\text{Total Surface Area} = \text{Area of base} + \text{Area of sides}$$
$$\text{Total Surface Area} = 9\sqrt{3} + 90$$
$$\text{Total Surface Area} \approx 15.588 + 90$$
$$\text{Total Surface Area} \approx 105.6 \text{ cm}^2$$

### Worked Example 11: Examining the Surface Area of a Cone

**Question:**
If a cone has a height of $h$ and a base of radius $r$, show that the surface area is: $\pi r^2 + \pi r\sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Sketch and label the cone**
<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Step 2: Identify the faces that make up the cone**
The cone consists of two faces:
1. The circular base with radius $r$.
2. The curved wall (lateral surface).

When the curved wall is opened out, it forms a sector of a circle.
<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

The curved surface can be divided into many thin triangles with a height approximately equal to $a$ (the slant height). The area of these triangles can be summed as follows:

$$\text{Area of sector} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height (of a small triangle)}$$
$$\text{Area of sector} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2\pi r \times a$$
$$\text{Area of sector} = \pi r a$$

**Step 3: Relate slant height to vertical height**
Using the Pythagorean theorem on the right-angled triangle formed by the height $h$, radius $r$, and slant height $a$:
$$a^2 = r^2 + h^2$$
$$a = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$$

**Step 4: Combine the areas**
The total surface area is the sum of the base area and the curved surface area:
$$\text{Total Surface Area} = \text{Area of base} + \text{Area of curved surface}$$
$$\text{Total Surface Area} = \pi r^2 + \pi r a$$
Substituting $a = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$:
$$\text{Total Surface Area} = \pi r^2 + \pi r\sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$$

---

### Calculating the Surface Area of a Right Cone

To calculate the total surface area of a right cone, follow these logical steps:

#### Step 3: Calculate the slant height ($a$)
The slant height ($a$) is the hypotenuse of the right-angled triangle formed by the vertical height ($h$) and the radius ($r$) of the base. Use the theorem of Pythagoras:
$$a = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$$

#### Step 4: Calculate the area of the circular base ($A_b$)
The base of the cone is a circle. Use the standard area formula for a circle:
$$A_b = \pi r^2$$

#### Step 5: Calculate the area of the curved walls ($A_w$)
The lateral surface area (the curved part) of the cone is calculated using the radius and the slant height:
$$A_w = \pi r a$$
Substituting the formula for $a$ from Step 3:
$$A_w = \pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$$

#### Step 6: Find the total surface area ($A$)
The total surface area is the sum of the base area and the curved wall area:
$$A = A_b + A_w$$
$$A = \pi r^2 + \pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$$
Factoring out the common terms:
$$A = \pi r (r + \sqrt{r^2 + h^2})$$

### 13.4 The Effect of Multiplying a Dimension by a Factor of $k$

When one or more dimensions of a prism or cylinder are multiplied by a constant $k$, the surface area and volume change accordingly. These relationships allow for simpler calculations when dimensions are scaled up or down.

#### Worked Example: Scaling a Rectangular Prism
Consider a rectangular prism with dimensions $l$, $b$, and $h$. If we multiply one dimension (height $h$) by a factor of $5$, the new volume and surface area are calculated as follows:

| Dimensions | Volume | Surface Area |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Original** | $V = l \times b \times h = lbh$ | $A = 2[(l \times h) + (l \times b) + (b \times h)] = 2(lh + lb + bh)$ |
| **Multiply $h$ by $5$** | $V_1 = l \times b \times 5h = 5(lbh) = 5V$ | $A_1 = 2[(l \times 5h) + (l \times b) + (b \times 5h)] = 2(5lh + lb + 5bh)$ |

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Key Observations:**
*   When one dimension is multiplied by $k$, the volume is multiplied by $k$.
*   The surface area does not change by a single constant factor because the individual faces are affected differently depending on whether they include the scaled dimension.

### Worked Example 18: Calculating the new dimensions of a rectangular prism

**Question:**
Consider a rectangular prism with a height of $4\text{ cm}$ and base lengths of $3\text{ cm}$ and $3\text{ cm}$.
<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

1. Calculate the surface area and volume.
2. Calculate the new surface area ($A_n$) and volume ($V_n$) if the base lengths are multiplied by a constant factor of $3$.
3. Express the new surface area and volume as a factor of the original surface area and volume.

**Solution:**

**1. Original Calculations:**
*   **Volume ($V$):** $V = l \times b \times h = 3 \times 3 \times 4 = 36\text{ cm}^3$
*   **Surface Area ($A$):** $A = 2(lh + lb + bh) = 2(3 \times 4 + 3 \times 3 + 3 \times 4) = 2(12 + 9 + 12) = 2(33) = 66\text{ cm}^2$

**2. New Calculations (Multiplying base lengths by $k=3$):**
*   New dimensions: $l = 9\text{ cm}$, $b = 9\text{ cm}$, $h = 4\text{ cm}$
*   **New Volume ($V_n$):** $V_n = 9 \times 9 \times 4 = 324\text{ cm}^3$
*   **New Surface Area ($A_n$):** $A_n = 2(9 \times 4 + 9 \times 9 + 9 \times 4) = 2(36 + 81 + 36) = 2(153) = 306\text{ cm}^2$

**3. Expressing as a factor:**
*   **Volume factor:** $\frac{V_n}{V} = \frac{324}{36} = 9$. Since only two dimensions were multiplied by $3$, the volume increased by $3^2 = 9$.
*   **Surface Area factor:** $\frac{A_n}{A} = \frac{306}{66} \approx 4,636$. (Note: When only specific dimensions are changed, the surface area does not scale by a simple $k^2$ factor for all faces).

### Worked Example: Effect of Changing Dimensions on Volume and Surface Area

This example demonstrates how changing the dimensions of a rectangular prism affects its volume and surface area.

#### Step 1: Calculate the original volume and surface area
Given dimensions: $l = 3\text{ cm}$, $b = 3\text{ cm}$, $h = 4\text{ cm}$.

**Volume ($V$):**
$$V = l \times b \times h$$
$$V = 3 \times 3 \times 4$$
$$V = 36\text{ cm}^3$$

$$A = 2[(l \times h) + (l \times b) + (b \times h)]$$
$$A = 2[(3 \times 4) + (3 \times 3) + (3 \times 4)]$$
$$A = 66\text{ cm}^2$$

---

#### Step 2: Calculate the new volume and surface area
Two of the dimensions are multiplied by a factor of $3$. The new dimensions are $3l$, $3b$, and $h$.

**New Volume ($V_n$):**
$$V_n = 3l \times 3b \times h$$
$$V_n = 3(3) \times 3(3) \times 4$$
$$V_n = 324\text{ cm}^3$$

**New Surface Area ($A_n$):**
$$A_n = 2[(3l \times h) + (3l \times 3b) + (3b \times h)]$$
$$A_n = 2[(3(3) \times 4) + (3(3) \times 3(3)) + (3(3) \times 4)]$$

### Lesson: The Effect of Multiplying Dimensions by a Factor of $k$

When the dimensions of a 3D object are changed by a constant factor $k$, the volume and surface area change according to specific mathematical relationships.

---

#### Worked Example 19: Multiplying the height of a rectangular prism by $k$

**Question:** Prove that if the height of a rectangular prism with dimensions $l$, $b$, and $h$ is multiplied by a constant value $k$, the volume will also increase by a factor of $k$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Calculate the original volume:**
    The original volume $V$ is given by:
    $$V = l \times b \times h$$

2.  **Calculate the new volume:**
    The new dimensions are $l$, $b$, and $kh$. The new volume $V_n$ is:
    $$V_n = l \times b \times (kh)$$
    $$V_n = k(lbh)$$
    $$V_n = kV$$

3.  **Conclusion:**
    If the height of a rectangular prism is multiplied by a constant $k$, the volume increases by a factor of $k$.

---

#### Worked Example 20: Multiplying the radius of a cylinder by $k$

**Question:** Consider a cylinder with a radius of $r$ and a height of $h$. Calculate the new volume and surface area (expressed in terms of $r$ and $h$) if the radius is multiplied by a constant factor of $k$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Original Formulas:**
    *   Volume: $V = \pi r^2 h$
    *   Surface Area: $A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$

2.  **New Dimensions:**
    The new radius is $kr$ and the height remains $h$.

3.  **New Volume ($V_n$):**
    Substitute the new radius into the volume formula:
    $$V_n = \pi (kr)^2 h$$
    $$V_n = \pi (k^2 r^2) h$$
    $$V_n = k^2 (\pi r^2 h)$$
    $$V_n = k^2 V$$
    *The volume increases by a factor of $k^2$.*

4.  **New Surface Area ($A_n$):**
    Substitute the new radius into the surface area formula:
    $$A_n = 2\pi (kr)^2 + 2\pi (kr)h$$
    $$A_n = 2\pi k^2 r^2 + 2\pi krh$$
    *Note: Unlike volume, the surface area does not scale by a single constant factor because the $r^2$ term scales by $k^2$ while the $rh$ term scales by $k$.*

| Radius ($r$) | Cylinder | Multiplied by $k^2$ |

### Worked Example: Scaling Dimensions of a Cylinder

This example demonstrates how changing the dimensions of a cylinder by a factor $k$ affects its volume ($V$) and surface area ($A$).

#### Step 1: Calculate the original volume and surface area
The original formulas for a cylinder are:
$$V = \pi r^2 \times h$$
$$A = \pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$$

#### Step 2: Calculate the new volume and surface area
If the radius is scaled by a factor $k$, the new radius is $kr$. The new volume ($V_n$) and surface area ($A_n$) are calculated as follows:

**New Volume:**
$$V_n = \pi(kr)^2 \times h$$
$$V_n = \pi k^2 r^2 \times h$$
$$V_n = k^2 \times \pi r^2 h$$
$$V_n = k^2 V$$

**New Surface Area:**
$$A_n = \pi(kr)^2 + 2\pi(kr)h$$
$$A_n = \pi k^2 r^2 + 2\pi krh$$
$$A_n = k^2 (\pi r^2) + k(2\pi rh)$$

### Geometric Solids: Pyramids, Cones, and Spheres

#### Definitions
*   **Pyramid:** A geometric solid with a polygon as its base and sides that converge at a single point called the **apex**. The sides are not perpendicular to the base.
*   **Right Pyramid:** A pyramid where the line connecting the apex to the centre of the base is perpendicular to the base.
*   **Naming:** Pyramids are named according to the shape of their base (e.g., triangular pyramid, square pyramid).
*   **Cones:** Similar to pyramids, but with a circular base.
*   **Spheres:** Solids that are perfectly round and appear the same from any direction.

---

For right pyramids, right cones, and spheres:

*   **Square pyramid:** $\frac{1}{3} \times b^2 \times H$

*   **Right cone:** $\frac{1}{3} \times \pi r^2 \times H$
*   **Sphere:** $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$

#### 3. Scaling and Proportionality
**Problem 16:** If the length of each side of a square is a quarter ($\frac{1}{4}$) of its original size, what will the area of the new square be?

*   **Concept:** If side length $s$ becomes $k \cdot s$, the area becomes $k^2 \cdot \text{Area}$.

    *   New height $H' = \frac{1}{2}H$
    *   New Volume $V' = \pi (\frac{1}{2}r)^2 (\frac{1}{2}H)$
    *   $V' = \pi (\frac{1}{4}r^2) (\frac{1}{2}H) = \frac{1}{8} (\pi r^2 H)$
*   **Result:** The new volume will be $\frac{1}{8}$ of the original volume.

### 19. Analysis of Geometric Solids

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Given Dimensions:**
*   **Cylinder:** Radius $r = 4\text{ cm}$, Height $h = 10\text{ cm}$

*   **Cylinder:** $351.9 \times 9 = 3167.1\text{ cm}^2$
*   **Triangular Prism:** $384.0 \times 9 = 3456.0\text{ cm}^2$
*   **Rectangular Prism:** $76.0 \times 9 = 684.0\text{ cm}^2$
