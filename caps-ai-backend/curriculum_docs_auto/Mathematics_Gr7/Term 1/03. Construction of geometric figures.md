## 4.1 Angles revision

When two lines point in different directions, they are said to be **at an angle** to each other.
*   If the directions are almost the same, the angle is **small**.
*   If the directions are very different, the angle is **big**.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Words used to describe angles
*   **Arms of the angle:** The two lines that are at an angle to each other.
*   **The vertex:** The point where the two arms meet.
*   **Vertices:** The plural of "vertex".

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Symbols to describe angles
*   **Arrowheads:** These indicate that the lines continue indefinitely.
*   **Arm length:** The length of the arms does not change the size of the angle. Whether the arms are long or short, the angle size remains the same.
*   **Two angles at a vertex:** There are always two angles formed at a vertex. It is important to use an arc to clearly indicate which angle is being referred to.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Labelling angles
There are various ways to label angles, including:
1.  **Using a dot or a star:** Placing a symbol inside the angle.
2.  **Using numbers:** Placing a number (e.g., $\angle 1$) inside the angle.
3.  **Right angle:** A specific angle measuring $90^\circ$, often indicated by a square symbol at the vertex.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Naming Angles
An angle is formed where two lines meet. You can name an angle in three ways:
*   Using three letters with the vertex (the corner) in the middle: $\hat{ABC}$ or $\hat{CBA}$.
*   Using only the letter at the vertex: $\hat{B}$.
*   The "hat" symbol ($\hat{\phantom{x}}$) placed over the letter indicates the position of the angle.

## 4.2 The degree: a unit for measuring angles

Just as we require standard units (like metres, centimetres, or kilometres) to measure length, we require a standard unit to measure angles. 

Historically, it was decided that a full rotation (a revolution) should be divided into 360 equal parts. We call these parts **degrees**. The symbol for a degree is $^\circ$.

### Comparing Angles Using A4 Paper


![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0003-07.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0003-07.png)


**Instructions:**
1. Take a sheet of A4 paper.
2. Number the four corners (each is a right angle).
3. Tear the corners off to use as measuring tools.

#### Investigations:
1. **Straight angle:** Show that a straight angle is equal to two right angles ($90^\circ + 90^\circ = 180^\circ$).
2. **Revolution:** Show that a revolution is equal to four right angles ($4 \times 90^\circ = 360^\circ$).
3. **Right angle construction:** Create a right angle using three of your corners.
4. **Checking angles:** Use a corner to check if an unknown angle is:
   * **Acute:** Smaller than the corner.
   * **Right:** Exactly the same as the corner.
   * **Obtuse:** Larger than the corner.

#### Folding Exercises:
(a) Fold corner 1 to measure $45^\circ$ (half of $90^\circ$).
(b) Fold corner 2 to measure $30^\circ$ (a third of $90^\circ$).
(c) Fold corner 3 to measure $22,5^\circ$ (a quarter of $90^\circ$).

(d) **Calculation Task:** Which is bigger: a right angle or (half a right angle + a third of a right angle + a quarter of a right angle)?

**Calculation:**
$$\text{Right angle} = 90^\circ$$
$$\text{Sum} = \frac{1}{2}(90^\circ) + \frac{1}{3}(90^\circ) + \frac{1}{4}(90^\circ)$$
$$\text{Sum} = 45^\circ + 30^\circ + 22,5^\circ = 97,5^\circ$$
Since $97,5^\circ > 90^\circ$, the sum of the parts is bigger.

**Important:** Keep your folded pieces of paper for the next lesson.

# 4.3 Using the Protractor

A **protractor** is a specialized instrument used for measuring angles. Regardless of the physical size of the protractor, all protractors measure angles in the same way.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0004-03.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0004-03.png)


### Key Components of a Protractor
*   **Origin:** The center point of the protractor's base line.
*   **Base Line:** The horizontal line that aligns with one arm of the angle.
*   **Degree Scales:** The markings along the curved edge. Most protractors feature two scales:
    *   **Clockwise degree scale:** Used when the angle opens to the right.
    *   **Anticlockwise degree scale:** Used when the angle opens to the left.

---

### Measuring Familiar Angles
To practice using your protractor, measure the following standard angles:
*   $90^\circ$
*   $45^\circ$
*   $30^\circ$
*   $22,5^\circ$

**Troubleshooting Checklist:**
If your measurements are incorrect, verify the following:
1.  Is the **vertex** of the angle placed exactly at the **origin** of the protractor?
2.  Is the bottom arm of the angle perfectly aligned with the **base line**?
3.  Were the initial folds (used to create the angles) made accurately?

---

### How to Use a Protractor to Measure an Angle

#### Step 1: Ensure Angle Arms are Long Enough
For an accurate reading, the arms of the angle must extend beyond the edge of the protractor. 

*   **Instruction:** If the arms are too short, use a sharp pencil and a ruler to extend them.
*   **Tip:** Ensure the ruler is perfectly aligned with the existing arm to avoid introducing errors.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0004-04.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0004-04.png)


*   **Incorrect Method:** Using only a small part of the arm or failing to align the ruler carefully results in inaccurate measurements.
*   **Correct Method:** Align the ruler carefully along the entire length of the arm to extend it accurately.

### Step 2: Line up the angle and your protractor

To measure an angle accurately, you must position your protractor correctly. Follow these two essential rules:

1.  **The Origin:** Place the origin (the center point) of the protractor exactly on the **vertex** of the angle.
2.  **The Base Line:** Align the base line of the protractor exactly on top of one of the arms of the angle.

#### Positioning Checklist
*   **Incorrect:** If the origin is not on the vertex, your measurement will be inaccurate.
*   **Incorrect:** If the base line is at an angle to the arm, your measurement will be inaccurate.
*   **Correct:** The origin is on the vertex AND the base line is perfectly aligned with the arm.

### Step 3: Measure the Angle

To measure an angle accurately using a protractor, follow these guidelines:

*   **Understanding the Scales:** A protractor features two scales: a clockwise scale and an anticlockwise scale.
*   **Choosing the Correct Scale:** Always identify the scale that starts with $0^\circ$ on the arm of the angle you are measuring.
*   **Reading the Measurement:** Follow the angle arm until it passes under the degree scale. The value at this point is the measurement of the angle.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0006-05.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0006-05.png)


#### Key Observations
*   **Inner Scale:** Used when the $0^\circ$ mark is on the right side of the base line (anticlockwise).
*   **Outer Scale:** Used when the $0^\circ$ mark is on the left side of the base line (clockwise).
*   **Consistency:** Regardless of which arm you align with $0^\circ$, the resulting measurement for the same angle will be identical (e.g., $37^\circ$).

### 4.5 Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

#### Perpendicular Lines
*   **Definition:** Perpendicular lines are lines that meet each other at an angle of $90^{\circ}$.
*   **Notation:** If line $AB$ is perpendicular to line $DC$, we write: $AB \perp DC$.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0010-08.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0010-08.png)


#### Parallel Lines
*   **Definition:** Parallel lines never meet each other. They maintain an equal distance apart and travel in the same direction.
*   **Notation:** If line $PQ$ is parallel to line $RS$, we write: $PQ \parallel RS$.
*   **Visual Indicator:** Arrows placed on the middle of the lines indicate that they are parallel to each other.

# Constructing Circles with a Compass

## Key Concept: Concentric Circles
**Definition:** Concentric circles are circles that share the exact same midpoint (centre).

---

## Instructions for Constructing a Circle
To construct a circle with a specific radius (e.g., $2\text{ cm}$) using a compass:

### Step 1: Setting the Radius
Place the pointed tip of the compass on the zero line of your ruler. Carefully widen the arms of the compass until the pencil tip is exactly at the $2\text{ cm}$ mark. Ensure the pointed tip remains on zero and the gap does not change.

### Step 2: Marking the Centre
Mark point $A$ in the centre of your page. Gently push the pointed tip of the compass into point $A$ just deep enough to keep it in place.



![Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0014-12.png](images/Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0014-12.png)
> **[Diagram Context - Textbook_Mathematics_Gr7_Graphs.pdf-0014-12.png]**

The visible labels include headings for "Number of weeks," "Money saved in rand," "Number of friends," and "Chocolates per friend," with corresponding numerical values and grid axes labeled "Money saved (R)" and "Number of chocolates per friend." Conceptually, this diagram helps learners investigate rates of change by demonstrating how to translate word problems into tables and visual bar graphs, highlighting the difference between constant growth (direct proportion) and decreasing values (inverse proportion).



### Step 3: Positioning
Hold the handle of the compass between the forefinger and thumb of your writing hand. Keep your other hand away from the compass. Use only one hand to draw the circle.

### Step 4: Drawing the Circle
Twist the handle between your thumb and forefinger:
*   **Right-handed:** It is easiest to twist the compass clockwise.
*   **Left-handed:** It is easiest to turn the compass anticlockwise.
*   **Technique:** Let the pencil tip *drag* over the paper. Do not push down too hard; apply light pressure on the pointed arm so the pencil moves smoothly.

### Tips for Drawing Circles

If you are having difficulty drawing accurate circles, consider the following troubleshooting tips:

*   **Check Compass Width:** If your circles are turning into spirals, the arms of your compass have likely moved. Use a ruler to check and reset the width.
*   **Tighten the Hinge:** If the compass arms will not stay in the set position, the nut at the hinge is loose. Tighten it or ask your teacher for assistance.
*   **Mastering the "Twist":** To turn the compass smoothly, imagine you are rolling a small piece of soft clay between your thumb and forefinger. Let the compass hang from your hand in the air and practice this sliding movement on scrap paper until you can turn it easily.

### Conceptualizing Angles
Angles can be understood in two primary ways:
1. **Intersection:** The space between two lines that cross or meet at a common point called a vertex.
2. **Rotation:** The amount of turning about a point (e.g., opening a door). A full turn is referred to as a **revolution**.

### 4.2 The degree: a unit for measuring angles

#### Concept: Why do we need units?
Just as we need standard units (like metres or centimetres) to measure length, we need a standard unit to measure the size of an angle. Without these units, it would be impossible to accurately construct buildings, tailor clothes, or mark out sports fields.

#### Definition: The Degree
A degree is the standard unit used to measure angles. Historically, it was decided that one full revolution (a complete circle) should be divided into $360$ equal parts. Each of these parts is called one degree, represented by the symbol $^\circ$.

*   **Full revolution:** $360^\circ$
*   **Unit angle:** If we fit $360$ equal wedges around a point, each wedge has an angle of $1^\circ$.

#### Angles as Fractions of a Revolution
Angles can be understood as fractions of a full revolution ($360^\circ$). To find the size of an angle in degrees, multiply the fraction by $360^\circ$.

*   **Obtuse:** If the corner of the paper is smaller than the angle, the angle is obtuse ($> 90^\circ$).

#### 4. Worked Example: Calculating Angle Sums

**Calculation:**
$$\left(\frac{1}{2} \times 90^\circ\right) + \left(\frac{1}{3} \times 90^\circ\right) + \left(\frac{1}{4} \times 90^\circ\right)$$
$$= 45^\circ + 30^\circ + 22,5^\circ$$
$$= 97,5^\circ$$

**Conclusion:**
Since $97,5^\circ > 90^\circ$, the combined angle is larger than a right angle.

### Measuring Angles with a Protractor

#### 1. Key Concepts
*   **Vertex:** The point where two lines meet to form an angle.
*   **Base Line:** The line on the protractor that must align with one arm of the angle.
*   **Origin:** The centre point of the protractor, which must be placed exactly on the vertex of the angle.
*   **Obtuse Angle:** An angle larger than $90^\circ$ but smaller than $180^\circ$.
*   **Reflex Angle:** An angle larger than $180^\circ$.

---

#### 2. Steps to Measure an Angle
1.  **Positioning:** Place the origin of the protractor exactly on the vertex of the angle.
2.  **Alignment:** Pivot the protractor until the base line lies exactly on one of the arms of the angle.
3.  **Scale Selection:** Determine whether to use the inner or outer scale based on which side the angle opens.

#### 3. Measuring Reflex Angles
To measure a reflex angle (e.g., $\angle BAC$), use one of the following methods:

**Method A: Addition**
1.  Lengthen one side of the angle to create a straight line ($180^\circ$).
2.  Measure the remaining acute or obtuse angle ($\angle DAC$).
3.  Calculate the total:
    $$\angle BAC = 180^\circ + \angle DAC$$
    *Example:* If $\angle DAC = 60^\circ$, then $\angle BAC = 180^\circ + 60^\circ = 240^\circ$.

**Method B: Subtraction**
1.  Measure the interior obtuse angle.
2.  Subtract this value from a full revolution ($360^\circ$).
    $$\angle \text{Reflex} = 360^\circ - \angle \text{Obtuse}$$

### 4.7 Using the Compass

#### Key Concepts and Definitions
*   **Circle:** A shape where the distance from the centre to any point on the edge (circumference) is constant in all directions.
*   **Radius:** The fixed distance from the centre of a circle to any point on its edge.
    *   **Plural:** Radii (pronounced "ray-dee-eye").
    *   **Singular:** Radius (pronounced "ray-dee-us").
*   **Compass:** A specialized instrument used for drawing circles accurately. It consists of a pointy tip (the centre) and a pencil tip (the drawing edge).

#### Why use a Compass instead of String?
Using a string-and-pencil method is often inaccurate because:
1.  String is stretchy, which changes the radius.
2.  The pencil can wander off course, resulting in a spiral or wobbly curve rather than a perfect circle.

#### Anatomy of a Compass
<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

*   **The Pointy Tip:** Acts as the fixed centre of the circle.
*   **The Pencil Tip:** Used to draw the circumference.
*   **The Handle:** Held between the forefinger and thumb.
*   **Operation:** Use only one hand to twist the compass by the handle between your forefinger and thumb.
*   **Adjusting the Radius:** Change the gap between the two arms of the compass to match the desired radius measurement on a ruler. Clamp the pencil tightly to ensure the gap remains constant while drawing.

#### Teaching Guidelines
1.  **Demonstration:** Teachers should demonstrate the correct technique for handling the compass.
2.  **Practice:**
    *   Learners must practice measuring a radius accurately using a ruler.
    *   Learners should practice drawing full circles.
    *   Learners should practice making small marks on a circumference using the compass point.
3.  **Investigation:** Encourage learners to consider if other shapes (like squares, hexagons, or ellipses) maintain a constant distance from the centre to the edge in all directions.

#### Think About It
*   **Question:** Can you think of any other figure where the distance between the centre and the edge is constant in all directions?
    *   A square?
    *   A hexagon?
    *   An oval shape (ellipse)?

# 4.9 Parallel and Perpendicular Lines with Circles

## Key Concepts and Definitions

### Parallel Lines
Parallel lines are lines that are always the same distance apart and will never meet, no matter how far they are extended.

### Perpendicular Lines
Perpendicular lines are lines that meet at right angles ($90^\circ$).

### Intersection
When two lines (or arcs) cross each other, we say they **intersect**. The **intersection point** is the specific location where they meet.

### Extension: Geometric Constructions with Circles

#### 1. Investigating Points on a Circumference
*   **Concept:** A circle is defined as the set of all points that are at a fixed distance (the radius) from a central point.

#### 2. Intersection of Two Circles
*   **Concept:** When two circles of the same radius ($r = 3\text{ cm}$) are drawn with centers at $A$ and $B$, the points where the circles intersect ($P$ and $Q$) are equidistant from both $A$ and $B$.
*   **Investigation:** 
    *   Draw a circle with center $A$ and radius $3\text{ cm}$.
    *   Draw a second circle with center $B$ and the same radius ($3\text{ cm}$).
    *   Label the intersection points $P$ and $Q$.
    *   Connect points $P$ and $Q$ to form line segment $PQ$.
    *   **Observation:** Line $PQ$ is perpendicular to line $AB$. Any point on line $PQ$ is equidistant from points $A$ and $B$.
