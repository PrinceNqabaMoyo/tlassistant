## 2.1 Circuits and Current Electricity

### What is an electric current?
An electric current is the movement of charge in a closed, conducting circuit.

#### Atomic Structure and Delocalised Electrons
In atoms, electrons are arranged in the outer space around a central nucleus. In metals, electrons are not associated with a particular atom; they are able to move freely within the metal. We say these electrons are **delocalised**.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Definition of Electric Current
When a metal conducting wire is connected to a source of energy in a complete circuit, the delocalised electrons move in the same general direction through the wire. This flow of electrons through a conductor is defined as **electric current**.

---

### New Words
* **Delocalised:** Electrons that are not bound to a specific atom and can move freely.
* **Component:** A part of an electrical circuit (e.g., battery, bulb, resistor).
* **Conductor:** A material that allows electric charge to flow through it easily.
* **Electric circuit:** A closed path through which an electric current flows.
* **Electric current:** The flow of electric charge.
* **Qualitative:** Relating to the nature or quality of something rather than its quantity.
* **Resistor:** A component that opposes the flow of electric current.
* **Switch:** A device for making and breaking the connection in an electric circuit.

#### Revision: Electric Circuits
An electric circuit is a system designed to transfer electrical energy. Key principles include:

*   **Source of Energy:** Every circuit requires a source, such as a cell or a battery.
*   **Terminals:** Cells possess both positive and negative terminals.
*   **Complete Pathway:** A circuit must provide a continuous, unbroken path for electricity to flow.
*   **Closed Circuit:** The circuit must be **closed** (complete) for a device, such as a light bulb, to function.
*   **Closed System:** An electric circuit is defined as a closed system that facilitates the transfer of electrical energy.
*   **Components:** Circuits are constructed from various individual components.

### 2.2 Components of a Circuit

A **complete circuit** is a continuous conducting pathway for electricity. It travels from one terminal of a cell, through a conducting material, passes through a device, and returns to the other terminal of the cell.

#### Key Definitions
*   **Ammeter:** An instrument used to measure the electric current in a circuit.
*   **Cell:** A single unit that provides electrical energy (often referred to as a battery when multiple cells are combined).

### Cells

#### Key Concepts
*   **Electrical Cells:** These act as the source of energy for an electric circuit.
*   **Potential Energy:** Stored energy within the chemicals inside a cell.
*   **Electric Current:** The movement of electrons through conducting wires.

#### How Cells Work
1.  **Chemical Reaction:** Inside a cell, chemicals react with each other.
2.  **Energy Transfer:** This reaction provides electrons with the potential energy required to move through a circuit.
3.  **Kinetic Energy:** As electrons move, they possess kinetic energy.
4.  **Capacity:** Cells come in various sizes (AAA, AA, C, D, and 9-volt). While most small cells have a rating of $1,5V$, larger cells have a larger capacity, meaning they can supply energy for a longer duration before going "flat."
5.  **"Flat" Cells:** A cell goes "flat" when it is no longer able to supply energy through its chemical reactions.

#### Cells vs. Batteries
*   **Terminology:** In shops, cells are often referred to as "batteries."
*   **Definition:** Technically, a battery consists of two or more cells connected together.
*   **Circuit Diagrams:** When representing a battery in a circuit diagram, you must draw two or more cells connected together.

#### What is a Resistor?
Resistors are components that allow electrons to move through them, but they do not make it easy. They are said to **resist** the movement of electrons, thereby influencing the electric current in a circuit.

#### Practical Application: The Kettle
Inside a kettle, there is a large metal coil. This coil acts as a **heating element**.
*   **How it works:** When the kettle is switched on, electrons move through the element.
*   **Energy Transfer:** Because the element is a large resistor, the electrons expend a significant amount of energy to overcome the resistance. This energy is transferred to the surroundings as **heat**, which boils the water.

# Activity: Melting Metal

This activity explores the relationship between electrical resistance, current, and heat energy in a circuit.

### Materials Required
*   Three $1,5\text{ V}$ cells
*   Copper conducting wires with crocodile clips
*   Steel wool
*   Heat-resistant mat or piece of wood
*   Torch light bulb
*   Variable resistor
*   Ammeter

### Instructions
1.  **Circuit Setup:** Arrange the components as shown in the diagram, connecting the cells, ammeter, light bulb, variable resistor, and steel wool in a series circuit. Place the steel wool on the heat-resistant mat.
2.  **Preparation:** Twist a few strands of steel wool to form a thin wire.
3.  **Connection:** Use the steel wool to complete the circuit.
4.  **Initial Test:** Set the variable resistor to its **highest resistance**. Close the switch and observe the effect on the steel wool and the light bulb.
5.  **Measurement:** Record the reading on the ammeter.
6.  **Variable Adjustment:** Open the switch. Adjust the variable resistor to its **lowest resistance**.
7.  **Final Test:** Close the switch again and observe the changes in the steel wool and the light bulb.

### Lesson: Understanding Electric Fuses

#### Core Concept: The Function of a Fuse
A fuse is a safety device designed to protect electrical circuits from damage caused by excessive current (overheating). 

**How it works:**
*   **Heating Effect:** When an electric current flows through a conductor, it generates heat. If the current becomes too high, the heat generated can cause a fire.
*   **The Fuse Mechanism:** A fuse contains a thin wire made of a metal with a low melting point. 
*   **Safety Action:** If the current exceeds a safe limit, the fuse wire heats up rapidly and melts. This creates an "open circuit," which immediately stops the flow of electricity and prevents further damage.

---

#### Analysis of Circuit Behavior
Based on the experimental activity involving a circuit with a light bulb, an ammeter, and steel wool (acting as a fuse), we can derive the following principles:

| Question | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Why is the light bulb included?** | The light bulb acts as a load to demonstrate the flow of current. It provides a visual indicator that the circuit is complete and functioning. |
| **What happens to current when resistance decreases?** | According to Ohm's Law, $I = \frac{V}{R}$. If resistance ($R$) decreases while voltage ($V$) remains constant, the current ($I$) increases. The ammeter reading will rise. |
| **What happens when the steel wool burns?** | The circuit becomes "open." The path for electrons is broken, meaning the electric current drops to $0\text{ A}$. |

---

#### Mathematical Context: Ohm's Law
The relationship between current, voltage, and resistance is fundamental to understanding why fuses are necessary.

*   **Formula:** 
    $$I = \frac{V}{R}$$
    Where:
    *   $I$ = Current (measured in Amperes, $\text{A}$)
    *   $V$ = Voltage (measured in Volts, $\text{V}$)
    *   $R$ = Resistance (measured in Ohms, $\Omega$)

**Key Takeaway:**
When resistance ($R$) is reduced to near zero (a short circuit), the current ($I$) tends toward infinity. This massive surge of current causes the fuse wire to reach its melting point almost instantly, protecting the rest of the circuit components.

### Short Circuits

#### Definition
A **short circuit** occurs when an alternative, "easier" path is accidentally created in an electric circuit. 

*   **Why it is "easier":** The new path offers very little resistance to the electric current. 
*   **Consequence:** Because the resistance is so low, the current flows along this short circuit path instead of through the main circuit. This can be dangerous and cause significant damage to appliances.

#### Safety Warning
Using metal objects (like a butter knife) to remove items from an appliance (like a toaster) is dangerous. 
*   Metal is a **conductor**.
*   If you touch the heating element with a knife, you create a short circuit.
*   The electric current will flow through the knife and through you, which can cause severe injury.

> **TAKE NOTE:** There are different types of fuses. Some require you to replace the fuse if the wire melts. Others work differently to break the circuit and can simply be reset once the problem in the circuit is fixed.

# Electrical Safety and Magnetism

## 1. Circuit Protection: Fuses vs. Circuit Breakers

Electrical circuits require protection to prevent damage caused by overloads or short circuits. Two common devices used for this purpose are fuses and circuit breakers.

### Fuses
A fuse is a safety device containing a thin wire that melts when too much current flows through it. 
*   **Function:** When the current exceeds a specific limit, the wire melts, breaking the circuit and stopping the flow of electricity.
*   **Limitation:** Once a fuse "blows," it must be physically replaced before the circuit can function again.

### Circuit Breakers
A circuit breaker is a modern alternative to a fuse.
*   **Function:** Like a fuse, it stops the current flow during an overload or short circuit.
*   **Advantage:** Unlike a fuse, a circuit breaker does not need to be replaced. It can be reset (either manually or automatically) to restore the circuit to operation.

## 3. Introduction to Magnetism

A **magnet** is a material that produces a magnetic field. All magnets possess two distinct poles: a **North Pole** and a **South Pole**.

### Fundamental Laws of Magnetism
The interaction between magnetic poles follows these rules:
*   **Attraction:** Opposite poles (North and South) attract each other.
*   **Repulsion:** Like poles (North and North, or South and South) repel each other.

### Magnetic Fields
Every magnet is surrounded by a **magnetic field**, which is the region in space where the magnetic force can be detected. 

*   **Key Concept:** In later lessons, we will explore how an electric current flowing through a conductor can produce its own magnetic field, a principle that forms the basis for electromagnets and electric motors.

# The Earth as a Magnet

## Key Concepts
*   **Bar Magnet:** A rectangular piece of magnetic material that has a North pole and a South pole.
*   **Magnetic Field:** The region around a magnet where magnetic forces can be detected.
*   **Earth's Magnetism:** The Earth acts like a giant bar magnet. Its magnetic field is what allows us to use compasses for navigation.
*   **Compass:** A device containing a small, freely rotating magnet (the needle). The needle points towards magnetic north because it is attracted to the Earth's magnetic pole.

## Understanding Earth's Magnetic Field
The Earth possesses a magnetic field that functions as if there were a massive bar magnet running through its core. 
*   **Orientation:** The Earth's magnetic South pole is located under the Earth's geographic North pole.
*   **Navigation:** Because opposite poles attract, the North-seeking pole of a compass needle is attracted to the Earth's magnetic North pole, allowing us to determine direction.

# Activity: Making an Electromagnet

An electromagnet is a type of magnet in which the magnetic field is produced by an electric current. The magnetic field disappears when the current is turned off.

### Materials Required
*   One iron nail (approximately $15\text{ cm}$ long)
*   $3\text{ metres}$ of $22$ gauge insulated copper wire
*   Two D cell batteries
*   Paper clips
*   Iron filings

### Instructions
1.  **Coil the wire:** Wrap the insulated copper wire tightly around the nail. Ensure that you wrap the wire in the same direction consistently.
2.  **Prepare the ends:** Strip a small amount of the insulation off each end of the copper wire to expose the metal.
3.  **Connect to power:** Attach the two ends of the insulated copper wire to the terminals of the batteries to complete the circuit.
4.  **Test magnetism:** Hold the wrapped nail above the paper clips.
5.  **Disconnect:** Disconnect the wire from the battery terminals.
6.  **Observe:** Hold the wrapped nail above the paper clips again.
7.  **Visualize:** Place iron filings on a piece of paper around the electromagnet to observe the shape of the magnetic field.

---

| Question | Focus Area |

| 2. Why were the paper clips attracted to the nail? | Understanding the relationship between electric current and magnetism. |
| 3. Did the disconnected nail attract the paper clips? Why? | Understanding the temporary nature of an electromagnet. |

### Scientific Principles
*   **Electromagnetism:** When an electric current flows through a wire, it creates a magnetic field around the wire. By coiling the wire around an iron core (the nail), the magnetic field is concentrated and amplified.
*   **The Iron Core:** The iron nail acts as a ferromagnetic material that becomes magnetized when the current flows, significantly increasing the strength of the magnetic field compared to a wire coil alone.
*   **Temporary Magnetism:** Because the magnetism is dependent on the flow of electrons (current), the nail loses its magnetic properties almost immediately once the circuit is broken.

### Electrolysis: Core Concepts

**Electrolysis** is the process of breaking down a substance into its component elements by passing an electric current through a liquid or solution. This process demonstrates that electric current can trigger chemical reactions.

#### Key Terminology
*   **Electrolysis:** The chemical decomposition produced by passing an electric current through a liquid or solution containing ions.
*   **Electrodes:** Conductors (often carbon or metal rods) through which electricity enters or leaves the electrolyte.
*   **Electroplating:** A practical application of electrolysis where a thin layer of metal is deposited onto another surface.

#### Practical Application: Purification of Copper
Electrolysis is used industrially to purify metals. For example, to purify copper:
1.  A copper sulphate solution is used as the electrolyte.
2.  Two copper electrodes are placed in the solution: one made of **pure copper** and one made of **impure copper**.
3.  As the current flows, the impure copper electrode breaks down.
4.  Pure copper is then deposited onto the pure copper electrode, effectively refining the metal.

### Scientific Principles
The separation of substances like copper chloride occurs because the electric current provides the energy required to overcome the chemical bonds holding the compound together. 

**Safety Note:** When performing experiments involving electrolysis, it is critical to ensure that electrodes do not touch. If the electrodes touch, it creates a **short circuit**, which bypasses the electrolyte solution and can damage the power source or cause overheating.

# Electroplating and Electric Circuits

## Electroplating
Electroplating is one of the most important practical applications of electrolysis. It is the process of coating one metal with a thin, even layer of another metal.

### Why do we electroplate?
*   **Cost-effectiveness:** Inexpensive metals can be coated with precious metals (e.g., coating a base metal ring with gold) to make jewellery more affordable.
*   **Protection:** Metals that rust easily (like iron) can be coated with a layer of zinc to protect them from corrosion.
*   **Durability and Aesthetics:** Many everyday items, such as bathroom taps, car parts, and wheel rims, are electroplated with chromium to improve appearance and prevent wear.

## 1. The Electrical Circuit
An electrical circuit is a **closed, conducting pathway** that allows for the transfer of energy.

*   **Components:** Circuits are made of various components, including:
    *   **Cells/Batteries:** Store energy in the form of chemicals. When the circuit is closed, these chemicals react to provide energy.
    *   **Switches:** Used to open or close the pathway.
    *   **Conducting Wires:** Provide the path for the flow of charges.
    *   **Resistors:** Components that oppose the flow of charges. Examples include bulb filaments, heating wires, and elements.

## 2. Current and Flow of Charges
*   **Definition:** The flow of charges through a circuit is called **current**.
*   **Energy Transfer:** Energy is transferred from the cell/battery to the rest of the circuit due to the flow of charges.
*   **Resistance:** Resistors oppose the flow of charges. This process can result in:
    *   **Useful output energy:** Such as light from a bulb.
    *   **Heat:** Resistors often heat up during operation.

## 3. Circuit Hazards and Effects
*   **Short Circuit:** Occurs when there is an unintended path for current, often leading to overheating.
*   **Heating:** Excessive current through resistance wires can cause components to overheat.
*   **Effects of Current:** The flow of current can cause various effects, including:
    *   **Electrolysis:** A chemical process driven by electric current.
    *   **Electromagnets:** The creation of magnetic fields using electric current.

# Revision: Electric Circuits and Energy

### 1. Definition of an Electric Circuit
An electric circuit is a closed, continuous path through which an electric current can flow. It typically consists of a source of electrical energy (like a battery), conducting wires, and a load (like a light bulb) that converts the electrical energy into another form.

### 2. Energy in a Battery
A battery contains **chemical potential energy**. This energy is converted into electrical energy when the battery is connected to a circuit.

### 3. Electric Current and Metallic Conduction
*   **Definition:** An electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor.
*   **Mechanism in Metals:** In metals, atoms are arranged in a lattice structure where the outer electrons are not bound to any specific atom. These are known as **delocalised** electrons. Because these electrons are free to move throughout the metal structure, they can flow when a potential difference (voltage) is applied, creating an electric current.

### 4. Conductors of Electricity
Materials that allow electric current to flow through them easily are called conductors. Examples include:
1. Copper
2. Aluminium
3. Iron (or Steel)

### 5. Insulators of Electricity
Materials that do not allow electric current to flow through them are called insulators. Examples include:
1. Plastic
2. Rubber
3. Glass

### 6. Activity: Testing Conductivity
To test whether a material is a conductor or an insulator, you must create a simple series circuit. If the material is a conductor, it will complete the circuit and the light bulb will glow. If it is an insulator, the circuit remains open and the bulb will not glow.

| Component | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Battery** | Provides the energy source (Voltage). |
| **Copper Wires** | Provide the path for the current. |
| **Light Bulb** | Acts as an indicator (load) to show if current is flowing. |
| **Test Material** | Placed in the gap of the circuit to check conductivity. |

**Experimental Setup:**
*   Connect one terminal of the battery to the light bulb using a wire.
*   Connect a second wire to the other terminal of the battery.
*   Leave a gap between the end of the second wire and the light bulb.
*   Place the material you are testing across this gap. If the bulb lights up, the material is a conductor.

### 11. Safety: Fuses vs. Conducting Wire
**Question:** An electrician wants to replace a faulty fuse with a normal piece of conducting wire. Should you let him? Why or why not? [3 marks]

*   **Answer:** No, you should not let him.
*   **Reasoning:** A fuse is a safety device designed to melt and break the circuit if the current becomes too high (overload). A normal piece of conducting wire will not melt at the same threshold, meaning it will not protect the circuit from overheating, which could lead to electrical fires or damage to appliances.

---

### 12. Short Circuits
**Question:** A child, while inserting an electric plug into the socket, did not see that there was a thin piece of aluminium foil stuck between the pins of the plug. When he turned the switch on, he noticed a spark at the plug, and at the lights went out. What could have happened to cause the spark and to make the lights go out? [4 marks]

*   **Explanation:** The aluminium foil created a **short circuit**. Because aluminium is a conductor, it provided a path of very low resistance between the live and neutral pins. This caused a massive surge of current to flow instantly. The spark was caused by the rapid discharge of energy, and the lights went out because the high current caused the circuit breaker to trip or the fuse to blow, cutting off the power supply to protect the system.

---

### 13. Circuit Breakers vs. Fuses
**Question:** What is the benefit of using a circuit breaker rather than a fuse? [2 marks]

*   **Answer:** A fuse is a "one-time" device that must be physically replaced once it blows. A circuit breaker is a switch that can be reset (flipped back on) once the fault is cleared, making it more convenient and cost-effective.

## Key Concepts and Misconceptions

### Circuit Schematics
*   **Definition:** Circuit diagrams are schematic representations of electrical circuits.
*   **Important Note:** A physical circuit built in the laboratory will not look exactly like the schematic diagram. Learners must understand that diagrams are symbolic representations.

### Common Misconception: Wire Colour Coding
*   **The Myth:** Many learners believe that black wires carry negative current and red wires carry positive current.
*   **The Reality:** Colour coding is often arbitrary. In practice, red wires can be used for negative connections, or a single colour of wire can be used for an entire circuit. The colour of the wire does not determine the polarity of the current.

---

## Digital Tools: PhET Simulations
If physical equipment is limited, PhET simulations provide a virtual environment to build and test circuits.

### Essential Skills for PhET Simulations
To effectively use the simulation software, learners should master the following operations:

1.  **Adding Components:** Click, hold, and drag components from the sidebar onto the workspace.
2.  **Connecting Components:** Drag wires to connect components. Be cautious with light bulbs; incorrect connections can result in a **short circuit**.
3.  **Editing/Deleting:** 
    *   To disconnect: Right-click on the junction between two components.
    *   To remove: Right-click on the component itself.
4.  **Measurement:** Utilize the voltmeter and ammeter tools. The non-contact ammeter is recommended for ease of use, while the standard ammeter provides a more realistic experience.
5.  **Managing Files:** Use the "reset all" button to clear the screen, or save circuits to resume work in future lessons.
6.  **Adjusting Settings:** Right-click on components (such as resistors or batteries) to modify their specific properties, such as resistance or potential difference ($V$).

# 8.1 Circuits and Current Electricity

In the study of electricity, we use the concept of **conventional current**. This is defined as the movement of positive charges. This convention was established before the discovery of electrons to allow scientists to communicate and model electrical systems consistently.

3. **Energy source:** The battery.

### Electrolysis of Copper Chloride

#### Experimental Setup Notes
*   **Electrodes:** Pencil carbon can be used as an electrode, though it is not as effective as pure carbon because it is not pure.
*   **Solution Preparation:** Dissolve $15\text{ g}$ of copper chloride in $100\text{ ml}$ of warm water.
*   **Circuit Indicators:** A torch bulb is used to indicate that a current is flowing and that the circuit pathway is complete.
*   **Troubleshooting:** If the bulb does not glow, check the following:
    *   Ensure the electrodes are not touching each other.
    *   Ensure the crocodile clips are not touching each other.
    *   Ensure the crocodile clips are not touching the solution.

#### Observations and Results
1.  **Copper Formation:** A layer of copper will develop on one of the electrodes.
2.  **Gas Evolution:** Bubbles will form at the other electrode; you may be able to smell the chlorine gas.
3.  **Chemical Process:** The electric current chemically separates the copper chloride into pure, solid copper and chlorine gas.
4.  **Reaction Cessation:** If bubbles stop forming, the reaction has stopped.
5.  **Short Circuits:** If electrodes touch, a short circuit occurs. The current will bypass the copper chloride solution, and no chemical separation will take place.

### 11. Fuses as Safety Devices
*   **Definition:** A fuse is a safety device designed to prevent overheating in an electrical circuit.
*   **Function:** It contains a metal wire that melts if the current becomes too high. This breaks the circuit and prevents potential fire hazards or damage to appliances.

### 12. Short Circuits and Conductivity
*   **Conductivity:** Materials like aluminium foil are conductors of electricity.
*   **Short Circuit:** If a conductor creates an unintended path for current, a short circuit occurs. This results in a very large current flow, which causes the fuse to melt and the circuit to break, effectively switching off the electricity to prevent damage.

### 13. Circuit Breakers vs. Fuses
*   **Fuse:** A single-use device; once the metal wire melts, the fuse must be replaced.
*   **Circuit Breaker:** A reusable safety device. It automatically detects faults, breaks the circuit, and can be reset (manually or automatically) once the fault has been repaired.

### 14. How Light Bulbs Work (Tungsten Filament)
*   **Mechanism:** When an electric current passes through a tungsten filament, it encounters high resistance.
*   **Energy Transfer:** As electrons move through the filament, energy is transferred to the wire.
*   **Result:** The wire heats up significantly due to this resistance, causing it to glow and emit light.

### 15. Electromagnetism and Magnetic Fields
*   **a)** Yes, an electric current generates a magnetic field.
*   **b) Evidence:** When current flows through a wire, plotting compasses placed nearby will change their orientation. Instead of pointing in their natural direction, the needles align in a circle around the wire, confirming the presence of a magnetic field generated by the current.

### 16. Maintenance
*   **Purpose:** Protective measures are taken to prevent corrosion and enhance the value/longevity of electrical components.
