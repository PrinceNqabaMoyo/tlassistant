## Core Concepts & Introduction

### The Relationship Between Density, Mass, and Volume
One of the fundamental physical relationships in this chapter is how density, mass, and volume relate to one another. 

The density of a substance is defined as its mass per unit volume. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Where:
* $\rho$ (rho) is the density of the material (typically measured in $\text{g/cm}^3$ or $\text{kg/m}^3$)
* $m$ is the mass of the material (measured in $\text{g}$ or $\text{kg}$)
* $V$ is the volume occupied by the material (measured in $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$)

# Matter and Materials

## What does the particle model of matter teach us?

The particle model describes matter in a very specific way. It describes four important aspects of matter:

* **All matter is made up of particles** that are incredibly small - much too small to see with the naked eye. The particles can be atoms or combinations of atoms that are bonded.
* **There are forces** between the particles.
* **The particles in matter are always moving.** The more energy they have, the faster they move.
* **The spaces between the particles in matter are empty.** You might assume that the spaces between particles are filled with air, but this is not the case. They contain nothing at all (a vacuum).

## Why is the particle model of matter so useful?

The particle model of matter is one of the most useful scientific models because it describes matter in all three states (solid, liquid, and gas). Understanding how the particles of matter behave is vital if we hope to understand science!

The model also helps us to understand what happens to the particles when matter changes from one state to another.

# 2.2 Solids, liquids and gases

We can use the particle model to help us understand the behaviour of each of the states of matter. We are going to look at each state in turn.

There is one very important thing to remember when we consider the different states of matter. For any matter, the individual particles of that matter are exactly the same in all three states, solid, liquid and gas. It is the *behaviour* of the particles that changes in each state.

Take a good look at the picture of the particles in a solid (table salt) above. You will see that they are packed in a **regular arrangement**. There are very small spaces between the particles in a solid.

Particles are held together by **forces of attraction**. In solids, these forces are strong enough to hold the particles firmly in position.

Does that mean the particles in a solid do not move at all? No. The particles in a solid move a little bit. They **vibrate** in their fixed positions. The more energy the particles have, the faster and more strongly they vibrate.

Do you see how we have used the particle model of matter to explain the properties of solids that we can **observe**? For example, the fact that the particles in solids are closely packed and have strong forces between them explains why solids have a fixed shape and why you cannot compress them.

# The liquid state

An important characteristic of liquids is that they flow. They fill containers they are poured into. Liquids are also not very compressible. How can these properties be explained? 

In the liquid state, particles do not have fixed positions. They move about freely, but they stay close together because the forces of attraction between them are quite strong, but not as strong as in solids.

Have you noticed how a liquid always takes the shape of the container it is in? Within the liquid, the particles slip and slide past each other. This is why liquid flows. Their particles are free to move around, filling the spaces left by other particles. Look at the image of the juice being poured. Let's zoom in and have a look at what the particles are doing as the juice is poured.

## Study Summary: The Particle Model of Matter

To help you complete the activity, review these key concepts of the CAPS curriculum:

### 1. Solids
* **Arrangement:** Particles are packed closely together in a regular, fixed pattern (lattice).
* **Movement:** Particles cannot move from their positions; they only vibrate in fixed positions.
* **Forces:** Extremely strong forces of attraction hold the particles together.
* **Spaces:** Very small spaces between the particles.

### 2. Liquids
* **Arrangement:** Particles are close together but arranged randomly (no fixed pattern).
* **Movement:** Particles can move and slide past one another, allowing liquids to flow and take the shape of their container.
* **Forces:** Strong forces of attraction, but weaker than those in solids.
* **Spaces:** Small spaces between the particles, slightly larger than in solids.

### 3. Gases
* **Arrangement:** Particles are very far apart and have a completely random arrangement.
* **Movement:** Particles move rapidly, randomly, and constantly in all directions, colliding with each other and the walls of the container.
* **Forces:** Very weak forces of attraction between particles.
* **Spaces:** Very large spaces between the particles compared to the size of the particles themselves.

# Matter and Materials: Diffusion

## Core Concepts: Diffusion in Liquids and Gases

**Diffusion** is the spontaneous movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration. This process continues until the particles are uniformly distributed throughout the space.

*   **Diffusion in Liquids:** Occurs relatively slowly because liquid particles are close together and experience frequent collisions, which limits their speed of movement.
*   **Diffusion in Gases:** Occurs much faster than in liquids. Gas particles have high kinetic energy, move at high speeds, and have large empty spaces between them, allowing them to mix rapidly.
*   **Factors Influencing Diffusion Rate:**
    *   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of particles, increasing the rate of diffusion.
    *   **State of Matter:** Gases diffuse faster than liquids.
    *   **Air Currents/Agitation:** Physical movement (such as stirring a liquid or waving arms to move air) introduces convection currents, which speeds up the mixing process.

---

## Experiment: How Fast Do Gases Mix?

This practical activity investigates the rate of diffusion of gas molecules (vanilla essence vapor) through air under different conditions.

### Safety and Environmental Control
*   This experiment must be performed with the **windows closed** to prevent external drafts from affecting the natural rate of diffusion.

### Step-by-Step Procedure

1.  **Prepare the Class:** Learners should raise their hands as soon as they can smell the vanilla essence.
2.  **Begin the Experiment:** Pour some vanilla essence into a saucer.
3.  **Record Start Time:** Note the exact time when the vanilla essence is poured.
4.  **First Detection:** Record the time when the first learner puts up their hand to indicate they can smell the vanilla essence.
5.  **Halfway Mark:** Record the time when approximately half ($\frac{1}{2}$) of the learners in the class have their hands raised.
6.  **Full Diffusion:** Record the time when the learners sitting at the very back of the class first smell the vanilla essence.
7.  **Introduce Agitation (Variable Test):** Repeat steps 1–5 under the same conditions, but this time, have a designated person wave their arms to 'wave' and push the air towards the back of the class.

# Particle Model of Matter: Random Movement and Diffusion

---

## 1. Introduction: The Behavior of Fluids
When observing a colored liquid mixing with water, it is impossible to predict the exact direction in which the color will swirl. This unpredictable mixing is driven by the constant, invisible motion of the individual particles making up the liquids.

---

## 2. Random Movement of Particles

Particles in both **liquids** and **gases** are in a state of constant, continuous motion. 

### Key Characteristics of Particle Movement:
* **Unpredictability:** The movements of individual particles are completely unpredictable. Because of this, we say that particles move **randomly**.
* **Collisions:** As particles travel, they constantly bump into one another. 
  * For example, a single gas particle traveling from point $A$ to point $B$ does not move in a straight line. Instead, it undergoes up to $8 \times 10^9$ (eight billion) collisions every single second!
  * Each collision forces the particle to change its direction, resulting in a highly irregular, zigzag path.
* **Distance Travelled:** Because of these constant collisions and changes in direction, the **actual distance** travelled by a particle is significantly longer than the **direct straight-line distance** between its starting point $A$ and ending point $B$.

```
       [Zigzag Path of a Particle]
       
          (Collision)
   A *-------> o
              \
               \
                o-------> o (Collision)
               (Collision) \
                            \---> B
```

---

## 3. Diffusion

The random movement of particles is the fundamental cause of a process known as **diffusion**.

### Definition of Diffusion
> **Diffusion** is the process by which particles in a liquid or a gas spread out from an area of **high concentration** to an area of **low concentration** until they are evenly distributed.

### How Diffusion Works (Step-by-Step):

1. **Initial State (High Concentration):** 
   When particles are first introduced into a container, they are packed closely together in one localized area. This is an area of **high concentration**.
   
2. **Random Motion & Spreading:** 
   Due to their inherent kinetic energy, the particles move randomly in all directions, colliding with each other and the walls of the container.
   
3. **Final State (Even Distribution):** 
   Over time, this continuous random movement causes the particles to spread out and fill all the available space in the container evenly, reaching a state of equilibrium (low concentration throughout).

```
   [Initial State]                 [Final State]
+-----------------+             +-----------------+
|  o o o          |             |  o    o    o    |
|  o o o          |    ====>    |    o    o    o  |
|  o o o          |  Over Time  |  o    o    o    |
+-----------------+             +-----------------+
 High Concentration              Evenly Distributed
   in one corner                   (Diffusion)
```

---

## Summary for Revision

* **Liquids and Gases:** Particles are always moving randomly.
* **Collisions:** Occur billions of times per second ($8 \times 10^9 \text{ collisions/s}$), causing constant changes in direction.
* **Diffusion Direction:** Particles always move down a concentration gradient, from **high concentration** $\rightarrow$ **low concentration**.
* **Goal of Diffusion:** To distribute particles evenly across the entire available volume.

# Factors Affecting the Rate of Diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration. Particles diffuse because they are in **constant motion**. The rate at which this process occurs depends on several key physical factors.

---

## 1. Key Factors Influencing Diffusion Rate

### The Mass of the Particles
* **Concept:** Lighter particles diffuse faster than heavier particles.
* **Explanation:** At any given temperature, lighter particles have a higher average speed than heavier particles, allowing them to spread out more rapidly.

### The State of the Particles
* **Concept:** The physical state (gas vs. liquid) determines the baseline speed of the particles.
* **Explanation:** Particles in a gas are always moving very fast (high average speed), whereas particles in a liquid travel much more slowly.

### The Temperature of the Particles
* **Concept:** Temperature is a direct measure of the average kinetic energy ($E_k$) of the particles:
  $$E_k \propto T$$
* **Explanation:** The higher the temperature ($T$), the more kinetic energy the particles possess. This causes them to move faster, thereby increasing the rate of diffusion.

### The Size of the Spaces Between Particles
* **Concept:** The availability of empty space determines how easily foreign particles can move through a substance.
* **Explanation:** If there are large spaces between the particles of one substance, the particles of another substance can slip into those spaces with minimal resistance.

---

## 2. Understanding Diffusion: The Crowd Analogy

To understand why particles diffuse at different rates in different states of matter, imagine trying to walk through a densely packed crowd of people:

* **High Density (Liquid-like):** The closer people are to one another, the more often you will bump into someone and be forced to change your direction. It takes a long time to travel from point $A$ to point $B$.
* **Low Density (Gas-like):** If the crowd is sparse and spread out, you can walk long distances in a straight line without colliding with anyone, reaching your destination much faster.

---

## 3. Comparison: Diffusion in Liquids vs. Gases

The table below summarizes the differences in particle behavior and diffusion rates between liquids and gases:

| Property | Liquids | Gases |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Particle Spacing** | Particles are very close together. | Particles are far apart with large empty spaces. |
| **Collision Frequency** | High; particles collide constantly. | Low; collisions occur much less frequently. |
| **Path of Movement** | Short, zigzag paths due to immediate collisions. | Longer straight-line paths before a collision occurs. |
| **Rate of Diffusion** | **Slower** (takes longer for a particle to travel from $A$ to $B$). | **Faster** (particles travel quickly across distances). |

# Matter and Materials: States of Matter and Phase Changes

## Core Concepts: The Three States of Matter

Matter exists in three primary physical states: **solids**, **liquids**, and **gases**. The state
 of a substance depends on the arrangement, spacing, and movement of its constituent particles, as well as the strength of the forces holding them together.

```
   [ Solid ]  ---(Heat/Melting)--->  [ Liquid ]  ---(Heat/Evaporation)--->  [ Gas ]
             <---(Cool/Freezing)---              <---(Cool/Condensation)---
```

### 1. Solids
* **Particle Arrangement:** Particles are closely packed together in a fixed, regular lattice structure.
* **Forces:** Held together by very strong intermolecular forces.
* **Movement:** Particles cannot move from their positions; they can only vibrate about fixed positions.
* **Shape and Volume:** Fixed shape and fixed volume.

### 2. Liquids
* **Particle Arrangement:** Particles are close together but randomly arranged.
* **Forces:** Held by weaker intermolecular forces than solids, allowing movement.
* **Movement:** Particles can slide, roll, and flow past one another.
* **Shape and Volume:** No fixed shape (takes the shape of the container), but has a fixed volume.

### 3. Gases
* **Particle Arrangement:** Particles are very far apart with large empty spaces between them.
* **Forces:** Extremely weak intermolecular forces.
* **Movement:** Particles move rapidly, randomly, and in all directions, colliding with each other and the walls of the container.
* **Shape and Volume:** No fixed shape and no fixed volume (fills the entire container).

---

## Phase Changes and Thermal Energy

Phase changes are physical transitions between states of matter. These changes occur when thermal energy (heat) is either added to or removed from a system.

Let $\Delta Q$ represent the change in thermal energy:
* **Heating ($\Delta Q > 0$):** Adding heat increases the kinetic energy of the particles, causing them to move faster and overcome the intermolecular forces holding them together.
* **Cooling ($\Delta Q < 0$):** Removing heat decreases the kinetic energy of the particles, causing them to slow down and allowing intermolecular forces to pull them back together.

### Summary of Phase Transitions

| Initial State | Final State | Process | Thermal Action Required | Example (Water) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Solid** | **Liquid** | Melting | Heat ($\Delta Q > 0$) | Ice turning into liquid water |
| **Liquid** | **Gas** | Evaporation / Boiling | Heat ($\Delta Q > 0$) | Water turning into steam |
| **Gas** | **Liquid** | Condensation | Cool ($\Delta Q < 0$) | Steam turning into liquid water |
| **Liquid** | **Solid** | Freezing / Solidification | Cool ($\Delta Q < 0$) | Liquid water turning into ice |

# Matter and Materials: Changes of State

---

## 1. Evaporation and the Vapour Phase

When thermal energy is added to a liquid, the particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. 

* **The Process of Evaporation:** Particles near the surface of the liquid acquire enough energy to overcome the intermolecular forces holding them together in the liquid state. Once free, they escape into the gas (or gaseous) state.
* **Vapour:** The gaseous state of a substance that is normally a liquid or solid at room temperature is called **vapour** (e.g., water vapour).
* **Factors Affecting Evaporation:** The rate of evaporation increases as the temperature of the liquid increases. 

### Revision Activity 1
**Question:** Why do we hang wet washing outside in the sunshine to dry?
**Explanation/Answer:** The thermal energy from the sun increases the temperature of the water in the clothes. This increases the kinetic energy of the water particles, allowing them to escape as water vapour much faster than they would at lower temperatures.

---

## 2. Evaporation vs. Boiling

While both evaporation and boiling involve a phase change from liquid to gas, they occur under different conditions:

| Feature | Evaporation | Boiling |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Temperature** | Occurs at any temperature. | Occurs only at a specific temperature called the **boiling point**. |
| **Location** | Occurs only at the surface of the liquid. | Occurs throughout the entire volume of the liquid. |
| **Visual Evidence** | No bubbles are formed. | Bubbles of vapour form throughout the liquid and rise to the surface. |

### Revision Activity 2
1. **Question:** What is the boiling point of water at sea level?
   **Answer:** $100^\circ\text{C}$

2. **Question:** Look carefully at the picture of boiling water. What is inside the bubbles?
   **Answer:** Water vapour (water in its gaseous state), not air.

---

## 3. Condensation and Solidifying

When we cool a substance, we remove thermal energy from its particles.

### Condensation
* **Definition:** **Condensation** is the phase change from a gas to a liquid. It is the exact opposite of evaporation.
* **Mechanism:** As the temperature of a gas decreases, energy is removed from the gas particles. Their movement slows down, and as their kinetic energy decreases, the attractive intermolecular forces pull them closer together to form a liquid.
* **Real-world Example:** Droplets of water forming on the cold outer surface of a glass of ice water. The water vapour in the surrounding air cools down when it contacts the cold glass, losing energy and condensing into liquid water.

---

## Key Vocabulary

* **Condensation:** The process by which a gas cools and changes into a liquid.
* **Solidifying (Freezing):** The process by which a liquid loses energy and changes into a solid.
* **Boiling Point:** The specific temperature at which a liquid changes into a gas throughout the entire volume of the liquid.

# Chapter 2: Particle Model of Matter

## Phase Changes: Condensation and Solidification

When substances lose thermal energy (cool down), their particles undergo phase changes. Two key processes in this cooling cycle are **condensation** and **solidification**.

---

### 1. Condensation (Gas to Liquid)

When a gas is cooled, thermal energy is removed from its particles. 

* **The Process:** As gas particles lose energy, their movement slows down. As they slow down, the attractive forces between them start to take effect, pulling the particles closer together. Eventually, they return to the liquid state.
* **Analogy:** Just like birds, animals, or people huddle together to keep warm when they get cold, gas particles come together and "huddle" into droplets when cooled.
* **Real-World Example:** Water vapour in the air condenses into liquid water droplets when it comes into contact with a cold surface, such as a glass window pane.

---

### 2. Solidification / Freezing (Liquid to Solid)

What happens if we cool a liquid even further?

* **The Process:** By cooling a liquid, we continue to remove thermal energy. As the liquid particles lose energy:
  1. Their movement slows down even more.
  2. The attractive forces between the particles become stronger.
  3. The particles eventually "lock" into fixed positions in a regular lattice structure.
  4. They can no longer move freely and are only able to vibrate in their fixed positions.
* **Result:** The liquid has **solidified** (frozen) into a solid.

> ### Did You Know?
> While most substances contract (shrink in volume) when they solidify, water is a unique exception. The volume of water expands by approximately $9\%$ when it freezes into ice.

---

### Summary of Cooling Phase Changes

| Phase Change | Initial State | Final State | Energy Change | Particle Movement | Particle Spacing & Forces |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Condensation** | Gas | Liquid | Energy is removed (cooling) | Particles slow down | Particles move closer together as attractive forces take effect. |
| **Solidification** | Liquid | Solid | Energy is removed (further cooling) | Particles slow down further until they only vibrate | Particles lock into fixed positions; attractive forces become very strong. |

## Part 1: Investigation Review — Heating and Cooling Candle Wax

This section reviews the observations and conclusions from the investigation on heating and cooling candle wax. Use these questions and explanations to consolidate your understanding of phase changes and the particle model of matter.

### Revision Questions & Explanations

#### 1. What happened when you cooled the candle wax?
* **Observation:** As the liquid candle wax cooled down, it gradually became more viscous (thicker) until it solidified back into a hard, solid state.
* **Particle Model Explanation:** When the liquid wax cools, the particles lose kinetic energy and move more slowly. As their thermal energy decreases, the attractive forces between the particles pull them closer together, locking them back into a fixed, orderly arrangement characteristic of a solid.

#### 2. Would you say the melting point of candle wax is higher or lower than room temperature?
* **Answer:** The melting point of candle wax is **higher** than room temperature.
* **Explanation:** At room temperature (approximately $20^\circ\text{C}$ to $25^\circ\text{C}$), candle wax exists as a solid. For a substance to melt, it must reach its melting point. Since we have to heat the wax significantly above room temperature to make it melt, its melting point must be higher than room temperature.

---

### Conclusion: Particle Model of Matter in Phase Changes

When writing a scientific conclusion for this investigation, you must explain how changes of state occur by referring to the behavior of particles:

$$
\text{Solid (Wax)} \xrightarrow{+\text{ Heat Energy }} \text{Liquid (Wax)} \xrightarrow{-\text{ Heat Energy }} \text{Solid (Wax)}
$$

* **Heating (Melting):** Adding heat energy increases the kinetic energy of the particles in the solid wax. The particles vibrate more vigorously until they overcome the strong attractive forces holding them in fixed positions. They begin to slide past one another, transitioning into a liquid state.
* **Cooling (Solidification/Freezing):** Removing heat energy (cooling) decreases the kinetic energy of the liquid particles. As they slow down, the attractive forces pull them back into a tightly packed, fixed structure, transitioning back into a solid state.

### Scientific Explanation of the Activity

#### What do you observe?
* **During Heating:** The balloon rapidly expands and inflates, even though no air was blown into it.
* **After Heating (Cooling):** If you place the heated balloon into a bowl of ice-cold water, it quickly deflates back to its original size, and droplets of liquid water form on the inside walls of the balloon ("raining" inside the balloon).

#### How does this happen? (The Science)
1. **Phase Change (Liquid to Gas):** The microwave heats the liquid water inside the balloon, causing it to boil and turn into water vapor (steam). 
2. **Gas Expansion:** According to the particle model, gas particles have high kinetic energy and are spaced far apart. The water vapor exerts pressure on the inside walls of the balloon, causing it to expand.
3. **Condensation (Gas to Liquid):** When cooled in ice water, the water vapor particles lose kinetic energy, slow down, and come back together to form liquid water droplets along the inner surface of the balloon.

# 2.4 Density, Mass, and Volume

In Natural Sciences and Mathematics, we frequently work with the physical properties of matter. Three fundamental properties of matter are **mass**, **volume**, and **density**. 

---

### Key Vocabulary

*   **Mass:** A measure of the amount of matter that an object or substance contains.
*   **Volume:** The amount of physical space that an object or substance occupies.
*   **Density:** A measure of how much mass is contained within a given volume.
*   **Physical Quantity:** A property of a material or system that can be quantified by measurement.

---

## 1. Mass: 'How Much' Matter We Have

The **mass** of an object or a substance tells us how much matter it consists of. The greater the mass of an object, the more matter it contains. 

### Key Properties of Mass
*   **Mass is constant:** The mass of an object stays the same no matter where it is in the universe (unless a piece of it is physically removed). For example, a gold bar will have the exact same mass—and the same number of gold atoms—whether it is in Gauteng, Bloemfontein, London, or on the Moon.

### Units of Mass and Conversions
Mass is measured using standard metric units:
*   **Kilograms ($\text{kg}$)** is the standard SI unit for mass.
*   **Grams ($\text{g}$)** are used for smaller objects.
*   **Milligrams ($\text{mg}$)** are used for very small amounts of matter.

#### Conversion Factors:
*   $1\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ g}$
*   $1\text{ g} = 1000\text{ mg}$

---

### Worked Examples & Activities

#### Activity 1: Reading Product Labels
Look at a standard bag of rice (such as the Tastic rice bag shown in the lesson). 
*   **Question:** How much rice is in the bag?
*   **Answer:** The label indicates a mass of $1\text{ kg}$.

---

#### Activity 2: Unit Conversion (Kilograms to Milligrams)
*   **Question:** How many milligrams ($\text{mg}$) are in one kilogram ($\text{kg}$)?
*   **Step-by-Step Solution:**
    1. Convert kilograms to grams:
       $$1\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ g}$$
    2. Convert those grams to milligrams:
       $$1000\text{ g} \times 1000\text{ mg/g} = 1\,000\,000\text{ mg}$$
    3. State final answer:
       $$1\text{ kg} = 10^6\text{ mg} = 1\,000\,000\text{ mg}$$

---

#### Activity 3: Unit Conversion (Grams to Kilograms)
*   **Question:** A gold bar has a mass of $250\text{ g}$. How much is this in kilograms ($\text{kg}$)?
*   **Step-by-Step Solution:**
    1. Identify the conversion factor: $1\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ g}$.
    2. To convert from a smaller unit ($\text{g}$) to a larger unit ($\text{kg}$), divide by $1000$:
       $$\text{Mass in kg} = \frac{250\text{ g}}{1000}$$
       $$\text{Mass in kg} = 0.25\text{ kg}$$

---

## 2. Volume: 'How Much Space' Matter Takes Up

The amount of three-dimensional space that an object occupies is called its **volume**. 

### Calculating and Measuring Volume
*   **Liquids** are typically measured in **litres ($\text{L}$)** or **millilitres ($\text{mL}$)**.
*   **Regular Solids** have their volume calculated by multiplying their three dimensions:
    $$\text{Volume} = \text{length} \times \text{width} \times \text{height}$$

### Defining a Litre ($\text{L}$)
A litre is defined as the volume of space inside a cube that is $10\text{ cm}$ wide, $10\text{ cm}$ long, and $10\text{ cm}$ deep.

$$\text{Volume of Cube} = 10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} = 1000\text{ cm}^3$$

Therefore:
$$1\text{ Litre (L)} = 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1000\text{ mL}$$
$$1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ mL}$$

## 1. Understanding Volume

**Volume** is the amount of three-dimensional space that an object or substance occupies. 

### Key Volume Conversions and Relationships
* When calculating the volume of a cube with side lengths of $1\text{ cm}$:
  $$1\text{ cm} \times 1\text{ cm} \times 1\text{ cm} = 1\text{ cm}^3$$
* $1\text{ cm}^3$ is exactly equal to $1\text{ ml}$ (millilitre).
* For a larger cube with side lengths of $10\text{ cm}$:
  $$10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} = 1000\text{ cm}^3$$
* Since $1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ ml}$, then:
  $$1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1000\text{ ml} = 1\text{ litre (L)}$$

```
       ┌────────────────────────┐
      ╱                        ╱│
     ╱                        ╱ │
    ╱                        ╱  │
   ┌────────────────────────┐   │ 10 cm
   │                        │   │
   │                        │   │
   │                        │   ╱
   │                        │  ╱ 10 cm
   │                        │ ╱
   └────────────────────────┘/
             10 cm
   
   Volume = 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm = 1000 cm³ = 1 Litre
```

### Measuring Units
* **Millilitres ($\text{ml}$):** Used to measure smaller volumes of liquids.
* **Litres ($\text{L}$):** Used for larger volumes. 
  $$1000\text{ ml} = 1\text{ L}$$

## 2. Density: How "Tightly Packed" a Material Is

**Density** is a measure of how much mass of a material fits into a given volume. It describes the ratio of an object's mass to its volume.

### The Density Formula
We can express density mathematically using the following word equation:

$$\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$$

Using standard scientific symbols:
* Let $D$ = Density
* Let $m$ = Mass
* Let $V$ = Volume

$$D = \frac{m}{V}$$

---

### Understanding Density Conceptually

* **Comparing Equal Volumes:** If you have two objects of the exact same size (volume), the one with the **higher mass** (the heavier one) is **more dense**.
* **Everyday Language:** In daily life, we often describe dense objects as feeling "heavy" for their size, while less dense objects feel "light" or "fluffy".
  * *Example:* A dense piece of chocolate cake feels heavy and compact, whereas a sponge cake of the same size feels light because it contains more air pockets (less mass in the same volume).

### Summary of Relationships
* **High Density:** Particles are tightly packed together. High mass per unit volume.
* **Low Density:** Particles are loosely packed or have space between them. Low mass per unit volume.

# Chapter 2: Particle Model of Matter

## Core Concepts: Understanding Density, Mass, and Volume

To understand why different materials feel heavier or lighter even when they are the same size, we must look at the relationship between **mass**, **volume**, and **density**.

### 1. Volume ($V$)
Volume is the amount of three-dimensional space an object occupies. It is typically measured in cubic centimeters ($\text{cm}^3$) or cubic meters ($\text{m}^3$).

### 2. Mass ($m$)
Mass is a measure of the amount of matter in an object. It is typically measured in grams ($\text{g}$) or kilograms ($\text{kg}$).

### 3. Density ($\rho$)
Density is defined as the mass per unit volume of a substance. It describes how tightly packed the particles are within a given space. 

The mathematical formula for density is:

$$\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$$

Or in symbols:

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Where:
*   $\rho$ (Greek letter rho) = Density (measured in $\text{g/cm}^3$ or $\text{kg/m}^3$)
*   $m$ = Mass (measured in $\text{g}$ or $\text{kg}$)
*   $V$ = Volume (measured in $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$)

# 2.5 Density and States of Matter

We have learnt about the three states of matter (solids, liquids, and gases) and the properties of each. One of the key differences between these states is the distance between the particles:

*   **Solids:** Particles are tightly packed together in a fixed, regular pattern.
*   **Liquids:** Particles are close together but can move past one another in a random arrangement.
*   **Gases:** Particles are much further apart with large spaces of empty volume between them.

To understand how these arrangements affect the physical properties of matter, we must look at the concept of **density**.

---

## Core Concepts: Density, Mass, and Volume

**Density** is defined as the amount of mass per unit volume of a substance. It describes how closely packed the particles are within a given space.

The mathematical formula for density is:

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Where:
*   $\rho$ (rho) = Density (measured in $\text{g/cm}^3$ or $\text{kg/m}^3$)
*   $m$ = Mass (measured in $\text{g}$ or $\text{kg}$)
*   $V$ = Volume (measured in $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$)

### Key Relationships:
1.  **Constant Volume ($V$):** If the volume of different containers is kept constant, the container with more particles will have a greater mass ($m$), and therefore a higher density ($\rho$).
2.  **Particle Spacing:** Because particles in a solid are packed closest together, solids generally have the highest density. Gases, with particles spread far apart, have the lowest density.

---

## Activity: Which has the highest density: a solid, a liquid, or a gas?

### Instructions:

```
   [ Container A ]          [ Container B ]          [ Container C ]
   +-----------+            +-----------+            +-----------+
   | O O O O O |            |  O  O  O  |            |     O     |
   | O O O O O |            | O  O  O  O|            |           |
   | O O O O O |            |  O  O  O  |            |  O     O  |
   +-----------+            +-----------+            +-----------+
      (Solid)                  (Liquid)                  (Gas)
```

### Questions and Explanations

#### Question 1
*   **Which container (A, B or C) contains the greatest number of particles?**
*   **Which container contains the smallest number of particles?**

**Answer & Explanation:**
*   **Greatest number of particles:** **Container A** (Solid). The particles in a solid are tightly packed in a fixed, orderly arrangement, allowing more particles to fit into the same volume.
*   **Smallest number of particles:** **Container C** (Gas). The particles in a gas are widely spaced with large empty gaps between them, meaning very few particles fit into the same volume.

---

#### Question 2
*   **Which container (A, B or C) contains the material with the greatest mass?**
*   **Which container has the smallest mass? Why do you say so?**

**Answer & Explanation:**
*   **Greatest mass:** **Container A**. 
*   **Smallest mass:** **Container C**.
*   **Reasoning:** 
    Mass is determined by the total quantity of matter (the number of particles) in an object. Since all three containers contain the same material, each individual particle has the exact same mass. 
    
    *   **Container A** has the highest number of particles, so it has the greatest total mass ($m$).
    *   **Container C** has the fewest particles, so it has the smallest total mass ($m$).

Since the volume ($V$) of all three containers is identical, we can use the density formula:

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

This demonstrates that:

$$\rho_{\text{solid}} > \rho_{\text{liquid}} > \rho_{\text{gas}}$$

Therefore, the solid state (Container A) has the highest density, and the gaseous state (Container C) has the lowest density.

## Density and the States of Matter

We have just performed a conceptual activity (a 'thinking' activity) in which we compared the densities of the three states of the same material.

The high density of a solid material explains why it cannot be compressed. The particles in a solid are tightly packed and cannot be squeezed closer together into a smaller volume.

Liquids are also very dense. The density of a liquid is roughly the same as the density of the solid state of the same substance. This is because their particles are close together, even though they are not locked into fixed positions. Most liquids cannot be compressed into smaller volumes.

# Thermal Expansion and Contraction

## Introduction to Expansion
Materials change size based on temperature fluctuations. When materials are heated, they expand; when they are cooled, they contract. 

*   **Example:** Metal roof panels expand and contract as the outside temperature changes. As they move, they scrape against each other and the nails holding them in place, which creates creaky, groaning noises.

## Engineering for Expansion
Because different solids expand at different rates, engineers must account for these changes when designing structures to prevent damage. If materials are not given space to expand, the internal forces generated can be strong enough to crack or buckle the material.

### Case Study: Concrete Roads
Engineers leave gaps between concrete blocks in roads and paths. 
*   **With gaps:** The concrete has space to expand safely when it gets hot.
*   **Without gaps:** The concrete blocks push against each other, creating immense pressure that causes the road surface to crack.

*   **Air valve:** A mechanism that allows air to enter or leave a container (like a tyre) and controls the flow.

### What is gas pressure?
Gases consist of millions of fast-moving particles. These particles are in constant motion, colliding with each other and with the inner walls of their container.



![Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr8_Particle-model-of-matter.pdf-0044-01.png](images/Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr8_Particle-model-of-matter.pdf-0044-01.png)


*   **Definition:** Gas pressure is the force exerted by gas particles as they bump (collide) against the sides of their container.
*   **Relationship:** The pressure is directly related to the number of collisions. 
    *   More particles in a container = More collisions.
    *   More collisions = Higher pressure.

> **DID YOU KNOW?**
> Wind is simply moving air. The movement of air is caused by differences in pressure between areas of the Earth's atmosphere. Wind blows as the atmosphere attempts to equalise uneven pressures by moving air from a high-pressure area to a low-pressure area.

---

### How can gas pressure be measured?
Gas pressure can be measured using a device called a **pressure gauge**. A common example is the tyre pressure gauge used to check the air pressure inside a vehicle's tyre.

#### How a tyre pressure gauge works:
1.  **Connection:** The round end of the gauge is pressed against the **air valve** of the tyre.
2.  **Mechanism:** This action opens the valve, allowing a small amount of air to escape from the tyre into the gauge.
3.  **Collision:** The escaping air particles collide with a disc inside the gauge.
4.  **Measurement:** The force generated by these collisions pushes a bar out of the back of the gauge. The distance the bar is pushed out corresponds to the pressure inside the tyre, which is read using the numbers marked along the bar.

# The Relationship Between Gas Volume and Pressure

## How Changing Volume Affects Pressure
When a gas is compressed into a smaller volume, the gas particles have less space to move. This results in the particles colliding more frequently with each other and with the inner walls of the container. 

**Key Principle:** Increased frequency of collisions against the container walls results in **increased gas pressure**.

## Expanding Gas into a Larger Volume
If a fixed amount of gas is moved from one container into another container that is twice as large:

1. **Particle Count:** The number of gas particles remains the same.
2. **Space:** The particles now occupy a much larger volume.
3. **Spacing:** There is twice as much space between the molecules compared to the smaller container.

### Concept Check: Density
When gas is moved into a larger container, the density of the gas **decreases**. 

Density is defined by the relationship between mass and volume:
$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Where:
*   $\rho$ (rho) = density
*   $m$ = mass of the gas particles (which remains constant)
*   $V$ = volume of the container (which has increased)

Since the volume ($V$) in the denominator increases while the mass ($m$) stays the same, the overall density ($\rho$) must decrease.

## Key Concepts

### The Particle Model of Matter
All matter consists of extremely small particles called **atoms** or **molecules**. The **particle model of matter** describes how these particles are arranged and behave, helping us understand the macroscopic properties of materials.

#### States of Matter
*   **Solids:**
    *   Closely and regularly packed; locked into position.
    *   Movement is restricted to vibration only.
    *   Held together by strong forces.
    *   Very small spaces between particles.
*   **Liquids:**
    *   Close together but not locked in position.
    *   In constant motion, sliding past each other.
    *   Held together by moderately strong forces.
    *   Small spaces between particles (slightly larger than in solids).
*   **Gases:**
    *   In constant, fast motion.
    *   Not arranged; free to move.
    *   Weak forces between particles.
    *   Far apart with large empty spaces between them.

### Diffusion
Because particles in liquids and gases are in constant motion, they can **diffuse**. Diffusion is the process where particles spread out from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration until they are evenly distributed. This process occurs faster in gases than in liquids because gas particles are further apart.

### Changes of State
Changes of state are typically caused by heating or cooling:
*   **Heating:** Solid $\rightarrow$ Liquid (**Melting**) $\rightarrow$ Gas (**Evaporation**).
*   **Cooling:** Gas $\rightarrow$ Liquid (**Condensation**) $\rightarrow$ Solid (**Freezing**).

### Density
Density is a measure of a material's "relative heaviness." It depends on:
1.  The mass of the individual particles (larger mass = denser).
2.  The size of the spaces between particles (larger spaces = less dense).

#### Density Formula
The relationship between mass and volume is expressed as:
$$\text{Density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}$$

*   **Note on Texture:** Materials with a loose texture (e.g., bread or sponge) contain empty spaces, meaning they have less mass relative to their volume and are therefore less dense.
*   **Floating:** Materials that are less dense will always float on materials that are more dense.

# Revision: The Particle Model of Matter

## 1. The Particle Model of Matter
The particle model of matter states that all matter is composed of tiny, discrete particles (atoms or molecules) that are in constant motion. The state of matter (solid, liquid, or gas) is determined by the amount of kinetic energy these particles possess and the strength of the forces of attraction between them.

## 2. The Anomaly of Water
Water is unusual because, unlike most substances, its solid form (ice) is less dense than its liquid form. 
*   **Explanation:** As water cools toward its freezing point, the molecules arrange themselves into a rigid, hexagonal crystalline lattice structure due to hydrogen bonding. This structure holds the molecules further apart than they are in the liquid state, causing the substance to expand as it freezes.

## 3. Changes of State
The following table outlines the processes involved when matter transitions between states:

| Change of State | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Melting** | When heat is added and a solid changes to a liquid |
| **Condensing** | When heat is removed from a gas, causing it to change into a liquid |
| **Evaporation / Boiling** | When heat is added and the particles at the surface of a liquid change to the gas state |
| **Solidifying (Freezing)** | When heat is removed from a liquid, causing it to change into a solid |

## 4. Particle Behavior During Melting
When heat is added to a solid, the following process occurs at the particle level:
1.  **Increased Kinetic Energy:** The heat energy is transferred to the particles, causing them to vibrate more vigorously in their fixed positions.
2.  **Overcoming Forces:** As the temperature reaches the melting point, the particles gain enough kinetic energy to overcome the strong forces of attraction holding them in a rigid, fixed structure.
3.  **Transition to Liquid:** The particles break free from their fixed positions and begin to slide past one another, allowing the substance to flow and take the shape of its container.

**9. Safety at Petrol Stations**
*   **Question:** Why is it dangerous to light a match or use a cell phone near a petrol station?
*   **Explanation:** Petrol is highly volatile and evaporates easily, creating a mixture of fuel vapour and air. This mixture is highly flammable. A spark from a match or an electrical discharge from a cell phone provides enough activation energy to ignite the vapour, potentially causing an explosion.

**10. Compressibility of Matter**
*   **Question:** Why can air be compressed in a bicycle pump, but water cannot? Use the words 'particles', 'spaces', and 'compress'.
*   **Explanation:**

    *   **Water:** In a liquid, the **particles** are already packed closely together with very little **space** between them. Because there is no room to push the particles closer, water is virtually impossible to **compress**.

### Key Concepts and Explanations

#### 1. Why solids have a fixed shape
Solids maintain a fixed shape because their particles are held in a regular, fixed arrangement by strong intermolecular forces. In contrast, gas particles have no fixed arrangement and very weak forces between them, allowing them to move freely and fill the shape of any container.

#### 2. Compressibility
*   **Gases:** Have very large spaces between particles, allowing them to be "squashed" (compressed) into a smaller volume.
*   **Liquids:** Have very small spaces between particles, making them extremely difficult to compress.

#### 3. The Nature of Powders (e.g., Cake Flour)
A common misconception is that powders are liquids because they can "pour" and take the shape of a container. 
*   **Fact:** Powders are solids made of very fine grains. 
*   **Evidence:** You cannot evaporate a powder, and touching a powder does not make your fingers wet, which distinguishes it from a liquid.

#### 3. The Exception: Water and Ice

# 5.8 Pressure

## Activity: Understanding Gas Pressure
This activity explores how gas particles behave within confined spaces such as bags, balloons, and tyres.

### Observations and Demonstrations
1. **Blowing into a bag:** It is initially easy to blow into an empty bag, but it becomes increasingly difficult as the bag fills with air.
   * **Note:** If a bag is strong, it may not pop. A 'weak' bag may leak at the seams when forced beyond its capacity.
2. **Blowing into a balloon:** It is difficult to start, then becomes easier, and finally becomes difficult again as the balloon reaches its capacity.
   * **Note:** The initial difficulty is due to the tight rubber material. Stretching the balloon beforehand can make it easier to start.
   * **Note:** After one week, a balloon will appear wrinkled and smaller than its original size.
3. **Bottle experiment:**
   * It is impossible to blow up a balloon inside a sealed bottle.
   * The balloon swells up and fills the inside of the bottle (if the setup allows for air displacement).
4. **Tyre pump:** At first, it is easy to pump air into a tyre, but it becomes progressively more difficult as the tyre fills.

---

### Scientific Explanations

#### Why does pressure increase?
* **Particle Density:** When you blow into a container, you are forcing more air particles into that space.
* **Collisions:** As the number of particles increases, the frequency of collisions between the particles and the container walls increases. This increase in collisions results in higher pressure.

#### Why do containers break or pop?
* **Bags:** When a bag reaches its maximum capacity, the force of the particle collisions against the material exceeds the strength of the material, causing it to break.
* **Balloons:** When a balloon is stretched to its maximum size, the force of the particles colliding against the inside surface becomes too great for the polymer strands of the rubber to hold, causing the balloon to pop.
   * **Note:** The polymer strands actually tear apart due to the applied force.

#### Why do balloons shrink over time?
* **Particle Escape:** Air particles can slowly escape through tiny, microscopic holes in the balloon material.
* **Result:** As particles escape, there are fewer particles inside to collide with the walls. This reduction in collisions results in lower internal pressure, causing the balloon to shrink.
   * **Note:** While the air inside may contract slightly, the primary reason for shrinkage is the escape of air molecules through the porous polymer material.

---

### Particle Model of Matter: Explanations and Revision

#### Key Concepts in Gas Behavior
*   **Balloon in a Bottle:** A balloon inside a bottle does not inflate easily because the bottle is already filled with air. The air particles inside the bottle push back against the balloon. If a hole is made in the bottle, the air inside can escape, allowing the balloon to expand into the space.
*   **Tyre Pressure:** Pumping a tyre increases the number of air particles inside. More particles lead to more frequent collisions, resulting in higher pressure. As the tyre stretches to accommodate more particles, it requires more force to stretch the material further.
*   **Enrichment Concepts:** 
    *   Gases expand to fill the volume of their container.
    *   Density decreases as volume increases (assuming mass remains constant).

    *   Particles have specific sizes.
    *   There are empty spaces between particles.
    *   Particles behave differently depending on their state of matter.
2.  **Density of Water:** Water is unique because its solid state (ice) is less dense than its liquid state. This is due to the large spaces between particles in the solid structure of ice.

*   **Melting:** When heat is added, particles gain kinetic energy and vibrate faster. Eventually, they move fast enough to overcome the forces holding them in fixed positions, allowing them to slide past each other (transitioning from solid to liquid).

#### Compressibility of Matter
*   **Gases:** Particles have large spaces between them, making them easy to compress.
*   **Liquids:** Particles are packed closely together with very small spaces between them, making them virtually incompressible.
*   **Example:** A bicycle pump plunger moves easily when filled with air (gas) because the gas is compressed. It does not move much when filled with water (liquid) because the liquid cannot be compressed.

| Feature | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Arrangement of particles** | Very closely packed. Regular arrangement | Close together, but disordered. | Far apart and disordered. |
| **Spaces between particles** | Very small spaces | Small spaces. | Very large |
| **Forces of attraction** | Strong | Strong but weaker than in solids | Very weak |
| **Movement of particles** | Vibration only | Sliding movement, random. | Fast and random movement |
| **Shape** | Fixed shape | No fixed shape. Depends on the container | No fixed shape |
| **Volume** | Fixed volume | Fixed volume | No fixed volume. Depends on the container |
| **Compressibility** | Cannot be compressed | Slight compression is sometimes possible | Very compressible |
| **Diffusion** | Does not diffuse | Diffuses slowly | Diffuses quickly |
| **Density** | Highest density (except ice) | Almost as dense as the solid | Lowest density |
