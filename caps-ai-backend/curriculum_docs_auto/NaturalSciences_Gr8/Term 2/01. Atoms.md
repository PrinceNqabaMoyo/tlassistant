# Chapter 1: Atoms

## What are atoms?

Just as a brick wall is made up of many individual bricks, most forms of matter are made up of many small particles called **atoms**.

### Historical Background
* **Early Greek Philosophers:** Proposed that all matter is made up of incredibly small, discrete units (similar to bricks in a wall).
* **Democritus (460 - 370 BC):** The first to call these units **atomos**. 
  * *Note:* **Atomos** is a Greek phrase meaning 'not cut' or 'that which is indivisible'. 
  * From this phrase came the modern term **atom**.
* **Delay in Acceptance:** It took more than 2000 years for Democritus's ideas to be accepted by scientists, largely because it was difficult to convince people of the existence of particles so small that no one had ever seen them. *(Note: Nowadays, advanced microscopes connected to sophisticated computer software allow scientists to "see" atoms).*

### Scale of Atoms
Atoms are unimaginably small. For example, about $5\,000\,000\,000\,000\,000\,000$ ($5 \times 10^{18}$) of them would fit inside the full stop at the end of a sentence. 

*Note: Atoms can have different sizes.*

---

## What are elements?

Democritus's ideas were largely ignored for over 2000 years until an Englishman named **John Dalton** reintroduced them to the scientific world in **1803**. 

Dalton made five claims about atoms, known as **postulates**. The first three core postulates taught by Dalton regarding elements are:

1. Each element consists of indivisible, minute particles called **atoms**.
2. **All atoms of a given element are identical.**
3. **Atoms of different elements have different masses.**

# 1.2 Sub-atomic particles

The modern model of the atom teaches us that all atoms are made up of **sub-atomic particles** (meaning "smaller than the atom"). 

After many decades of studying atoms, scientists discovered that all atoms are made up of three different kinds of sub-atomic particles:
* **Electrons**
* **Protons**
* **Neutrons**

---

## Core Concepts & Terminology

### 1. Protons
* **Location:** Located deep inside the atom in a central zone called the **nucleus**.
* **Electrical Charge:** Positively charged.
* **Behavior/Forces:** 
  * Like charges repel (meaning the same charges push each other away).
  * Opposite charges attract.
  * When two protons get near each other, they push each other away.
  * When an electron gets near a proton, they attract each other.

### 2. Neutrons
* **Location:** Located together with protons in the tightly packed **nucleus** at the centre of the atom.
* **Electrical Charge:** Neutral (neither positively nor negatively charged).

### 3. Electrons
* **Location:** Move in a zone around the **atomic nucleus** at extremely high speeds, forming an "electron cloud" that is much larger than the nucleus.
* **Size:** The smallest of the three sub-atomic particles (about $2000$ times smaller than protons and neutrons).
* **Electrical Charge:** Negatively charged.

---

## Summary of Sub-Atomic Particles

| Particle | Location in the Atom | Electrical Charge | Relative Size |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Proton** | Nucleus (Centre) | Positive ($+$) | Relatively large |
| **Neutron** | Nucleus (Centre) | Neutral ($0$) | Similar to a proton |
| **Electron** | Electron Cloud (Surrounding the nucleus) | Negative ($-$) | Extremely small (about $\frac{1}{2000}$ of a proton) |

---

## Key Rules of Charge

1. **Like charges repel:** $$(\text{Positive} + \text{Positive}) \text{ or } (\text{Negative} + \text{Negative}) \rightarrow \text{Push away}$$
2. **Opposite charges attract:** $$(\text{Positive} + \text{Negative}) \rightarrow \text{Pull together}$$

# ACTIVITY: Make your own model of an atom

## Core Concepts & Background

### Dalton's Postulates
1. Each element consists of indivisible, minute particles called **atoms**.
2. All atoms of a given element are **identical**.
3. Atoms of different elements have **different masses**.

### Atomic Number and Protons
Each element on the Periodic Table has its own unique type of atom. The atoms of different elements differ because they contain different numbers of **protons**. 
* The **atomic number** of an element is equal to the number of **protons** in an atom of that element.
* In a neutral atom, the number of **electrons** is equal to the number of **protons**.
* In most atoms, the number of **neutrons** in the nucleus is similar to or greater than the number of protons.

> **DID YOU KNOW?**
> The nucleus is extremely dense, meaning protons and neutrons are tightly packed and very heavy relative to their size. If the nucleus were scaled up to the size of a full stop, it would weigh as much as a fully loaded minibus taxi, or $2.5\text{ tonnes}$!

There are two main classes of pure substances:

| **Chemical Formula** | A representation of the elements and their ratios in a chemical compound using symbols. |
| **Chemical Reaction** | A process that involves rearrangement of the molecular or ionic structure of a substance. |
| **Decomposition Reaction** | A type of chemical reaction in which a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. |

# Lesson Revision Notes: Elements and Compounds

## Core Concepts

### 1. Elements
* **Definition:** An element is a pure material that is made up of **atoms of only one kind**.
* **Properties:** Because they consist of identical atoms, any sample of a specific element possesses uniform properties throughout.
* **Examples:**
  * **Copper ($\text{Cu}$):** Consists entirely of copper atoms.
  * **Silver ($\text{Ag}$):** Consists entirely of silver atoms.

---

### 2. Compounds
* **Definition:** A compound is a material made up of **two or more kinds of atoms that are chemically bonded together**.
* **Key Characteristics:**
  * A compound consists of atoms, but there is **more than one kind** present.
  * The different atoms are combined in small clusters (molecules or formula units), and **all of these clusters are exactly the same**.

---

# Lesson: Molecules and Elements

## Core Concepts

* **Molecule:** Two or more atoms that have chemically bonded with each other.
* **Types of Molecules:**
  * **Molecule of an Element:** Consists of atoms of the *same* kind.
  * **Molecule of a Compound:** Consists of atoms of *different* kinds.

## Elements and Structure

* **Metals:** Metals on the left-hand side and middle part of the Periodic Table are solids at room temperature. They exist as tightly packed arrays of individual atoms (e.g., silver and copper). Not all elements have molecules.
* **Non-metals:** Many non-metals on the right-hand side of the Periodic Table are gases at room temperature and exist as molecules.

## Diatomic and Triatomic Molecules

* **Diatomic Molecules:** Molecules made up of **two** atoms each. Examples include:
  * Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$)
  * Nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$)
  * Hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$)
  * Chlorine ($\text{Cl}_2$)
* **Triatomic Molecules:** Molecules made up of **three** of the same atoms bonded together, such as ozone ($\text{O}_3$).

### Core Concepts: Elements vs. Compounds

* **Elements:** Substances that are made up of only one kind of atom. Examples include oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$), hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$), and nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$).
* **Compounds:** Materials that consist of atoms of two or more different elements. These elements are not just physically mixed, but chemically bonded together at the atomic level. Examples include water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$), and sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$, salt).

---

### Understanding Chemical Formulae

Consider the formulas for water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$):

* Both substances consist of hydrogen atoms and oxygen atoms.
* The formula $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ tells us that one molecule of this substance is made up of two atoms of hydrogen and two atoms of oxygen.
* **Are $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ the same substance?** No. 

#### Properties of Hydrogen Peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$)
Although hydrogen peroxide is similar to water in that it is a clear, colourless liquid at room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$), it differs in many ways:
* Hydrogen peroxide has a boiling point of $150^\circ\text{C}$ and is a very effective bleach for clothes and hair.
* Concentrated hydrogen peroxide is so reactive that it is used as a component in rocket fuel!

## Key Concepts & Definitions

* **Corrosive Substances:** Substances that cause damage to metal or other materials through a chemical process (e.g., rainwater causing rust that eats away at metal).

## Separating Substances and Mixtures

* **Question:** Is it possible to separate copper chloride into copper and chlorine using physical separation methods (such as sieving, filtering, evaporation, distillation, or chromatography)?
* **Answer:** **No.** 

None of those methods can break the bonds between atoms in a substance. Copper and chlorine are chemically bonded in copper chloride, as shown by its chemical formula: $\text{CuCl}_2$.

### Key Concepts
* **Physical separation methods:** Can only be used to separate **mixtures** into the substances they are made of. They cannot break chemical bonds.
* **Pure substances:** Always consist of one type of matter throughout. Matter can be an element or a compound.

## 1.4 Mixtures of Elements and Compounds

In Grade 7 Matter and Materials, we learnt that a **mixture** is a combination of two or more materials. Pure substances always consist of one type of matter throughout. We can distinguish between elements and compounds by looking at the different kinds of atoms they are made up of:

* **Elements:** Made up of just one kind of atom.
* **Compounds:** Made up of more than one kind of atom, but always combined in a fixed ratio.

All material can be classified as either a pure substance (just one substance throughout) or a mixture of substances.

### 1. Elements
* **Definition:** An element consists of atoms or molecules where all the atoms are of **only one kind**.
* **Molecules of Elements:** Molecules that consist of two atoms of the same element bonded together are known as **diatomic molecules**.

### 2. Compounds
* **Definition:** A compound consists of two or more different kinds of atoms.
* **Fixed Ratio:** In a given compound, atoms are always bonded together in the **same fixed ratio** (e.g., water as $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ versus hydrogen peroxide as $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$).

### 3. Pure Substances vs. Mixtures
* **Pure Substances (Elements and Compounds):** Consist of the same kinds of particles throughout.
* **Mixtures:** Always contain more than one kind of particle. Examples in the natural world include air, sea water, rocks, and living organisms.

# Summary: Key Concepts

## Atoms
* All matter is made up of tiny particles called atoms.
* The atoms of each element are unique and essentially identical to each other.
* All the known elements are listed on the Periodic Table.

## Sub-atomic particles
* The three main sub-atomic particles that determine the structure of the atom are protons, neutrons and electrons.
* Protons are positively charged and are found in the nucleus, deep in the centre of the atom.
* Neutrons are similar to protons in size and mass, but they do not carry any charge (they are neutral). They are also found in the atomic nucleus.
* Electrons are negatively charged particles, much smaller than protons and neutrons. A cloud of fast-moving electrons surrounds the atomic nucleus.
* In a neutral atom, the number of protons always equals the number of electrons; hence the atom is neutral.

# Concepts and Structure: Matter, Atoms, Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

## 1. Atoms
* **Definition:** Atoms are tiny particles that make up all matter.
* **Structure:** Atoms have sub-atomic particles, namely:
  * Positively charged particles.
  * Negatively charged particles (which move around).
* **Interactions:** Atoms react together in a chemical reaction to form **molecules**.

## 2. Elements
* **Definition:** Elements are pure substances consisting of atoms that are identical.
* **Characteristics:** 
  * Elements are listed on the Periodic Table.
  * The atoms of an element are different from those of another element.
  * Elements are pure substances that **cannot** be chemically broken down into $2$ or more substances.

## 3. Compounds
* **Definition:** Compounds are pure substances made of molecules.
* **Characteristics:**
  * Compounds have atoms that are **chemically bonded** together.
  * Atoms in a compound are combined in a **fixed ratio**.
  * Compounds **can** be chemically broken down into other substances.

## 4. Mixtures
* **Definition:** Mixtures consist of elements and/or compounds (such as air, water, and rocks) combined together.
* **Characteristics:**
  * Mixtures can exist in living things and the environment.
  * Mixtures can be separated into their components by **physical methods**.

# Chapter 1: Atoms, Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

## Core Concepts Revision

To answer questions about the classification of matter, we must understand how particles are arranged and combined.

### 1. Elements
An **element** is a pure substance consisting of only one type of atom. 
* **Monatomic elements:** Exist as individual, unbonded atoms (e.g., Helium, $\text{He}$).
* **Diatomic molecules of an element:** Exist as pairs of the same atoms chemically bonded together (e.g., Oxygen, $\text{O}_2$; Hydrogen, $\text{H}_2$).

### 2. Compounds
A **compound** is a pure substance formed when two or more different types of atoms are chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio (e.g., Water, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$; Carbon Dioxide, $\text{CO}_2$). 
* The particles of a compound are identical molecules or formula units.

### 3. Mixtures
A **mixture** consists of two or more different substances (elements or compounds) that are physically intermingled but **not** chemically bonded together. 
* The composition of a mixture can vary, and the individual components retain their own chemical properties.

### 4. Pure Substances
A **pure substance** consists of only one type of particle. This category includes:
* All pure elements (whether monatomic or diatomic).
* All pure compounds.

### Part A: Particle Diagram Analysis
*Note: Refer to your textbook's diagram blocks to identify the specific letters/numbers for each question below.*

#### a) Which blocks represent the particles of an element?
* **How to identify:** Look for blocks that contain only one type of atom (represented by spheres of the same color and size), either as single atoms or bonded identical pairs.

#### b) Which block represents the particles of a compound?
* **How to identify:** Look for a block where every particle is identical, but each particle is made of two or more different types of atoms bonded together.

#### c) Which block represents the particles of a mixture?
* **How to identify:** Look for a block containing a variety of different, unbonded particles (e.g., a mix of single atoms, diatomic elements, or compounds).

#### d) Which block represents the particles of a pure substance?
* **How to identify:** Look for any block that contains only one type of particle throughout (this includes blocks containing only a pure element or only a pure compound).

#### e) Which block represents diatomic molecules of an element?
* **How to identify:** Look for a block containing pairs of identical spheres bonded together.

# Chapter 4: Atoms

## Chapter Overview
* **Duration:** 2 weeks

This chapter introduces the fundamental building blocks of matter and the essential classification schemes used by scientists to describe and communicate properties of matter. 

### Key Conceptual Challenges
* **Terminology Confusion:** Learners often confuse key terms such as **atom** versus **molecule**, and **element** versus **compound**. Clear definitions and repetitive reinforcement are critical to overcoming these difficulties.
* **The Sub-Microscopic Scale:** Atoms and molecules are far too small to be seen even with the most powerful light microscopes. Scientists use the term **sub-microscopic** to describe diagrams and models representing entities at this atomic scale. Developing the ability to visualize and interpret chemical events at the sub-microscopic level is a foundational skill in chemistry.

To help learners develop these visualization skills, they should practice drawing and constructing physical models of molecules using play-dough or plasticine.

## Practical Activity: Making Play-Dough for Molecular Modeling

Using physical models helps learners transition from abstract concepts to concrete understanding. Below is a simple, cost-effective recipe to make modeling dough in the classroom.

### Ingredients
* $1$ cup flour
* $2$ cups warm water
* $1$ cup salt
* $2$ tablespoons vegetable oil
* $1$ tablespoon cream of tartar (optional, used to improve elasticity)
* Food colouring (various colours to represent different types of atoms)

### Method
1. **Mix and Heat:** Combine all ingredients in a pan and stir continuously over low heat. The mixture will begin to thicken until it resembles the consistency of mashed potatoes.
2. **Cool:** Once the dough pulls away from the sides of the pan and clumps in the centre, remove the pan from the heat. Allow the dough to cool until it is safe to handle. 
   * *Note:* If the dough remains sticky, it needs to be cooked for slightly longer.
3. **Knead:** Turn the cooled dough out onto a clean surface and knead it vigorously until it becomes smooth. Divide the dough into several smaller balls.
4. **Colour:** Create a small depression in the centre of each ball and add a few drops of food colouring. Work the colour thoroughly through the dough. Add more food colouring to increase the intensity of the colour as desired. Assign different colours to represent different elements (e.g., red for oxygen, white for hydrogen, black for carbon).

# Atoms and the Conservation of Mass

* **Conservation of Mass:** Atoms cannot be created or destroyed during a **chemical reaction**. The total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
* **Splitting Atoms:** Although the word "atom" is derived from the Greek word meaning *indivisible*, atoms are not truly indivisible. They can be "smashed" apart or split into smaller sub-atomic parts when extreme kinetic energy is present (such as in an atomic bomb).

# Conceptual Discussion: Globes vs. Flat Maps

To better understand spatial relationships and models, consider the differences between a flat map of the Earth and a spherical globe:

* **Realistic Positioning:** A globe shows the positions of the continents relative to each other much more realistically than any flat map can.
* **Size Accuracy:** A globe is far more accurate than a flat map in terms of the relative sizes of different countries.
* **Earth's Rotation:** A globe helps us visualize the movement of the Earth—how it spins on its own axis to create day and night.
* **Seasons:** A globe demonstrates how the Earth tilts and moves around the Sun to create the seasons.
* **Coordinates:** We can easily locate and learn about lines of latitude and longitude using a globe.

### Organizing the Periodic Table

When looking at the Periodic Table, it is vital to understand how elements are ordered:
* Elements are arranged in order of increasing **atomic number** (the number of protons in the nucleus).
* Elements are **NOT** arranged strictly by increasing **atomic mass number** (the total number of protons and neutrons), even though the general trend is similar.

#### Exceptions to the Mass Trend:
In some cases, an element with a higher atomic number actually has a lower atomic mass than the element preceding it. Examples of these exceptions include:
* **Argon** ($\text{Ar}$) and **Potassium** ($\text{K}$)
* **Cobalt** ($\text{Co}$) and **Nickel** ($\text{Ni}$)
* **Tellurium** ($\text{Te}$) and **Iodine** ($\text{I}$)

# Topic: Atomic Structure and Dalton's Atomic Theory

---

## 1. Dalton's Three Postulates

John Dalton's atomic theory laid the foundation for modern chemistry. His three core postulates are:

1. **Indivisible Particles:** Each element consists of indivisible, minute particles called **atoms**.
2. **Identical Atoms:** All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties.
3. **Different Masses:** Atoms of different elements have different masses.

> *Note:* Modern chemistry has updated these postulates (for example, we now know that **isotopes** are atoms of the same element that have different numbers of neutrons, meaning not all atoms of an element are completely identical in mass).

---

## 2. Atomic Number and Elements

Each element on the Periodic Table has its own unique type of atom. Atoms of different elements differ because they contain different numbers of protons.

$$\text{Atomic Number} = \text{Number of Protons in the Nucleus}$$

### Examples:
* An atom with $7$ protons has an atomic number of $7$ (Nitrogen, $\text{N}$).
* An atom with $16$ protons has an atomic number of $16$ (Sulfur, $\text{S}$).

---

# Decomposition of Compounds: Electrolysis of Copper(II) Chloride

This section explores how a chemical compound can be broken down into its constituent elements using electrical energy (a process known as **electrolysis**).

---

## 1. Experimental Setup and Materials

To set up the electrical circuit for this investigation, you will need:
*   **Connecting wires** with crocodile clips at both ends (ideal for establishing secure electrical connections).
*   A **power source** (such as batteries or a low-voltage power pack).
*   An optional **switch** to control the flow of current.
*   **Electrodes** (typically carbon/graphite rods).
*   **Copper(II) chloride solution**: This can be prepared by dissolving two teaspoons of copper(II) chloride ($\text{CuCl}_2$) powder in a cup of tap water.

---

## 2. Key Observations

During the electrolysis of copper(II) chloride, the following observations can be made:

1.  **Initial color of the solution:** Blue.
2.  **Initial appearance of the electrodes:** Dark grey or black.
3.  **Reaction during the process:** One electrode becomes covered in small bubbles, while the other electrode turns brown.
4.  **Final color of the solution:** The solution remains blue, but its intensity decreases (it becomes a lighter blue).

---

## 3. Understanding the Observations

If you compare the copper(II) chloride solution before and after the experiment, you will notice that the "after" solution is not as deep blue as the "before" solution. This change is due to two main chemical processes:

### Deposit on the Cathode (Negative Electrode)
*   The blue color of the solution is due to the presence of copper ions ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$).
*   As the reaction takes place, these copper ions are removed from the solution. They accept electrons at the electrode and become solid copper metal ($\text{Cu}$), which precipitates as a reddish-brown layer.
*   *Note for learners:* Even if you have not yet studied ions or electron sharing, you can think of this as copper particles coming out of the solution and accumulating on the surface of the electrode. This deposit has the characteristic reddish-brown color of a copper coin.

### Gas at the Anode (Positive Electrode)
*   Chloride ions ($\text{Cl}^-$) in the solution travel to the other electrode, where they form chlorine gas ($\text{Cl}_2$).
*   This is observed as bubbles forming on the electrode.
*   If you carefully smell this electrode, you may notice a distinct "bleach-like" odor, which is characteristic of chlorine gas ($\text{Cl}_2$).

### Conclusion
Because copper ions and chloride ions are being removed from the liquid to form solid copper and chlorine gas, the concentration of the copper(II) chloride solution decreases, causing the blue color to fade.

---

## 4. Advanced Explanation: The Ionic Level (Extension)

While the primary focus at this level is demonstrating that a compound can be broken down into simpler elements, the underlying electrochemical processes can be explained as follows:

### The Anode (Positive Electrode)
*   The electrode connected to the positive terminal of the power supply becomes positively charged. This is called the **anode**.
*   Negatively charged chloride ions ($\text{Cl}^-$) in the solution are attracted to the positive anode.
*   At the anode, chloride ions give up electrons (oxidation) to form chlorine gas ($\text{Cl}_2$), which is released as bubbles:
    $$2\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{e}^-$$

### The Cathode (Negative Electrode)
*   The electrode connected to the negative terminal of the power supply becomes negatively charged. This is called the **cathode**.
*   Positively charged copper ions ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$) in the solution are attracted to the negative cathode.
*   At the cathode, copper ions gain electrons (reduction) to form solid copper metal ($\text{Cu}$), which deposits as a brown coating:
    $$\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu(s)}$$
