## Key Concepts Overview

### 1. Properties of Light
Light exhibits both wave-like and particle-like properties. In geometrical optics, we primarily treat light as traveling in straight lines.

### 2. Light Rays
A light ray is a line representing the path taken by light. A beam of light is a collection of many light rays.

### 3. The Speed of Light
The speed of light in a vacuum ($c$) is a universal constant:
$$c \approx 3 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

### 4. Refraction
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium into another of a different optical density. This occurs because the speed of light changes between media.

### 5. Snell's Law
Snell's Law describes the relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction when light passes between two media:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$
Where:
*   $n_1, n_2$ are the refractive indices of the two media.
*   $\theta_1$ is the angle of incidence.
*   $\theta_2$ is the angle of refraction.

### 6. Critical Angle and Total Internal Reflection
When light travels from a denser medium to a less dense medium, it may undergo total internal reflection if the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle ($\theta_c$). The critical angle is defined as:
$$\sin \theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$
(where $n_1 > n_2$)

## 5.1 Summary of Properties of Light

### Introduction
Light interacts with surfaces through reflection and refraction. These processes are essential for everyday phenomena such as seeing your reflection in a mirror, using telescopes, and long-distance communication.

### Light Rays
While light does not consist of distinct, individual lines, we use the model of **light rays** to represent the path of light.
*   **Definition:** A light ray is a narrow, imaginary line representing the direction in which light travels.
*   **Wave-Particle Duality:** Light behaves like a wave; a light ray can be thought of as the path of a point on the crest of a wave.

### Optics
*   **Definition:** The study of how light interacts with materials (such as mirrors, lenses, telescopes, microscopes, and prisms) is called **optics**.
*   **Geometrical Optics:** This is the specific method of using light rays and angles to determine properties such as the distance between an object and its reflection.

**DEFINITION: Light ray**

We can only see an object if light enters our eyes. This light must originate from one of two sources:
*   **Luminous objects:** Objects that produce their own light (e.g., a lightbulb).

## Terminology of Reflection

To describe the reflection of light, we use specific terminology based on the interaction between light rays and a surface.

### Key Definitions

*   **Incident ray:** The incoming light ray moving towards the surface.
*   **Reflected ray:** The light ray moving away from the surface.
*   **Normal:** An imaginary line drawn perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the surface at the point of incidence.
*   **Angle of incidence ($\theta_i$):** The angle measured between the incident ray and the normal.
*   **Angle of reflection ($\theta_r$):** The angle measured between the reflected ray and the normal.
*   **Plane of incidence:** The plane in which the incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal all lie.

## Summary of Geometric Relationships

1.  All angles ($\theta_i$ and $\theta_r$) are measured with respect to the **normal**, not the surface itself.
2.  The incident ray, reflected ray, and the normal all exist within the same **plane of incidence**.

### 5.3 Properties of Light: Reflection

#### The Plane of Incidence
The plane of incidence is defined as the plane that includes both the incident ray and the normal to the surface. The reflected ray also lies within this same plane.



![Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_2D-and-3D-wavefronts.pdf-0005-05.png](images/Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_2D-and-3D-wavefronts.pdf-0005-05.png)

#### Definition: Law of Reflection
The law of reflection states that:
1. The angle of incidence ($\theta_i$) is equal to the angle of reflection ($\theta_r$):
   $$\theta_i = \theta_r$$
2. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal all lie in the same plane.

---

#### Special Case: Normal Incidence
The simplest application of the law of reflection occurs when the angle of incidence is $0^\circ$. In this scenario, the light ray strikes the surface at a right angle, and the angle of reflection is also $0^\circ$. Consequently, the light is reflected directly back along its original path.

### Real World Applications: Parabolic Reflectors

A **parabolic reflector** is a mirror or dish with a parabolic shape. It is designed to manipulate light rays in specific ways based on the placement of the light source or the direction of incoming light.

#### 1. Car Headlights and Spotlights
*   **Function:** These use a parabolic mirror to create a **collimated beam**.
*   **Mechanism:** A light bulb is placed at the focus of the parabola. The light rays emitted by the bulb hit the parabolic mirror and are reflected outward as parallel rays.

#### 2. Telescopes
*   **Function:** These use a parabolic mirror to collect light from distant objects.
*   **Mechanism:** Incoming light from distant objects (like stars or galaxies) arrives as parallel rays. The parabolic mirror reflects these rays and focuses them to a single point, called the **focus**, where an image can be captured by a detector or camera.

### Absorption

In addition to reflection, light can be **absorbed**. Visible light covers a range of wavelengths in the electromagnetic spectrum, where different colours correspond to different wavelengths or frequencies.

#### How we perceive colour:
* **White objects:** Reflect all or most of the wavelengths of light falling on them.
* **Coloured objects:** Reflect specific wavelengths of light and absorb the rest. For example, a red apple reflects only red light into our eyes and absorbs the other wavelengths of the incident white light.
* **Black objects:** Absorb all the light falling on them.

**Note on Heat:** When an object absorbs light, it absorbs the energy from that light, which causes the object to heat up. This is why wearing a white t-shirt in the sun is cooler than wearing a black t-shirt; the white t-shirt reflects most of the light, while the black t-shirt absorbs it.

### 5.4 The Speed of Light

#### Key Concepts
*   **Constant Speed:** In physics, light travels at a constant speed within a specific medium.
*   **Maximum Speed:** Light has a maximum speed at which it can propagate; nothing can travel faster than this.
*   **Vacuum:** A region with no matter (not even air). Light travels at its absolute maximum speed in a vacuum.
*   **Speed in Air:** The speed of light in air is very close to its speed in a vacuum.

#### Definition: Speed of Light
The speed of light, denoted by the symbol $c$, is constant in a given medium. Its maximum speed in a vacuum is:
$$c = 3 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

### 5.5 Refraction

#### Definition: Refraction
Refraction is the change in direction of a light ray when it passes from one medium into another at an angle that is not perpendicular or parallel to the boundary.

#### Key Principles
*   **Dependence on Medium:** The speed of light depends on the medium through which it is travelling.
*   **Change in Speed:** When light moves from one medium to another (e.g., from air to glass), its speed changes.
*   **Change in Direction:** If the light ray hits the boundary of a new medium at an angle, the change in speed causes the light ray to change its direction (it appears to "bend").
*   **Frequency:** It is important to note that while the speed of light changes during refraction, the **frequency** of the light remains constant.

### Refraction

**Definition: Refraction**
Refraction occurs at the boundary of two media when light travels from one medium into the other. During this process, the speed of the light changes, but its frequency remains the same. If the light ray hits the boundary at an angle that is not perpendicular or parallel to the surface, it will change direction and appear to 'bend'.

#### Practical Example: The Straw in Water
Refraction is demonstrated when looking at a straw in a glass of water from an angle. The straw appears bent because the light rays leaving the straw change direction when they hit the surface between the liquid and the air. An observer's eyes trace these light rays backwards as straight lines, making the tip of the straw appear shallower in the liquid than it actually is.

### Refractive Index

The speed of light, and consequently the degree of bending of the light, depends on the refractive index of the material through which the light passes.

**Definition: Refractive index**
The refractive index (symbol $n$) of a material is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to its speed in the material. It provides an indication of how difficult it is for light to travel through that material.

#### Formula
The refractive index is calculated using the following equation:

$$n = \frac{c}{v}$$

**Where:**
*   $n$ = refractive index (a dimensionless quantity with no units)
*   $c$ = speed of light in a vacuum ($3,00 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$)
*   $v$ = speed of light in a given medium ($\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$)

### Refractive Index

The refractive index ($n$) of a material describes how light propagates through that medium. It is defined by the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum ($c$) to the speed of light in the material ($v$):

$$n = \frac{c}{v}$$

#### Key Concepts
*   **Constant Speed:** The speed of light in a vacuum, $c$, is a constant.
*   **Inverse Relationship:** Because $c$ is constant, if the refractive index ($n$) increases, the speed of light in the material ($v$) must decrease.
*   **Optical Density:** Light travels slower through materials with a higher refractive index.
*   **General Rule:** Since light travels slower in any material than in a vacuum, all values for $n$ are greater than $1$.

### Calculating the Speed of Light in a Medium

To calculate the speed of light in a specific medium when the refractive index ($n$) is known, follow these steps:

**1. Identify the knowns:**
*   The speed of light in a vacuum ($c$) is a constant: $c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.
*   The refractive index ($n$) of the medium (e.g., glycerine).

**2. Use the refractive index formula:**
The definition of the refractive index is:
$$n = \frac{c}{v}$$

**3. Rearrange and solve:**
To find the speed of light in the medium ($v$), rearrange the formula:
$$v = \frac{c}{n}$$

**Worked Example: Glycerine**
Given the refractive index of glycerine is $1,4729$:
$$v = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{1,4729}$$
$$v = 2,04 \times 10^8 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

### Refraction of Light

Refraction is the change in direction of a light ray when it passes from one transparent medium into another of a different optical density.

#### Key Concepts
*   **Optically dense medium:** A medium with a higher refractive index ($n$), where light travels slower.
*   **Optically less dense medium:** A medium with a lower refractive index ($n$), where light travels faster.
*   **Normal:** An imaginary line perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence.
*   **$\theta_1$:** Angle of incidence.
*   **$\theta_2$:** Angle of refraction.

#### Behaviour of Light Rays

1.  **From Optically Dense to Less Dense:**
    When light moves from a medium with a higher refractive index (e.g., water, $n=1.3$) to a medium with a lower refractive index (e.g., air, $n=1.0$), the light ray bends **away** from the normal.

### Key Concepts & Definitions

*   **Refraction:** The change in direction of a wave passing from one medium to another caused by its change in speed.
*   **Angle of Incidence:** The angle between the incident ray and the normal at the point of incidence.
*   **Angle of Refraction:** The angle between the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence.
*   **Normal:** An imaginary line perpendicular to the surface at the point where the light ray strikes the interface.
*   **Refractive Index ($n$):** A measure of how much the speed of light is reduced inside a medium compared to its speed in a vacuum. It is defined as:
    $$n = \frac{c}{v}$$
    Where:
    *   $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum ($3 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$)
    *   $v$ is the speed of light in the medium.

### 5.6 Snell's Law

The degree of bending (refraction) of light depends on the refractive index of the media involved. This relationship is defined by **Snell's Law** (also known as the Law of Refraction).

#### Definition: Snell's Law
$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$

**Where:**
*   $n_1$ = Refractive index of material 1
*   $n_2$ = Refractive index of material 2
*   $\theta_1$ = Angle of incidence
*   $\theta_2$ = Angle of refraction

### 5.6 Snell's Law

#### Key Concepts
*   **The Normal:** An imaginary line drawn perpendicular to the surface at the point where the light ray strikes the boundary between two media.
*   **Angle of Incidence ($\theta_1$):** The angle between the incident ray and the normal.

#### Theoretical Background
Snell's Law is defined by the equation:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$

Where:
*   $n_1$: Refractive index of the first medium (for air, $n_1 = 1,0$).
*   $\theta_1$: Angle of incidence.

    $$n_2 = \frac{n_1 \sin \theta_1}{\sin \theta_2}$$

### Principles of Light Bending

The relationship between the refractive indices determines how light bends relative to the normal.

#### Case 1: Light entering a denser medium ($n_2 > n_1$)
If $n_2 > n_1$, then according to Snell's Law:
$$\sin \theta_1 > \sin \theta_2$$
For angles smaller than $90^\circ$, $\sin \theta$ increases as $\theta$ increases. Therefore:
$$\theta_1 > \theta_2$$
**Conclusion:** The angle of incidence is greater than the angle of refraction, and the light ray is bent **toward** the normal.

#### Case 2: Light entering a less dense medium ($n_2 < n_1$)
If $n_2 < n_1$, then according to Snell's Law:
$$\sin \theta_1 < \sin \theta_2$$
For angles smaller than $90^\circ$, $\sin \theta$ increases as $\theta$ increases. Therefore:
$$\theta_1 < \theta_2$$
**Conclusion:** The angle of incidence is smaller than the angle of refraction, and the light ray is bent **away from** the normal.

### Refraction of Light

Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium into another of a different optical density. The direction of this bending depends on the refractive indices of the two media.

#### 1. Light moving from a higher refractive index to a lower refractive index
When light travels from a medium with a higher refractive index (e.g., water) to one with a lower refractive index (e.g., air), the light ray is bent **away from the normal**. In this scenario, the angle of incidence is less than the angle of refraction.

### Refraction at an Angle of Incidence of $0^\circ$

When a light ray lies exactly along the normal line, the angle of incidence is $0^\circ$. Using Snell's Law, we can calculate the angle of refraction ($\theta_2$):

$$\sin \theta_2 = \frac{n_1}{n_2} \sin \theta_1$$

Substituting $\theta_1 = 0^\circ$:

$$\sin \theta_2 = \frac{n_1}{n_2} \sin(0^\circ)$$
$$\sin \theta_2 = 0$$
$$\therefore \theta_2 = 0^\circ$$

**Conclusion:**
If a light ray is incident at $0^\circ$, the angle of refraction is also $0^\circ$. The direction of the light ray remains unchanged; however, the speed of the light changes as it enters the new medium. Therefore, refraction still occurs, even though it is not easily observed.

### Understanding Refraction: The Lawn-Mower Analogy

Refraction is the change in direction of a wave passing from one medium to another caused by its change in speed. We can visualize this using the analogy of a lawnmower moving across different grass lengths:

*   **Moving into longer grass:** When one side of the mower enters longer grass, those wheels slow down because of increased resistance. The wheels still in the shorter grass continue at a higher speed, causing the mower to turn **inwards** (towards the normal).
*   **Moving into shorter grass:** When the mower moves from long grass to short grass, the side in the short grass speeds up, causing the mower to turn **outwards** (away from the normal).

This is physically equivalent to light entering a medium with a higher refractive index (slower speed) or a lower refractive index (higher speed).

### Worked Example: Using Snell's Law

#### Question
Light is refracted at the boundary between water and an unknown medium. If the angle of incidence is $25^{\circ}$ and the angle of refraction is $20,6^{\circ}$, calculate the refractive index of the unknown medium.

#### Solution

**Step 1: Identify given information and the unknown**
*   Angle of incidence: $\theta_1 = 25^{\circ}$
*   Angle of refraction: $\theta_2 = 20,6^{\circ}$
*   Refractive index of water: $n_1 = 1,333$
*   Unknown: Refractive index of the second medium ($n_2$)

**Step 2: Determine the approach**
To solve this, we use Snell's Law:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$

Rearranging the formula to solve for $n_2$:
$$n_2 = \frac{n_1 \sin \theta_1}{\sin \theta_2}$$

**Step 3: Calculation**
Substitute the known values into the equation:
$$n_2 = \frac{1,333 \times \sin(25^{\circ})}{\sin(20,6^{\circ})}$$

Using a calculator:
*   $\sin(25^{\circ}) \approx 0,4226$
*   $\sin(20,6^{\circ}) \approx 0,3518$

$$n_2 = \frac{1,333 \times 0,4226}{0,3518}$$
$$n_2 \approx 1,601$$

**Conclusion:**
The refractive index of the unknown medium is approximately $1,60$. (This value would then be compared against a standard table of refractive indices to identify the material).

### Calculating Refractive Index ($n_2$)

To calculate an unknown refractive index, we use Snell's Law:

$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$

Rearranging the formula to solve for $n_2$:

$$n_2 = \frac{n_1 \sin \theta_1}{\sin \theta_2}$$

**Example Calculation:**
Given $n_1 = 1,333$, $\theta_1 = 25^\circ$, and $\theta_2 = 20,6^\circ$:

$$n_2 = \frac{1,333 \sin 25^\circ}{\sin 20,6^\circ}$$
$$n_2 = 1,6$$

**Step 3: Identify the unknown medium**
By comparing the calculated refractive index to standard values (e.g., Table 5.1), we can identify the material. A refractive index between $1,5$ and $1,9$ indicates the medium is typical glass.

# Snell's Law: Refraction of Light

## Core Concept: Snell's Law
Snell's Law describes the relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction when light passes through the boundary between two different isotropic media. It is expressed by the formula:

$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$

Where:
*   $n_1$: Refractive index of the first medium.
*   $\theta_1$: Angle of incidence (the angle between the incident ray and the normal).
*   $n_2$: Refractive index of the second medium.
*   $\theta_2$: Angle of refraction (the angle between the refracted ray and the normal).

---

## Worked Example: Using Snell's Law

**Question:** A light ray passes from water to diamond with an angle of incidence of $75^\circ$. Calculate the angle of refraction and discuss the meaning of the result.

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Identify the refractive indices**
*   Refractive index of water ($n_1$) = $1,33$
*   Refractive index of diamond ($n_2$) = $2,42$

**Step 2: Substitute values into Snell's Law and solve**
$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$
$$1,33 \sin 75^\circ = 2,42 \sin \theta_2$$
$$\sin \theta_2 = \frac{1,33 \sin 75^\circ}{2,42}$$
$$\sin \theta_2 \approx 0,531$$
$$\theta_2 = \arcsin(0,531) \approx 32,1^\circ$$

**Step 3: Discussion**
Because the light ray passes from a medium of lower refractive index ($1,33$) to a medium of higher refractive index ($2,42$), the light ray is bent **towards the normal**.

### 5.7. Critical Angles and Total Internal Reflection

#### Total Internal Reflection
Total internal reflection occurs when an incident light ray, travelling through an optically denser medium, is reflected back at the boundary between that medium and another of lower optical density, instead of passing through and being refracted.



![Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_Electromagnetism.pdf-0029-00.png](images/Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_Electromagnetism.pdf-0029-00.png)

#### The Critical Angle
As the angle of incidence increases, a point is reached where the angle of refraction is $90^\circ$. At this point, the refracted ray travels exactly along the boundary of the two media. This specific angle of incidence is known as the **critical angle** ($\theta_c$).

**DEFINITION: Critical angle**
The critical angle is the angle of incidence where the angle of refraction is $90^\circ$. For this to occur, the light must travel from an optically more dense medium to an optically less dense medium.



![Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_Electromagnetism.pdf-0029-02.png](images/Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_Electromagnetism.pdf-0029-02.png)

*Figure 5.15: When the angle of incidence is equal to the critical angle, the angle of refraction is equal to $90^\circ$.*

#### Conditions for Total Internal Reflection
If the angle of incidence is increased beyond the critical angle, the light ray will no longer emerge from the medium. Instead, it is reflected back into the original medium. This phenomenon is called **total internal reflection**.

For total internal reflection to occur, the following two conditions must be met:

1.  Light must be travelling from an optically denser medium (higher refractive index) to an optically less dense medium (lower refractive index).
2.  The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle ($\theta_i > \theta_c$).

### Calculating the Critical Angle

Instead of measuring the critical angles of different materials experimentally, we can calculate the critical angle at the surface between two media using Snell's Law.

#### 1. Recap of Snell's Law
Snell's Law is defined as:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$

Where:
*   $n_1$: Refractive index of material 1.
*   $n_2$: Refractive index of material 2.
*   $\theta_1$: Angle of incidence.
*   $\theta_2$: Angle of refraction.

#### 2. Deriving the Critical Angle Formula
For total internal reflection to occur, the angle of incidence must equal the critical angle ($\theta_c$):
$$\theta_1 = \theta_c$$

At the critical angle, the angle of refraction is always $90^\circ$:
$$\theta_2 = 90^\circ$$

Substituting these into Snell's Law:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_c = n_2 \sin 90^\circ$$

Since $\sin 90^\circ = 1$, the equation simplifies to:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_c = n_2$$

Solving for $\theta_c$:
$$\sin \theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$
$$\therefore \theta_c = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{n_2}{n_1} \right)$$

> **TIP:** Remember that for total internal reflection, the incident ray is always in the denser medium.

### Calculating the Critical Angle

The critical angle ($\theta_c$) is the angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction is $90^\circ$. When light travels from a medium with a higher refractive index ($n_1$) to a medium with a lower refractive index ($n_2$), we use Snell's Law:

$$n_1 \sin \theta_c = n_2 \sin 90^\circ$$

Since $\sin 90^\circ = 1$, the formula simplifies to:

$$\theta_c = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{n_2}{n_1} \right)$$

#### Worked Example: Water to Air
For light travelling from water ($n_1 = 1,33$) to air ($n_2 = 1$):

1. **Set up the equation:**
   $$\theta_c = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{1}{1,33} \right)$$

2. **Calculate:**
   $$\theta_c = 48,8^\circ$$

**Final Answer:** The critical angle for light travelling from water to air is $48,8^\circ$.

### Fibre Optics

Total internal reflection is a fundamental principle used to confine and transmit light in **fibre optics**.

#### Structure of an Optical Fibre
An optical fibre consists of two primary components:
1.  **Inner Core:** The central part through which light pulses travel.
2.  **Outer Cladding:** The protective layer surrounding the core.

#### Specifications
*   **Core Diameter:** Approximately $50\ \mu\text{m}$ ($50 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$).
*   **Overall Fibre Diameter:** Approximately $125\ \mu\text{m}$ ($125 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$).

#### How it Works
The difference in the refractive index between the cladding and the core ensures that light remains trapped inside the glass strand. If light is incident on the cable end at an angle greater than the critical angle, it undergoes total internal reflection, allowing the light to travel rapidly down the length of the cable.

#### Fibre Optics in Telecommunications
Optical fibres are widely used in telecommunications because they allow data to be transported over long distances with minimal signal loss.

*   **Capacity:** A single strand can handle over $3000$ transmissions simultaneously by sending pulses at slightly different angles (e.g., $72,23^{\circ}$ and $72,26^{\circ}$).

*   **Signal Maintenance:** Because signals weaken over long distances, *repeater stations* are used to amplify the signal before it is relayed to the next destination.

**Fact:** Endoscopy means *to look inside* and refers to looking inside the human body for diagnosing medical conditions.

*   **Minimally Invasive Surgery:** Endoscopy allows for diagnosis and treatment through a small incision.
*   **Advantages:** Compared to traditional open surgery, endoscopy is:
    *   Quicker.
    *   Cheaper.
    *   Less traumatic for the patient, leading to faster recovery times.

### Physics Exercises: Critical Angles and Total Internal Reflection

#### Key Concepts
*   **Critical Angle ($\theta_c$):** The angle of incidence in the optically denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the less dense medium is $90^\circ$.
*   **Snell's Law for Critical Angle:** 
    $$n_1 \sin(\theta_c) = n_2 \sin(90^\circ)$$
    Since $\sin(90^\circ) = 1$, the formula simplifies to:
    $$\sin(\theta_c) = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$
    Where $n_1$ is the refractive index of the denser medium and $n_2$ is the refractive index of the less dense medium.
*   **Total Internal Reflection:** Occurs when the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle ($\theta_i > \theta_c$).

# 5.8 Summary: Geometrical Optics

## Key Concepts in Geometrical Optics

### Light Rays and Reflection
*   **Light Rays:** Represented as straight lines with arrows, these are perpendicular to the light's wavefronts.
*   **Reflection:** When light hits a surface, the incoming ray is the **incident ray** and the ray that bounces off is the **reflected ray**.
*   **The Normal:** An imaginary line perpendicular to the surface at the point where the light strikes.
*   **Law of Reflection:** The angle of incidence ($\theta_i$) is equal to the angle of reflection ($\theta_r$), and both rays lie in the same plane as the normal.

### Speed of Light and Refraction
*   **Speed of Light ($c$):** A constant in a given medium. In a vacuum, it reaches a maximum speed of $3 \times 10^8 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.
*   **Refraction:** Occurs at the boundary of two media. As light travels from one medium to another, its speed changes, causing it to "bend" (change direction), though its frequency remains constant.
*   **Refractive Index ($n$):** The ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum ($c$) to its speed in a specific material ($v$).
    $$n = \frac{c}{v}$$
*   **Optical Density:** A measure of the refracting power of a medium.

### Angles and Snell's Law
*   **Angle of Incidence:** The angle between the normal and the incoming (incident) light ray.
*   **Angle of Refraction:** The angle between the normal and the refracted light ray.
*   **Snell's Law:** Defines the relationship between the refractive indices and the angles of incidence and refraction for two media:
    $$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$

### Behaviour of Light at Boundaries
*   **Refraction towards the normal:** Occurs when light travels into a medium of higher optical density.
*   **Refraction away from the normal:** Occurs when light travels into a medium of lower optical density.
*   **Critical Angle:** The specific angle of incidence where the angle of refraction is $90^\circ$, causing the refracted ray to travel exactly along the interface between the two media.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Properties of Light (Revision)
A review of the wave nature of light, electromagnetic spectrum, and the rectilinear propagation of light.

### 2. Refraction
The bending of light as it passes from one optical medium to another of different density. This occurs due to a change in the speed of light.

### 3. Snell's Law
The mathematical relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction when light passes between two media. It is expressed as:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$$
Where:
*   $n_1, n_2$ are the refractive indices of the two media.
*   $\theta_1$ is the angle of incidence.
*   $\theta_2$ is the angle of refraction.

### 4. Critical Angles and Total Internal Reflection
*   **Critical Angle ($\theta_c$):** The angle of incidence in a denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the less dense medium is $90^\circ$.
*   **Total Internal Reflection:** Occurs when light traveling from a denser medium to a less dense medium strikes the boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle, causing the light to reflect entirely back into the denser medium.

### 4. The Law of Reflection

#### Definition
The Law of Reflection states that:
1. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection ($\theta_i = \theta_r$).

#### Mathematical Expression
The relationship is expressed as:
$$\theta_i = \theta_r$$

### 5.5 Refraction
Refraction is the change in direction of a wave passing from one medium to another caused by its change in speed.

#### Refractive Index
The refractive index ($n$) of a material is a measure of how much the speed of light is reduced inside the medium compared to the speed of light in a vacuum ($c$).

The formula for the speed of light in a medium ($v$) is:
$$v = \frac{c}{n}$$

Where:
*   $c = 3 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ (speed of light in a vacuum)
*   $n$ = refractive index of the material
*   $v$ = speed of light in the material

---

### Exercise 5 – 2: Refractive index

**1. Calculate the speed of light in water (ice).**

*   **Given:** Refractive index of water (ice) $n = 1,31$
*   **Formula:** $v = \frac{c}{n}$
*   **Calculation:**
    $$v = \frac{3 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{1,31}$$
    $$v = 2,29 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

**2. Calculate the refractive index of an unknown substance where the speed of light through the substance is $1,974 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.**

*   **Calculation:**
    $$n = \frac{3 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{1,974 \times 10^{8} \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}$$
    $$n \approx 1,52$$
*   **Conclusion:** By comparing this value to a standard refractive index table, the substance can be identified.

### Geometrical Optics: Total Internal Reflection

#### Key Concept: Critical Angle ($\theta_c$)
The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the optically denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the less dense medium is $90^\circ$. Total internal reflection occurs when the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

The formula used to calculate the critical angle is derived from Snell's Law:
$$n_1 \sin \theta_c = n_2 \sin 90^\circ$$
$$\theta_c = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{n_2}{n_1} \right)$$

Where:
*   $n_1$ = refractive index of the denser medium
*   $n_2$ = refractive index of the less dense medium

---

# Applications of Optic Fibres in Medicine: Endoscopes

## Overview
Optic fibres play a critical role in modern medical diagnostics and surgery. The primary application discussed in this section is the **endoscope**.

## The Endoscope
An endoscope is a medical instrument that allows a doctor to examine the interior of a patient's body.

### Structure and Function
*   **Core Component:** The main part of an endoscope is the **optical fibre**.
*   **Mechanism:** Light is shone down the optical fibre to illuminate the area inside the body.
*   **Usage:** A doctor inserts the endoscope (usually down the throat) to view internal organs, such as the stomach.

## Minimally Invasive Surgery
Endoscopes have revolutionised surgical procedures by enabling **minimally invasive surgery**.

### Comparison: Endoscopy vs. Open Surgery

| Feature | Endoscopy (Minimally Invasive) | Open Surgery |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Incision Size** | Small incision (cut) | Large incision |
| **Cost** | Cheaper | Expensive |
| **Duration** | Quicker | Requires more time |
| **Patient Recovery** | Faster recovery | Slower recovery |
| **Trauma** | Less traumatic | More traumatic |

## Summary of Advantages
1.  **Diagnostic Precision:** Allows for direct visual examination of internal body structures.
2.  **Patient Comfort:** Reduces the physical trauma associated with traditional surgical methods.
3.  **Efficiency:** Decreases the time required for both the procedure and the subsequent recovery period.
4.  **Economic Benefit:** Provides a more cost-effective alternative to traditional open surgery.
