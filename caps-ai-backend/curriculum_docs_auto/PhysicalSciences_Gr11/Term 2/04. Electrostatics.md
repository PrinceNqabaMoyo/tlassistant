# Chapter 16: Electrostatics

## Introduction and Key Concepts
Electrostatics is the study of electric charge that is at rest or static (not moving). This chapter explores the basic principles of electrostatics and the principle of conservation of charge.

---

## Two Kinds of Charge
All objects contain large amounts of electric charge. There are two types of electric charge: **positive** and **negative**.

### Net Charge
*   **Neutral:** An object is electrically neutral if it contains equal amounts of positive and negative charge. The net charge is zero.
*   **Electrically Charged:** An object is electrically charged if there is an unequal amount of one type of charge compared to the other.

### Examples of Charge Distribution
The following table summarizes how net charge is calculated based on the distribution of protons (positive) and electrons (negative):

| State | Calculation | Net Charge |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Neutral | $6 + (-6) = 0$ | $0$ |
| Positively Charged | $8 + (-6) = 2$ | $+2$ |
| Negatively Charged | $6 + (-9) = -3$ | $-3$ |

### Charge Carriers
*   **Positive charge:** Carried by protons within the material.
*   **Negative charge:** Carried by electrons.
*   **Note:** The overall charge of an object is usually determined by changes in the number of electrons.

## Understanding Electric Charge

Objects become charged based on the movement of electrons. Protons (positive charges) remain stationary in the nucleus, while electrons (negative charges) can be added or removed.

*   **Positively charged:** Electrons are removed, making the object electron deficient.
*   **Negatively charged:** Electrons are added, giving the object an excess of electrons.

### Net Charge Calculation
The net charge of an object is determined by the sum of its positive and negative charges:

| State | Calculation | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Neutral** | $6 + (-6) = 0$ | Zero net charge |
| **Positively Charged** | $6 + (-4) = 2$ | Net charge of $+2$ |
| **Negatively Charged** | $6 + (-9) = -3$ | Net charge of $-3$ |

## Tribo-electric Charging

Objects can become charged through contact or friction (rubbing). When objects are rubbed together, negative charge (electrons) is transferred from one object to the other.

### Key Principles
*   **Charge Transfer:** When you rub your feet against a carpet, negative charges transfer from the carpet to you. You become negatively charged, and the carpet becomes positively charged by the same amount.
*   **Conservation of Charge:** Charge, like energy, cannot be created or destroyed. We say that charge is **conserved**.
*   **Neutral Objects:** Initially, objects like a plastic ruler and a cotton cloth have equal amounts of positive and negative charges, resulting in a net charge of zero.

### Note on Representation
We use the symbols $+$ and $-$ to illustrate the balance and changes in charge. It is important to remember:
1.  These symbols represent the **net balance**, not the actual physical location of the charges.
2.  Charges are spread throughout the material.
3.  The actual change occurs due to the increase or decrease of electrons on the surface of the materials.

## The Principle of Conservation of Charge

Electrostatics involves the study of stationary electric charges. A fundamental principle in this field is the **Law of Conservation of Charge**, which states that the total electric charge in an isolated system remains constant. Charge cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one object to another.

### Process of Tribo-electric Charging (Rubbing)

When two neutral materials are rubbed together, electrons are transferred from one material to the other.

#### 1. Before Rubbing
Both objects are neutral because they contain an equal number of positive and negative charges.
*   **Ruler:** 9 positive charges, 9 negative charges.
*   **Cotton Cloth:** 5 positive charges, 5 negative charges.
*   **Total System:** 14 positive charges, 14 negative charges.

## Tribo-electric Charging

The process of materials becoming charged when they come into contact with other materials is known as **tribo-electric charging**. Materials can be arranged in a **tribo-electric series** according to the likelihood of them gaining or losing electrons.

### Steps to Determine Charging
When analyzing how materials become charged, follow these steps:

1.  **Extract material properties:** Locate the materials in the tribo-electric series. Identify which material is more positive and which is more negative.
    *   *Example:* Silk falls above cotton in the series, making silk more positive relative to cotton.
2.  **Apply principles:** When two materials are rubbed together:
    *   The material that is **more negative** in the series gains electrons.
    *   The material that is **more positive** in the series loses electrons.
    *   *Example:* Silk (more positive) will lose electrons, and cotton (more negative) will gain electrons.
    *   *Result:* The cotton, having gained electrons, becomes negatively charged.

---

## Key Definitions

*   **Neutral:** A material with equal numbers of positive and negative charges (no overall charge).
*   **Electron Deficient:** A neutral material that has lost electrons, resulting in an overall **positive charge**.
*   **Excess Electrons:** A neutral material that has gained electrons, resulting in an overall **negative charge**.

---

## Important Notes

*   **Conservation of Charge:** In the real world, only a tiny fraction of charges move between objects, but the total charge of the system is always conserved.
*   **Tribo-electric Series:** The ordering of materials in this series is based on their tendency to lose or gain electrons.

## Fundamental Principles of Electrostatic Forces

*   **Like charges:** Charges of the same sign (positive-positive or negative-negative) exert a **repulsive** force on each other.
*   **Opposite (unlike) charges:** Charges of different signs (positive-negative) exert an **attractive** force on each other.

### Distance and Force Relationship
The magnitude of the electrostatic force is dependent on the distance between the charges:
*   The **closer** together the charges are, the **stronger** the electrostatic force between them.
*   The **further apart** the charges are, the **weaker** the electrostatic force between them.

## Problem Solving Strategy: Determining Charge
When determining the charge of an object based on its interaction with another charged object, follow these steps:

1.  **Analyse the problem:** Identify the observed interaction (attraction or repulsion) between the spheres.
2.  **Identify the principles:** Apply the fundamental laws of electrostatics:
    *   Like charges **repel** each other.
    *   Opposite charges **attract** each other.
3.  **Apply the principles:**
    *   **Case A (Attraction):** If a positively charged sphere attracts the target sphere, the target sphere must be **negatively charged** (since opposite charges attract).
    *   **Case B (Repulsion):** If a positively charged sphere repels the target sphere, the target sphere must be **positively charged** (since like charges repel).

---

## Conservation of Charge

In all electrostatic interactions, charge is neither created nor destroyed; it is simply transferred from one material to another.

### Definition: Principle of Conservation of Charge
The principle of conservation of charge states that the net charge of an isolated system remains constant during any physical process (e.g., when two charged objects make contact and then separate).

## Conductors and Insulators

Materials are classified based on how easily electrons can move through them:

*   **Conductors:** Materials that allow electrons to move relatively freely through them. Examples include most metals and the human body.
*   **Insulators (Non-conductors):** Materials that do not allow charge carriers (electrons) to move through them because the electrons are bound to the atoms. Examples include plastic and glass.

### Behaviour of Charge
*   **On Insulators:** If an excess of charge is placed on an insulator, it remains localized where it was placed.
*   **On Conductors:** If an excess of charge is placed on a conductor, the like charges repel each other and spread out over the outside surface of the object.
*   **Sharing Charge:** When two identical conductors are made to touch, the total charge is shared equally between them. Each conductor will be left with half of the total net charge:
    $$Q_{final} = \frac{Q_{total}}{2}$$

> **Tip:** The effect of the shape on charge distribution is the reason that we only consider identical conductors for the sharing of charge.

---

## Arrangement of Charge

The electrostatic force determines how charge is arranged on the surface of a conductor. Because charges can move freely within a conductive material, they respond to the repulsive forces between like charges.

### Charge Distribution
*   **Spherical Conductors:** Charges spread uniformly over the surface of the sphere.
*   **Irregularly Shaped Conductors:** There is a concentration of charge near the sharp point or points of the object.

# Chapter 16: Electrostatics

## Principle of Charge Conservation
When two identical conducting spheres on insulating stands are allowed to touch, they share the total charge evenly between them. If the initial charges are $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, the final charge $Q$ on each sphere is calculated as:

$$Q = \frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}$$

---

## Quantisation of Charge
The basic unit of charge, known as the **elementary charge ($e$)**, is the amount of charge carried by a single electron.

### Unit of Charge
The charge on a single electron is defined as:
$$q_e = 1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$$

All other charges in the universe consist of an integer multiple of this elementary charge. This principle is known as **charge quantisation**, expressed by the formula:

$$Q = nq_e$$

Where:
*   $Q$ = total charge (measured in Coulombs, C)
*   $n$ = an integer (number of electrons)
*   $q_e$ = elementary charge ($1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$)

### Units and Conversions
Charge is measured in **Coulombs (C)**. Because a Coulomb is a very large unit, we often use smaller prefixes:
*   **Micro-coulombs ($\mu\text{C}$):** $1 \mu\text{C} = 1 \times 10^{-6} \text{ C}$
*   **Nanocoulombs (nC):** $1 \text{ nC} = 1 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C}$

---

## Historical Fact: Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment
In 1909, Robert Millikan and Harvey Fletcher measured the charge on an electron. In this experiment, they sprayed oil droplets into the space between two charged plates. By observing the forces acting on the droplets, they were able to determine the fundamental charge of an electron.

---

## Worked Example: Charge Quantisation

**Question:**
An object has an excess charge of $-1,92 \times 10^{-17} \text{ C}$. How many excess electrons does it have?

**Solution:**
Using the formula $Q = nq_e$:

1. Identify the given values:
   * $Q = -1,92 \times 10^{-17} \text{ C}$
   * $q_e = -1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$ (using the negative sign for an electron)

2. Rearrange the formula to solve for $n$:
   $$n = \frac{Q}{q_e}$$

3. Substitute the values:
   $$n = \frac{-1,92 \times 10^{-17}}{-1,6 \times 10^{-19}}$$
   $$n = 120$$

**Answer:**
The object has an excess of $120$ electrons.

    To find the number of electrons ($N$), divide the total charge by the charge of a single electron:

### How an Electroscope Detects Charge
When a charged object (e.g., a positively charged rod) is brought close to the neutral metal plate of an electroscope without touching it, the following process occurs:

1.  **Charge Induction:** The positive rod attracts negative charges (electrons) from the gold foil and metal rod up toward the metal plate.
2.  **Charge Separation:** Because the metal is a conductor, charges move freely. This results in an accumulation of negative charge on the metal plate and an accumulation of positive charge on the gold foil leaves.
3.  **Repulsion:** The induced positive charges on the gold leaves repel each other, causing the leaves to spread apart.
4.  **Neutrality:** It is important to note that the electroscope remains neutral overall; the charges have simply been redistributed (induced to move) within the instrument.
5.  **Reversal:** If the charged rod is moved away, the charges redistribute evenly, and the leaves fall back together.

## Grounding

Grounding is the process of connecting a charged object to the Earth (the ground) to allow charge to flow.

### The Process of Grounding
If you bring a charged rod close to an uncharged electroscope and touch the metal plate with your finger:

*   **Charge Flow:** Electrons flow from the ground, through your body, and onto the metal plate.
*   **Attraction:** The electrons are attracted to the positive charge on the rod.
*   **Net Charge:** The electroscope is no longer neutral; it now has an excess of negative charge.
*   **Result:** If you remove the rod, the leaves remain apart because they now possess a permanent excess of negative charge, causing them to repel each other.

## Polarisation

### Definition
**Polarisation** is the phenomenon where a charged object exerts a force on a neutral insulator, causing a slight shift in the position of charges within the atoms of the insulator.

### Key Concepts
*   **Insulators (Non-conductors):** Materials where electrons are bound to their atoms and cannot move freely through the material.
*   **Mechanism:** When a charged object (e.g., a positively charged rod) is brought near a neutral insulator (e.g., polystyrene), the electrons within the atoms are attracted toward the rod, while the positive nuclei are pushed slightly to the opposite side of the atoms.
*   **Effect:** This microscopic shift creates a separation of charge within the atoms.
*   **Important Note:** The insulator remains **neutral** because no charge is added to or removed from the object. It is merely polarised.
*   **Macroscopic Result:** If the object is light enough (like a small polystyrene ball), the cumulative force of this polarisation can be sufficient to cause the object to be attracted to the charged rod.

## Investigation: Electrostatic Force

### Objective
To demonstrate that like charges repel and unlike charges attract.

### Procedure
1. Take a glass ball and rub it with a piece of silk.
2. Hang the ball from its middle using a piece of string so that it is free to move.
3. Rub another glass rod with silk and bring it close to the hanging glass ball.
   * **Observation:** The ball moves *away* from the rod (it is **repelled**).
4. Take a plastic rod and rub it with a piece of fur.
5. Bring the plastic rod close to the hanging glass ball.
   * **Observation:** The ball moves *towards* the rod (it is **attracted**).

# Chapter 16: Electrostatics

## Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Types of Charge:** There are two kinds of electric charge: positive and negative.
    *   **Positive charge:** Carried by protons in the nucleus.
    *   **Negative charge:** Carried by electrons.
*   **Neutral Objects:** Objects that are neutral have an equal number of positive and negative charges.
*   **Interaction of Charges:**
    *   **Unlike charges:** Attract each other.
    *   **Like charges:** Repel each other.
*   **Conservation of Charge:** Charge is neither created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred.
*   **Quantisation of Charge:** Charge is measured in coulombs (C) and is quantised in units of the charge of an electron ($1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$). The formula is:
    $$Q = n q_e$$
*   **Materials:**
    *   **Conductors:** Materials that allow charge to move through them easily.
    *   **Insulators:** Materials that do not allow charge to move through them easily.
*   **Charge Sharing:** When two identical, conducting spheres are brought into contact, they share their charge according to the following formula:
    $$Q = \frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}$$

### Electrostatics: Revision Exercises

#### 1. Conceptual Understanding of Electrostatic Forces
**Question 5:** Two charged spheres are hanging from strings. Both spheres have a positive (+) charge.

*   **Analysis:** According to the law of electrostatics, like charges repel each other, and opposite charges attract each other. Since both spheres are positively charged, they will exert a repulsive force on one another.
*   **Correct Option:** **d.** The spheres will swing away from each other due to the repulsive electrostatic force between them.

---

#### 2. Charging Methods (Insulators)
**Question 6:** Describe how insulators can be charged by contact or rubbing.

*   **Charging by Rubbing (Triboelectric Effect):** When two different insulating materials are rubbed together, electrons are transferred from one material to the other. The material that loses electrons becomes positively charged, while the material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged.
*   **Charging by Contact:** If a charged object touches an uncharged insulator, a small amount of charge may be transferred to the surface of the insulator. Because insulators do not allow charge to flow freely, the charge tends to remain at the point of contact.

---

#### 3. Practical Application: Perspex Ruler
**Question 7:** Using a perspex ruler and a piece of cloth.

*   **a. How to charge the ruler:** Rub the perspex ruler vigorously with the cloth.
*   **b. Explanation in terms of charge:** Friction causes electrons to be transferred from the atoms of the ruler to the cloth (or vice versa, depending on the material properties). This leaves the ruler with an excess or deficit of electrons, resulting in a net static charge.
*   **c. Attraction of paper:** The charged ruler induces a separation of charge in the neutral pieces of paper (polarization). The side of the paper closer to the ruler develops an opposite charge to that of the ruler, creating an attractive force that overcomes gravity.

---

#### 4. Charge Distribution on Conductors
**Question 8:** An uncharged hollow metal sphere is touched by a positively charged rod at point P.

*   **Concept:** Metals are conductors. When a charged object touches a conductor, the charge redistributes itself to minimize repulsion. On a spherical conductor, the excess charge will spread out as far as possible from each other.
*   **Result:** Because the sphere is a conductor, the positive charge transferred from the rod will move freely across the surface of the sphere until it is evenly distributed.
*   **Correct Option:** **b.** It is evenly distributed over the outer surface of the hollow sphere.

We know that charge is quantized and that protons are positive and carry the base unit of charge which is $+1,6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$.

As each proton carries the same charge the total charge must be the sum of all the individual proton charges. We just need to multiply the base charge by the number of protons:

$$Q = 235 \times 1,6 \times 10^{-19} = 376 \times 10^{-17}\text{ C}$$

17. Two identical, metal spheres have different charges. Sphere 1 has a charge of $-4.8 \times 10^{-18}\text{ C}$. Sphere 2 has $30$ excess protons. If the two spheres are brought into contact and then separated, what charge will each have? How many electrons or protons does this correspond to?

**Solution:**
The total charge ($Q_{\text{total}}$) is calculated by finding the charge of the protons on Sphere 2 and adding it to the charge of Sphere 1:

$$Q_{\text{total}} = q_1 + q_2$$
$$Q_{\text{total}} = (-4.8 \times 10^{-18}\text{ C}) + (30 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C})$$
$$Q_{\text{total}} = -4.8 \times 10^{-18}\text{ C} + 4.8 \times 10^{-18}\text{ C} = 0\text{ C}$$

As the spheres are identical in material, size, and shape, the charge will redistribute across the two spheres so that it is shared evenly. Each sphere will have half of the total charge:

$$\text{Charge on each sphere} = \frac{0\text{ C}}{2} = 0\text{ C}$$

So each sphere is now neutral. 

No net charge means that there is no excess of electrons or protons.
