## Key Concepts

### 1. Wavefronts
A wavefront is an imaginary surface joining all points in a wave that are in the same phase of oscillation. These are used to visualize the propagation of waves in 2D and 3D space.

### 2. Huygens Principle
Huygens' principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets. The new wavefront at a later time is the surface that is tangent to these secondary wavelets.

### 3. Diffraction
Diffraction is the bending of waves around the corners of an obstacle or through an aperture into the region of geometrical shadow.

### 4. Diffraction through a Single Slit
When a wave passes through a narrow slit, the slit acts as a point source, causing the wave to spread out. The intensity pattern produced depends on the relationship between the wavelength ($\lambda$) and the width of the slit ($a$).

### 6.3 Huygens Principle

Christiaan Huygens developed a principle to describe and determine the path of waves as they travel through a medium.

#### Definition: The Huygens Principle
Every point of a wavefront serves as a point source of spherical, secondary waves. After a time $t$, the new position of the wavefront will be that of a surface tangent to the secondary waves.

#### Application
The Huygens Principle is universal and applies to any wavefront, including those that are curved. 

**Example:**
Consider a single wavefront moving forward. According to the principle:
1.  At time $t$, the wavefront acts as a collection of point sources.
2.  These point sources generate circular secondary waves.
3.  After a small time interval $\Delta t$, the new wavefront is formed by the surface tangent to these secondary waves.

# 6.4 Diffraction

### Definition: Diffraction
Diffraction is the ability of a wave to spread out in wavefronts as the wave passes through a small aperture or around a sharp edge.

---

### Understanding Diffraction
When waves move through small holes, they appear to bend around the sides. This occurs because there are not enough points on the wavefront to form another straight wavefront. This bending around the sides is what we call **diffraction**.

**Example:**
If two rooms are connected by an open doorway and a sound is produced in a remote corner of one room, a person in the other room will hear the sound as if it originated at the doorway. To the second room, the vibrating air in the doorway acts as the source of the sound.

### Extension: Factors Affecting Diffraction
Diffraction effects are most noticeable when the wavelength of the wave is comparable to or larger than the size of the aperture or obstacle.

*   **Wavelength Relationship:**
    *   The amount of diffraction (the sharpness of the bending) increases with increasing wavelength.
    *   The amount of diffraction decreases with decreasing wavelength.
*   **Obstacle Interaction:**
    *   When the wavelength of the waves is significantly smaller than the obstacle, no noticeable diffraction occurs.
    *   When waves encounter obstacles, they pass around the barrier into the regions behind it, causing the water behind the barrier to be disturbed.

**Experimental Demonstrations:**
1.  **Ripple Tank:** Use water waves to observe diffraction around small barriers and through apertures.
2.  **Light:** Demonstrate diffraction using a single slit and a light source with coloured filters.

# General Experiment: Diffraction

Water waves in a ripple tank are used to demonstrate the phenomena of diffraction and interference.

## Experimental Procedure

### 1. Diffraction with High Frequency (Short Wavelength)
*   Set the wave generator to a high frequency.
*   Place obstacles (e.g., a brick or ruler) into the tank one at a time.
*   **Observation:** Observe how the wavefronts behave as they propagate near or past the obstacles.
*   **Analysis:** Determine how the degree of diffraction changes when the size of the object is altered.

### 2. Diffraction with Low Frequency (Long Wavelength)
*   Reduce the frequency of the wave generator to produce longer wavelengths.
*   Place the same obstacles into the tank.
*   **Observation:** Observe the behavior of the wavefronts.
*   **Analysis:** Compare the diffraction observed here to the high-frequency case.

### 3. Interference (In-Phase)
*   Remove all obstacles and introduce a second wave generator.
*   Synchronize both generators so they start at the same time with the same frequency.
*   **Observation:** Observe the interaction when the two sets of wavefronts meet.
*   **Analysis:** Identify regions of constructive and destructive interference.

### 4. Interference (Out-of-Phase)
*   Adjust the generators so they are out of phase (they do not generate waves at the same time).
*   **Observation:** Observe the interaction when the two sets of wavefronts meet.
*   **Analysis:** Identify regions of constructive and destructive interference.

# 6.5 Diffraction through a single slit

## Concept: Huygens' Principle
Diffraction occurs when waves encounter obstacles or apertures. According to Huygens' principle, every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets.

*   When a wavefront hits a barrier with a slit, only the points on the wavefront that pass through the slit continue forward.
*   The points at the edges of the slit act as new sources, causing the waves to bend (diffract) around the edges.

### Diffraction and Huygens' Principle

#### The Mechanism of Diffraction
When a wavefront approaches a barrier with a slit, it generates forward-moving wavefronts according to **Huygens' principle**. 

*   **Before the barrier:** The wavefronts are straight lines.
*   **At the barrier:** The barrier blocks the majority of the wavefront. The secondary waves that would normally interfere to maintain a straight wavefront are obstructed.
*   **After the barrier:** Because the secondary waves are blocked, the remaining wavefronts bend around the edges of the slit. Consequently, the wavefronts are no longer straight lines.

#### Diffraction Patterns
By applying Huygens' principle further, we can analyze the behavior of waves passing through a slit.

**Key Concepts:**
1.  **Point Sources:** Each point on the wavefront passing through the slit acts as a new point source of waves.
2.  **Interference:** If we consider two point sources (Source A and Source B) at the edges of the slit that emit waves of the same wavelength and frequency, they will interfere with each other.

**Interference Types:**
*   **Constructive Interference:** Occurs where a peak meets a peak, or a trough meets a trough. In diagrams, these points are often marked with a **solid diamond**.
*   **Destructive Interference:** Occurs where a trough meets a peak. In diagrams, these points are often marked with a **hollow diamond**.

When these waves strike a barrier, there will be specific locations along the barrier where constructive interference occurs and other locations where destructive interference occurs.

# Effect of Slit Width and Wavelength on Diffraction Patterns

The extent to which a diffracted wave spreads out after passing through a slit depends on two factors: the width of the slit ($w$) and the wavelength of the waves ($\lambda$).

### The Relationship
The degree to which diffraction occurs is proportional to the ratio of the wavelength to the slit width:

$$\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{\lambda}{w}$$

### Sanity Check: Special Cases
We can analyze the behavior of diffraction by testing extreme values for $\lambda$ and $w$:

| Scenario | Mathematical Representation | Result | Physical Interpretation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lambda = 1$, $w$ is very large | $\frac{1}{\text{very big number}}$ | Very small | Very little diffraction |
| $\lambda = 1$, $w$ is very small | $\frac{1}{\text{very small number}}$ | Very big | Large diffraction |
| $\lambda$ is very big, $w = 1$ | $\frac{\text{very big number}}{1}$ | Very big | Large diffraction |
| $\lambda$ is very small, $w = 1$ | $\frac{\text{very small number}}{1}$ | Very small | Little diffraction |

# Wave Nature of Light

Visible light is a form of electromagnetic (EM) radiation. While EM radiation exhibits diffraction, we do not typically observe the diffraction of light around everyday objects (like doors or windows) because the wavelength of light is extremely small compared to the size of these "slits."

### Worked Example: Diffraction of Green Light
To understand why we don't see light diffracting through a doorway, we can calculate the diffraction factor for green light.

**Given:**
*   Wavelength of green light ($\lambda$) = $532 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}$
*   Width of an open door ($w$) = $1 \text{ m}$

**Calculation:**
Using the proportionality relationship:

$$\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{\lambda}{w}$$
$$\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{532 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}}{1 \text{ m}}$$
$$\text{diffraction} \propto 532 \times 10^{-9}$$

**Conclusion:**
Because the result ($532 \times 10^{-9}$) is an extremely small number, the amount of diffraction for visible light passing through a door is negligible.

**Step 1: Determine what is required**
We need to compare the diffraction patterns to extract information about the relative wavelengths. We know that the diffraction pattern depends on the wavelength ($\lambda$) and slit width ($w$) through the relationship:

### Calculating Maxima and Minima

*Note: This section is for interest and extension. If you are following the CAPS curriculum, please skip to the end-of-chapter summary.*

#### Concept: Interference Spectrum
In an interference spectrum, there are multiple minima. These minima are symmetrical on either side of the central peak. The distances from the central peak to the minima are identical on both sides, acting as mirror images.

*   **Labelling:** The first minimum on the positive side is labelled $m = 1$.
*   **Labelling:** The first minimum on the negative side is labelled $m = -1$.
*   **Sequence:** Subsequent minima are labelled $m = \pm 2, \pm 3$, and so on.



![Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_2D-and-3D-wavefronts.pdf-0012-03.png](images/Textbook_PhysicalSciences_Gr11_2D-and-3D-wavefronts.pdf-0012-03.png)

The angle at which the minima in the interference spectrum occur is given by the formula:

**Where:**

*   $w$ is the width of the slit.
*   $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the impinging wavefronts.
*   $m$ is the order of the minimum, where $m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \dots$

### Diffraction Through a Single Slit

#### Key Concept: Diffraction Minimum
When light passes through a narrow slit, it creates a diffraction pattern. The angle $\theta$ at which the first minimum occurs can be calculated using the relationship between the slit width ($w$) and the wavelength of the light ($\lambda$).

**Formula:**
$$\sin \theta = \frac{m\lambda}{w}$$

Where:
*   $m$ = order of the minimum (for the first minimum, $m = 1$)
*   $\lambda$ = wavelength of light (in meters)
*   $w$ = width of the slit (in meters)
*   $\theta$ = angle of the minimum

---

#### Worked Example: Diffraction Minimum

**Question:**
A slit with a width of $2511\text{ nm}$ has green light of wavelength $532\text{ nm}$ impinge on it. The diffracted light interferes on a surface; at what angle will the first minimum be?

**Step 1: Identify given information**
*   Slit width ($w$) = $2511\text{ nm} = 2511 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$
*   Wavelength ($\lambda$) = $532\text{ nm} = 532 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$
*   Order ($m$) = $1$ (for the first minimum)

**Step 2: Applicable principles**
Use the diffraction formula:
$$\sin \theta = \frac{m\lambda}{w}$$

**Step 3: Substitution and Calculation**
$$\sin \theta = \frac{1 \times (532 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m})}{2511 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}$$
$$\sin \theta = \frac{532}{2511}$$
$$\sin \theta \approx 0,21186778$$
$$\theta = \sin^{-1}(0,21186778)$$
$$\theta \approx 12,23^\circ$$

**Conclusion:**
The first minimum is at approximately $12,23^\circ$ from the centre maximum.

$$\sin \theta = \frac{650}{2511}$$
$$\sin \theta = 0,258861012$$

## 6.6 Chapter Summary

### Key Concepts
*   **Wavefront:** An imaginary line that connects waves that are in phase.
*   **Huygens' Principle:** Every point of a wavefront serves as a point source of spherical, secondary waves. After a time $t$, the new position of the wavefront will be that of a surface tangent to the secondary waves.
*   **Diffraction:** The ability of a wave to spread out in wavefronts as the wave passes through a small aperture or around a sharp edge.
*   **Slit Diffraction:** When a wave passes through a slit, the wavefront spreads out or "bends" around corners.
*   **Degree of Diffraction:** The extent of diffraction depends on the width of the slit ($w$) and the wavelength of the wave ($\lambda$):
    $$\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{\lambda}{w}$$

# 6 2D and 3D Wavefronts

This chapter explores the nature of wavefronts, building upon Grade 10 knowledge of electromagnetic radiation and the properties of transverse and longitudinal waves.

---

### Key Concepts

#### 1. Definition of a Wavefront
Wavefronts are imaginary lines joining points on a wave that are in phase (i.e., points that are at the same position in their cycle of vibration).

#### 2. Huygens's Principle
Every point on a wavefront acts as a point source of spherical, secondary waves. After a time $t$, the new position of the wavefront is defined by the surface tangent to these secondary waves. This principle explains how waves propagate and interact with one another.

#### 3. Definition of Diffraction
Diffraction is the ability of a wave to spread out into wavefronts as it passes through a small aperture (slit) or around a sharp edge. This phenomenon is clearly observable with water waves in a ripple tank, where waves spread into the calm area beyond a barrier.

#### 4. Sketching Diffraction Patterns
Learners are required to create simple sketches of diffraction patterns and understand how waves interfere to form constructive (crests) and destructive (troughs) patterns.

#### 5. The Degree of Diffraction
The extent to which a wave diffracts is determined by the relationship between the wavelength ($\lambda$) and the width of the slit ($w$). The degree of diffraction is:
*   Proportional to the wavelength ($\lambda$).
*   Inversely proportional to the width of the slit ($w$).

#### 6. Diffraction of Light
These concepts are applied to light. When light passes through a single slit or a diffraction grating, the resulting pattern provides evidence for the wave nature of light.

**2. A water break at the entrance to a harbour consists of a rock barrier with a $50\text{ m}$ wide opening. Ocean waves of $20\text{ m}$ wavelength approach the opening straight on. Light with a wavelength of $500 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$ strikes a single slit of width $30 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$. Which waves are diffracted to a greater extent?**

*   **Solution:**
    We determine the extent of diffraction using the relationship:
    $$\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{\lambda}{w}$$

    **For water waves:**
    $$\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{20\text{ m}}{50\text{ m}}$$
    $$\text{diffraction} \propto 0,4$$

    **For light waves:**
    $$\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{500 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}{30 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}$$
    $$\text{diffraction} \propto 16,67$$

    **Conclusion:** The light waves are diffracted more.

### Diffraction Patterns and Wave Properties

The extent of diffraction is influenced by the wavelength of the wave. The relationship between frequency ($f$) and wavelength ($\lambda$) is inverse, defined by the wave equation $v = f\lambda$.

*   **Increased Wavelength:** Leads to more diffraction, resulting in a wider diffraction pattern.
*   **Decreased Wavelength:** Leads to less diffraction, resulting in a narrower diffraction pattern.

### Diffraction Calculations

The following calculations use the diffraction formula for a single slit:
$$\sin \theta = \frac{m\lambda}{w}$$
Where:
*   $\theta$ = angle of diffraction
*   $m$ = order of the minimum (assumed $m=1$ in these examples)
*   $\lambda$ = wavelength
*   $w$ = slit width

---

#### Part a) Water Waves and Light Diffraction
**1. Calculate the angle of diffraction for water waves:**
Given $\lambda = 3\text{ m}$ and $w = 16\text{ m}$:
$$\sin \theta = \frac{m\lambda}{w}$$
$$\sin \theta = \frac{3\text{ m}}{16\text{ m}}$$
$$\sin \theta = 0,1875$$
$$\theta = 10,81^\circ$$

**2. Determine the slit width ($w$) for light of $635\text{ nm}$ to have a smaller angle:**
Using the angle calculated above ($0,1875$) and $\lambda = 635 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$:
$$\sin \theta = \frac{m\lambda}{w}$$
$$0,1875 = \frac{635 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}{w}$$
$$w = \frac{635 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}{0,1875}$$
$$w = 3,4 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$$

**Conclusion:** The slit must be less than $3,4 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$.

---

#### Part b) Diffraction Angle and Slit Width
**1. Calculate the angle of diffraction:**
Given $\lambda = 786 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$ and $w = 30 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$:
$$\sin \theta = \frac{m\lambda}{w}$$
$$\sin \theta = \frac{786 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}{30 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}}$$
$$\sin \theta = 0,262$$
$$\theta = 15,18^\circ$$

**2. Determine the slit width ($w$) for light of $635\text{ nm}$ to have a smaller angle:**
Using the angle calculated above ($0,262$) and $\lambda = 635 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$:
$$\sin \theta = \frac{m\lambda}{w}$$
$$0,262 = \frac{635 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}{w}$$
$$w = \frac{635 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}{0,262}$$
$$w = 2,4 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$$

**Conclusion:** The slit must be less than $2,4 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$.
