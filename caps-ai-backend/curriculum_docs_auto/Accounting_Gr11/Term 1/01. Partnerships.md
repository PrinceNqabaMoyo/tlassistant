# Term 2 Topic 1: Partnerships: Analysis of Financial Statements

## Financial Statements
Financial statements provide a summary of a business's activities during a financial period. Because these statements do not inherently explain whether performance has improved or declined, owners and managers must perform **analysis and interpretation**.

*   **Analysis:** Investigating information using generally accepted calculations or ratios.
*   **Interpretation:** Determining the causes behind changes, improvements, or declines in results to facilitate informed decision-making.

### Key Concepts for Analysis
All forms of ownership utilize the following indicators to evaluate business health:
1.  **Profitability:** The ability to generate income relative to expenses.
2.  **Liquidity:** The ability to meet short-term financial obligations.
3.  **Solvency:** The ability to meet long-term financial obligations.
4.  **Risk and Gearing:** The relationship between borrowed funds and owner's equity.
5.  **Return on Investment (ROI):** An indication of how effectively the owner's capital has earned income.

*Note: The return on owner's equity is calculated as net profit expressed as a percentage of owner's equity.*

---

## Profitability
Profitability measures the business's efficiency in generating income compared to the costs and expenses incurred.

### Profitability Indicators

| Indicator | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Gross profit on sales** | Shows how much of each rand is left after the basic cost of products is deducted. |
| **Gross profit on cost of sales** | Compared with the mark-up percentage; indicates how well the business achieved its mark-up. |
| **Operating expenses on sales** | Shows what percentage of each rand received is used to pay expenses. |
| **Operating profit on sales** | Shows how much money remains after expenses have been paid. |
| **Net profit on sales** | Shows how much of each rand is left over for the owners. |

## Liquidity
Liquidity is the ability of a business to pay its short-term debts. It involves comparing current assets to current liabilities. Current assets are essential as they are used to fund day-to-day operations and pay ongoing expenses.

### Liquidity Ratios and Indicators

| Ratio | Formula / Calculation | Norm / Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Current ratio** | $\frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$ | Norm is $2:1$ (R2 available for every R1 debt). Indicates ability to cover short-term debts (payable within one year). |
| **Acid test ratio** | $\frac{\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventory}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$ | Norm is $1:1$ (R1 available for every R1 debt). Indicates ability to cover short-term debts even if stock cannot be sold. |
| **Stock turnover rate** | $\frac{\text{Cost of sales}}{\text{Average stock}}$ | Indicates how many times stock is converted into money within a year. Faster turnover leads to faster profit. |
| **Stock holding period** | $\frac{\text{Average stock} \times 365}{\text{Cost of sales}}$ | Indicates the period for which the business has stock available. |
| **Debtors collection period** | $\frac{\text{Average debtors} \times 365}{\text{Credit sales}}$ | Debtors usually have 30 days to pay. Taking longer may hinder the business's ability to pay expenses. |
| **Creditors payment period** | $\frac{\text{Average creditors} \times 365}{\text{Credit purchases}}$ | Creditors usually grant 60 to 90 days. Staying within this period maintains good supplier relationships. |

### Key Calculations for Averages
*   **Average stock:** $\frac{1}{2}(\text{stock at beginning} + \text{stock at end})$
*   **Average debtors:** $\frac{1}{2}(\text{debtors at beginning} + \text{debtors at end})$
*   **Average creditors:** $\frac{1}{2}(\text{creditors at beginning} + \text{creditors at end})$

---

## Solvency
Solvency is the ability of a business to meet all its obligations. 
*   **Solvent:** A business is solvent if its total assets exceed its total liabilities.
*   **Insolvent:** A business is insolvent if it can no longer meet its obligations, meaning it can no longer operate and is undergoing bankruptcy.

## 1. Solvency and Net Assets

| Concept | Formula / Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Net assets** | $\text{Total assets} - \text{Total liabilities} = \text{Net assets (Owner's equity)}$ |
| **Solvency ratio** | $\text{Total assets} : \text{Total liabilities}$ |

*   **Note:** If the solvency ratio is $1:1$, it implies that the owner's equity is $0$. Based on the accounting equation $A = OE + L$, a low ratio (e.g., $1:1$) indicates potential financial problems.

---

## 2. Risk and Gearing
Risk and gearing measure a business's financial leverage, indicating the proportion of equity (own capital) versus debt (borrowed capital) used to finance assets.

| Concept | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Debt–equity ratio** | $\text{Non-current liabilities} : \text{Owner's equity}$ |
| **Norm** | Anything below $1:1$ |
| **Low risk** | Higher equity, lower debt |
| **High risk** | Higher debt, lower equity |
| **Gearing** | Comparison between cost of borrowed money and return on equity |
| **Positive gearing** | High return, lower interest rate percentage |
| **Negative gearing** | High interest rate percentage, lower return |

---

## 3. Return on Investment
The primary aim of a business is to generate profit. Investors evaluate if the business earns more than alternative investments.

| Concept | Formula / Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Partner's earnings** | $\text{Interest on capital} + \text{Salary} + \text{Bonus} + \text{Share of remaining profit}$ |
| **Partner's owner's equity** | $\text{Capital of partner} + \text{Current account of partner}$ |
| **Return on partner's equity** | $\frac{\text{Earnings} \times 100}{\text{Average owner's equity of partner}}$ |
| **Average owner's equity** | $\frac{1}{2} (\text{Owner's equity at beginning} + \text{Owner's equity at end})$ |
| **Return on equity for business** | $\frac{\text{Net profit for year} \times 100}{\text{Average total equity}}$ |

## 4. Instructional Notes & Calculations

### Key Concepts
*   **Interest on Capital:** Calculated as:
    $$\text{Interest} = \text{Capital Balance} \times \text{Rate} \times \frac{\text{Time}}{12}$$
*   **Liquidity Ratios:**
    *   **Current Ratio:** $\frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$
    *   **Acid Test Ratio:** $\frac{\text{Current Assets} - \text{Inventory}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$
*   **Solvency Ratios:**
    *   **Solvency Ratio:** $\frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Total Liabilities}}$
    *   **Debt-Equity Ratio:** $\frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Equity}}$

*   **Insurance:** The insurance company refused to pay for the damages.

---

### Exercises

#### 9. Did the business make a profit or loss during this incident? Show calculations to prove your answer.

To determine the profit or loss, we compare the Carrying Value (Book Value) of the asset at the time of the accident to the amount recovered (the scrap value).

**Calculation:**
$$\text{Profit/Loss} = \text{Scrap Value} - \text{Carrying Value}$$

*   If the Carrying Value was greater than $R20\,000$, the business incurred a **loss** on the disposal/write-off of the asset.
*   If the Carrying Value was less than $R20\,000$, the business realized a **profit**.

*Note: In accounting practice, since the asset was destroyed and insurance refused the claim, the business effectively writes off the remaining carrying value as an expense (Loss on disposal of asset).*

#### 10. Would the above transaction have any effect on the percentage return earned by Morris in the next year? Explain.

Yes, it will have an effect. The percentage return on capital is calculated as:

$$\text{Percentage Return} = \frac{\text{Net Profit}}{\text{Average Capital}} \times 100$$

*   **Effect on Net Profit:** The loss incurred from the bakkie incident reduces the total net profit of the partnership for the year. Since Morris is a partner, his share of the profit will be lower.
*   **Effect on Capital:** If Morris pays $R20\,000$ into the business, his capital contribution increases, which changes the denominator in the return calculation.

#### 11. If you were a partner or the accountant of this business, what comments would you make about this incident and its treatment in the books? Briefly explain two points.

1.  **Internal Control and Ethics:** The incident highlights a failure in internal controls regarding the security of business assets. Allowing an unlicensed family member to use a business vehicle exposes the partnership to significant financial and legal risk.
2.  **Accountability:** It is appropriate for Morris to compensate the business for the scrap value. However, as a partner, he should ideally be held accountable for the full loss (the difference between the original carrying value and the scrap value) because the loss was caused by his personal negligence, not by normal business operations. This prevents the other partners from unfairly subsidizing his personal loss.

**2.1 Give two reasons why the net profit of a partnership is sometimes not distributed equally.**

**2.2 What does a debit balance on a partner’s current account mean?**
*   A debit balance indicates that the partner has withdrawn more money (drawings) than their share of the net profit and other entitlements (like salary or interest on capital) for the period. It represents an amount owed by the partner to the partnership.

**2.3 A partner takes trading stock for personal use. Is the transaction recorded as division of profit or as drawings?**
*   It is recorded as **drawings**. Taking stock for personal use reduces the partner's equity in the business and is treated as a withdrawal of assets, not a distribution of profit.

**2.4 Does the partnership pay income tax? Give a reason for your answer.**
*   No, the partnership itself does not pay income tax. Partnerships are not separate legal entities for tax purposes; instead, the individual partners are taxed on their share of the partnership's profit in their personal capacities.

**2.5 A partnership suffered a loss before appropriation of salaries and interest on capital. Are the salaries and interest on capital still brought into account?**
*   Yes, they are still brought into account. The appropriation section of the statement is used to distribute the loss (or profit) among the partners according to the partnership agreement, which usually includes the allocation of salaries and interest on capital even when a loss is incurred.

## Conceptual Understanding
### 2.6 Why are partner salaries not recorded in the Income Statement?
Partners are the owners of the business, not employees. An employee provides labour in exchange for a wage/salary, which is an expense to the business. A partner provides capital and management; their "salary" is an appropriation (distribution) of the profit they have generated, rather than a cost of doing business. Therefore, partner salaries are recorded in the **Appropriation Account**, not the Income Statement.

## Key Concepts and Formulas

To analyze the liquidity and solvency of a partnership, use the following financial indicators:

### 1. Liquidity Ratios
Liquidity measures the ability of the business to meet its short-term obligations.

*   **Current Ratio:** Measures the ability to pay short-term debts using current assets.
    $$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} : 1$$

*   **Acid Test Ratio:** A stricter measure of liquidity that excludes inventory (which is less liquid).
    $$\text{Acid Test Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets} - \text{Inventory}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} : 1$$

### 2. Solvency Ratio
Solvency measures the long-term financial stability of the business.

*   **Debt-Equity Ratio:** Measures the proportion of debt used to finance the business compared to the owners' equity.
    $$\text{Debt-Equity Ratio} = \frac{\text{Non-current Liabilities}}{\text{Total Equity}} : 1$$
