# Introduction to Accounting

## Basic Principles of Accounting
The primary objective of any business is to generate profit. Businesses utilize funds to acquire assets, which in turn facilitate the generation of income through business operations. These funds are sourced either from the owners (Owner's Equity) or from external sources (Liabilities).

### The Accounting Equation
The relationship between assets, equity, and liabilities is expressed by the fundamental accounting equation:

$$\text{ASSETS} = \text{OWNER'S EQUITY} + \text{LIABILITIES}$$
$$A = OE + L$$

**Purpose of Accounting:**
Accounting provides structured information to owners and managers, allowing them to evaluate whether business operations have resulted in a profit or a loss over a specific period. Standardizing this information ensures consistency across businesses and accounting periods, facilitating easier analysis and informed decision-making.

---

## GAAP Concepts
Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) provide the guidelines for recording and presenting financial information.

### Core GAAP Concepts
*   **Historical cost concept:** Assets are recorded at their original purchase price; these values are not adjusted for changes in market value.
*   **Prudence concept:** When a transaction can be recorded in multiple ways, the most conservative approach must be chosen.
*   **Materiality concept:** If an amount or transaction is insignificant and does not impact decision-making, strict accounting principles may be ignored for that specific item.
*   **Business entity concept:** The financial transactions of the business must be kept strictly separate from the personal financial transactions of the owner.
*   **Going concern concept:** It is assumed that the business will continue its operations into the foreseeable future to realize its goals.
*   **Matching concept:** Expenses incurred to generate income must be recorded in the same financial period as the income they helped to earn.

### Additional GAAP Concepts
*   **Consistency concept:** The same accounting methods must be applied consistently within a financial period and from one year to the next.
*   **Accrual basis:** Profit for a specific period is calculated by accounting for all values earned (income) and all values used (expenses) within that same period, regardless of when cash is actually received or paid.

# The Accounting Cycle

The accounting cycle is the fundamental process applied to every business to record, process, and report financial information.



![StudyGuide_Accounting_Gr11_Disposal-of-tangible-assets.pdf-0001-01.png](images/StudyGuide_Accounting_Gr11_Disposal-of-tangible-assets.pdf-0001-01.png)


## The Accounting Process Flow
1. **Transactions:** The initial economic events occurring in the business.
2. **Source Documents:** Evidence created to provide proof of each transaction.
3. **Subsidiary Journals:** Books of prime entry where similar transactions are recorded to simplify the posting process.
4. **Posting to Ledgers:** Transferring information from journals to the General Ledger and Subsidiary Ledgers.
5. **Trial Balance:** A summary of all ledger account balances to ensure the accounting equation ($Assets = Equity + Liabilities$) remains in balance.
6. **Final Accounts and Financial Statements:** The preparation of reports (e.g., Income Statement, Balance Sheet) to determine the financial position and performance of the business.

---

## Source Documents and Subsidiary Journals
Source documents provide the necessary information to record transactions accurately. The following table outlines the relationship between transactions, their corresponding source documents, and the appropriate subsidiary journals.

| Transaction | Source Document | Subsidiary Journal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| All money received | Duplicate receipt, Cash register roll, Duplicate deposit slip, EFT/Direct deposit, Credits on bank statement | Cash Receipts Journal (CRJ) |
| All payments made | Cheque counterfoil (Cheques issued), EFT (Electronic funds transfer), Debits on bank statement | Cash Payments Journal (CPJ) |
| All payments from petty cash | Petty cash voucher | Petty Cash Journal (PCJ) |
| Credit sales of merchandise | Duplicate invoice (Invoices issued) | Debtors Journal (DJ) |
| Returns by debtors or allowances to debtors | Original credit note (Credit note issued) | Debtors Allowances Journal (DAJ) |
| Credit purchases of merchandise and other assets/services | Original invoice (Invoices received) | Creditors Journal (CJ) |
| Returns to creditors or allowances received | Original debit note (Debit note issued) | Creditors Allowances Journal (CAJ) |
| Transactions that do not belong in other journals | Journal narration or Journal voucher | General Journal (GJ) |
| Calculation of salaries and wages | Staff register and contracts | Salaries Journal (SJ), Wages Journal (WJ) |

### Ledger Accounts and the Double Entry System

Subsidiary journals provide a record of transactions, but they do not provide enough information to calculate profit or loss. To do this, information is posted to **ledger accounts**, where all changes in the value of assets, liabilities, owner's equity, income, and expenses are recorded.

#### The Double Entry System
The business uses the **double entry system** to record information. Every transaction involves at least two accounts:
*   If you **debit** one account, you must **credit** another.
*   **Debit entries** are recorded on the left side of the T-account.
*   **Credit entries** are recorded on the right side of the T-account.



![StudyGuide_Accounting_Gr11_Calculations-involving-percentages.pdf-0002-06.png](images/StudyGuide_Accounting_Gr11_Calculations-involving-percentages.pdf-0002-06.png)

### The Accounting Equation
The accounting equation determines which side of an account (Debit or Credit) is used to record an increase or decrease:

$$\text{ASSETS} = \text{OWNER'S EQUITY} + \text{LIABILITIES}$$

| Account Type | Debit (Dr.) | Credit (Cr.) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Assets** | $+$ | $-$ |
| **Owner's Equity** | $-$ | $+$ |
| **Liabilities** | $-$ | $+$ |



![Textbook_Accounting_Gr11_Value-added-tax.pdf-0002-00.png](images/Textbook_Accounting_Gr11_Value-added-tax.pdf-0002-00.png)

### Owner's Equity
Owner's equity represents the amount the business owes to the owner or the owner's interest in the business. Since the main aim of a business is to make a profit, the owners (who provide the funds and take the risk) are entitled to that profit.

#### Profit Calculation Formulas
Profit is calculated in two stages:

1.  $$\text{Gross profit} = (\text{Sales} - \text{Debtors allowances}) - \text{Cost of sales}$$
2.  $$\text{Net profit} = \text{Gross profit} + \text{Income} - \text{Expenses}$$

#### Effect on Owner's Equity
*   **Sales and Income:** These increase profit, which in turn increases owner's equity.
*   **Cost of sales and Expenses:** These decrease profit, which in turn decreases owner's equity.

### Ledger Entry Rules
The following table summarizes how different account types are affected by debit and credit entries:

| Account Type | Account to debit | Account to credit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Assets | Increase | Decrease |
| Owner's equity | Decrease | Increase |
| Income/Sales/Profit | Decrease | Increase |
| Expenses/Cost of sales/Loss | Increase | Decrease |
| Liabilities | Decrease | Increase |

---

### General Ledger Sections
Accounts in the General Ledger are categorized into two sections based on their purpose in financial reporting:

| Section | Accounts | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Balance Sheet accounts section | Capital, Drawings, Assets, Liabilities | To determine financial position of the business in the financial statements (Balance Sheet) |
| Nominal accounts section | Sales, Cost of sales, Income, Expenses | To determine the result of operations (profit or loss) in the financial statements (Income Statement) |

---

### The Trial Balance
A **Trial Balance** is a list of all General Ledger accounts and their respective balances. 

*   **Purpose:** It proves that the total amount of debit balances is equal to the total amount of credit balances.
*   **Structure:** Accounts appear in the same sections and order as they do in the General Ledger.
*   **Application:** At the end of an accounting period, the Trial Balance is used to prepare the **financial statements**, which reveal the business's profit or loss (Income Statement) and its financial position (Balance Sheet).

---

### Forms of Ownership
The accounting cycle and the accounting equation apply to all business entities, including sole traders, partnerships, and companies. 

*   **Core Principle:** Owners are entitled to the profit, and owner's equity represents the amount the business owes to the owner(s) (the owners' interest in the business).
*   **Distinctions:** The primary differences between forms of ownership lie in the distribution of profit and the structure of the owner's equity.
*   **Curriculum Note:** In Grade 11, the focus is specifically on **sole traders** and **partnerships**.

### Structure of Owner's Equity

The structure of owner's equity differs based on the form of business ownership. The following table outlines these structures:

| Form of ownership | Structure of owner's equity |
| :--- | :--- |
| Sole trader | $\text{Capital} + \text{Net profit} - \text{Drawings}$ |
| Partnership | $\text{Capital} + \text{Current accounts of partners (Earnings} - \text{Drawings)}$ |

### Financial Statements

Financial statements are the formal records of the financial activities and position of a business.

#### Purpose of Financial Statements
*   **Organising Information:** Businesses must sort and organise transaction data into a format that is meaningful to external and internal users.
*   **Reporting Period:** Financial statements represent the transactions that occurred during a specific financial year.
*   **Business Analysis:** These statements act as the 'story' of the business. By reviewing them, owners can analyse the financial performance of the business and make informed, strategic decisions.
