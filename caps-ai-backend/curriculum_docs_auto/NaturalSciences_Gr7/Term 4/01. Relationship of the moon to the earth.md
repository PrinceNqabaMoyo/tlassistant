> **DID YOU KNOW?**
> Humanity got its first view of the far side of the Moon in 1959 when the Soviet Union launched the small spacecraft Luna 3. This was the first probe to get to the far side of the Moon and photograph it.

The Earth, just like all the other planets in the solar system, travels around the Sun, completing one revolution every year. As the Earth travels around the Sun it has a companion in space: our Moon!

> **TAKE NOTE**
> The time that it takes an object to make one complete orbit around another object, relative to the stars, is called the orbital period or synodic period.

The Moon orbits around the Earth completing one revolution every $27.3$ days. Our Moon rotates on its own axis and experiences daytime and dark nighttime just like the Earth does. However, the Moon spins much more slowly than the Earth does and completes one rotation on its axis once every $27.3$ days. Did you notice that the Moon takes the same amount of time to spin on its axis as it does to orbit completely around Earth? This means that from the Earth, we always see the same side of the Moon (called the 'nearside'). The side we do not see from Earth, called the 'farside', has been mapped during space missions to the Moon.

Viewed from above, the Moon moves in an anti-clockwise direction around the Earth. The Moon's orbit is not a perfect circle, it is elliptical, so its distance from Earth varies as it revolves around the Earth. The average distance is about $385\,000\text{ km}$, which is about $60$ times the radius of the Earth itself. For comparison, the Earth's average distance from the Sun is $149\,597\,871\text{ km}$, or about $23\,481$ times the radius of the Earth. You can see now why the Moon is called Earth's close companion!

# Chapter 2. Relationship of the Moon to the Earth

## Lesson Notes: Motions of the Earth, Moon, and Sun

### 1. Core Concepts

* **Earth's Motion Around the Sun:** 
  The Earth revolves in an orbit around the Sun. (Note: Diagrams representing astronomical bodies and distances are often not drawn to scale; the actual distances and sizes of the Sun, Earth, and Moon are significantly larger than what can be shown in a standard image).

* **The Moon's Motion Around the Earth:** 
  The Moon revolves in an orbit around the Earth while the Earth itself travels around the Sun.

* **Rotation vs. Revolution of the Moon:**
  * The Moon spins on its own axis at the exact same rate that it revolves around the Earth.
  * As the Moon completes $\frac{1}{4}$ of a turn on its axis, it simultaneously completes $\frac{1}{4}$ of its orbit around the Earth.
  * **Result:** Because of this synchronized rotation and revolution, the same side of the Moon always faces the Earth. Observers on Earth never see the "far side" of the Moon directly from the surface.

---

### 2. Summary of Key Orbital Characteristics

| Astronomical Body | Motion Type | Path / Reference | Primary Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Earth** | Revolution | Around the Sun | Changes in seasons / yearly cycle |
| **Moon** | Revolution | Around the Earth | Lunar phases as viewed from Earth |
| **Moon** | Rotation | On its own axis | The same side of the Moon always faces Earth |

---

* **Visualizing Scale:** Consider why scaling is a challenge in astronomy. If the Sun's diameter is represented by a scale factor $S$, calculate the relative distance to Earth using the scale ratio:
  $$\text{Scaled Distance} = \frac{\text{Actual Distance}}{\text{Actual Sun Diameter}} \times S$$

* **Further Investigation:**
  * Explore why humans only ever observe one side of the Moon from Earth.

# Chapter 2. Relationship of the Moon to the Earth

## Core Concepts: Angular Size and Solar Eclipses

* **Angular Size:** When viewed from the Earth's surface, the Moon and the Sun appear to be very similar in size. This apparent equal size is known as their **angular size**.
* **Total Solar Eclipse:** A total solar eclipse occurs when the Earth, Moon, and Sun are aligned in a straight line, with the Moon placed directly in between the Earth and the Sun. Because the Moon is much closer to Earth than the Sun, it appears large enough to completely block out the Sun's light, allowing us a glimpse of the Sun's outer corona.
* **Coincidence of Distance:** By pure chance, the Sun and Moon are currently at distances from Earth such that they share almost identical angular sizes as viewed from the Earth's surface. If the Moon's angular size were smaller, it would not be large enough to completely block out the Sun.

---

## Questions for Revision

1. **Which in reality is larger, the Moon or the Sun?**
   * *Answer:* The Sun is vastly larger in reality.

2. **Which is further away, the Moon or the Sun?**
   * *Answer:* The Sun is much further away from the Earth than the Moon.

3. **How do the angular sizes of the Moon and the Sun compare when viewed from the Earth's surface?**
   * *Answer:* They are very similar, appearing almost identical in size from our perspective on Earth.

4. **Why is this the case?**
   * *Answer:* Although the Sun is much larger than the Moon, it is also correspondingly much further away. This exact proportion makes their apparent angular sizes from Earth nearly identical.

## 2.2 Gravity

### New Words
* **Gravity**: The natural phenomenon by which all things with mass or energy are brought toward one another.
* **Mass**: The amount of matter in an object, usually measured in kilograms ($\text{kg}$).
* **Weight**: The force exerted on an object's mass by gravity ($W = mg$).
* **Acceleration due to gravity**: The acceleration gained by an object due to gravitational force, approximately $9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$ on Earth's surface.
* **Gravitational force**: The attractive force existing between any two objects that have mass.

---

### Core Concepts

* **Definition**: The word **gravity** describes the **gravitational pull (force)** an object experiences on or near the surface of a planet or moon. 
* **Universal Attraction**: The gravitational force is a force that attracts objects with mass towards each other. *Any object with mass exerts a gravitational force on any other object with mass*. 
* For example, the Earth exerts a gravitational pull on you, your desk, your chair, and falling objects, holding everything on the surface and preventing us from drifting off into space.

---

### Properties of Gravitational Force

1. **Centerward Direction**: The Earth's gravity pulls everything down towards the centre of the Earth. When you drop an object like a book or an apple, it falls directly to the ground.
2. **Mutual Pull**: Every object with mass exerts an equal and opposite pull on the Earth. (Note: The falling apple exerts a pull on the Earth, but the Earth does not move noticeably because its mass is immensely larger than the apple's).
3. **Distance Dependence**: The gravitational force between two objects decreases as the objects move further apart. 
   * If you **double** the distance between two objects, the gravitational force decreases by a factor of four ($2^2 = 4$).
   * If you **triple** the distance between two objects, the gravitational force decreases by a factor of nine ($3^2 = 9$).

---

### Astronomical Context

* **Sun vs. Earth Gravity**: The Sun is $333\,000$ times more massive than the Earth, meaning its gravity is much stronger than the Earth's. However, because we are so far away from the Sun, the gravitational force the Sun exerts on us is much smaller than the gravitational force the Earth exerts on us.
* **Orbits**: The Moon is held in orbit around the Earth by the gravitational force between the Earth and the Moon. Similarly, the Sun's gravity holds the Earth in orbit around the Sun.

# Revision Notes: Gravity, Mass, and Weight

## Core Concepts of Gravitational Force

All components in the Universe are held together by gravity. The strength of the gravitational pull between objects depends on two main factors:
1. **Mass:** The greater the mass of the objects, the stronger the gravitational pull between them.
2. **Distance:** The closer objects are to one another, the stronger the gravitational pull between them.

---

## Mass vs. Weight

In everyday language, mass and weight are often confused, but in physics and science, they have distinct definitions and properties.

### 1. Mass
* **Definition:** The amount of matter in an object; it tells you how many particles or atoms are present.
* **Unit of Measurement:** Kilograms ($\text{kg}$).
* **Properties:** Mass is **independent of location**—it remains constant regardless of where you measure it in the Universe.
* **Example:** A wooden block with a mass of $10\text{ kg}$ on Earth also has a mass of $10\text{ kg}$ on the Moon.

### 2. Weight
* **Definition:** The force acting on an object due to gravity. It depends on both the mass of the object and the strength of the local gravitational field.
* **Unit of Measurement:** Newtons ($\text{N}$).
* **Properties:** Weight **can change** depending on where the object is measured.
* **Calculation/Relationship:** On the surface of the Earth, gravity exerts a gravitational force of about $10\text{ N}$ for every kilogram of mass:
  $$\text{Weight} \approx \text{Mass} \times 10\text{ N/kg}$$
* **Example:** A person with a mass of $50\text{ kg}$ has a weight of:
  $$50\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ N/kg} = 500\text{ N}$$
  on the surface of the Earth.

---

## Gravity Across Different Celestial Bodies

The strength of gravity varies depending on the size and mass of the planet or moon:

* **The Moon:** The strength of gravity on the surface of the Moon is $\frac{1}{6}$ (one-sixth) that of the Earth. Therefore, an object weighs six times less on the Moon than it does on Earth.
* **Jupiter:** The strength of gravity on Jupiter is $2.5$ times greater than that of the Earth. Therefore, an object weighs $2.5$ times more on Jupiter than on Earth.

### Summary Comparison Table

| Celestial Body | Gravitational Strength relative to Earth | Mass of Astronaut | Weight of Astronaut ($120\text{ kg}$ mass) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Earth** | $1\times$ (Standard) | $120\text{ kg}$ | $1200\text{ N}$ |
| **Moon** | $\frac{1}{6}\times$ | $120\text{ kg}$ | $200\text{ N}$ |

> **Key Takeaway:** An astronaut's **mass remains the same** wherever it is measured (e.g., $120\text{ kg}$), but the astronaut's **weight depends on location** (e.g., $1200\text{ N}$ on Earth vs. $200\text{ N}$ on the Moon).

### The Moon's gravity

As you have already discovered, the Moon, like any other planet or moon, has its own gravity. The strength of gravity on the surface of the Moon is one-sixth that of the Earth, and so on the Moon you would weigh one-sixth of what you do on Earth. Due to the weak gravity on the Moon, you would be able to jump six times higher than usual! The astronauts had to learn to walk in strange ways (such as leaping or hopping) to move about on the surface of the Moon.

As we will find out in the next section, the Moon's gravity not only affects humans walking on the Moon, but also influences the Earth.

# Lesson Notes: Understanding Tides

## 1. Core Concepts

* **Low Tide:** Following high tide, the water level gradually drops until it reaches its lowest level on the beach.
* **High Tide:** Following low tide, the water gradually comes further up on the beach until it reaches its highest level.
* **Tidal Cycle:** This pattern of receding and advancing water repeats over and over again.
* **Frequency:** In general, there are **two low tides** and **two high tides** per day on the sea, which can be observed on beaches or in estuaries.
* **Timing:** The times of high and low tides are not exactly the same every day; they occur roughly **one hour later each day**.
* **Tidal Range:** The vertical difference between low tide and high tide.

---

## 2. Did You Know?

* Some lakes and rivers also have tides!

---

## 3. Practical Applications of Tide Information

Tides can be predicted, and low and high tide times are published in tide tables. 

* **Fishermen:** Use tide information to plan when they will fish.
* **Surfers:** Use this information to plan the best times to go surfing, as each beach has a particular time when the sea level is optimal for producing excellent surfing waves.

# Lesson Revision Notes: Tides, Spring Tides, and Neap Tides

## 1. Core Concepts: Daily Tide Cycles

* **High Tides and Low Tides:** At any given coastal location, you experience **two high tides** and **two low tides** per day.
* **Timing of Tides:** 
  * One high tide occurs when the Moon is directly above your location.
  * The second high tide occurs roughly **twelve hours later** when the Moon is directly opposite your location on Earth.
* **Cause of Daily Tides:** This predictable cycle happens because the Earth spins on its axis once every $24$ hours.

---

## 2. Factors Affecting Tide Heights

* **Constant Mass of the Moon:** The Moon has the same amount of mass throughout its orbit, meaning it exerts the **same gravitational pull** during all phases. Therefore, changes in tidal heights are *not* caused by a changing gravitational pull from the Moon itself.
* **Relative Alignment:** Variations in tidal heights (such as very high or moderate tides) are caused by the **combined gravitational effects and relative alignment of the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon**.

---

# Chapter 2. Relationship of the Moon to the Earth

## Lesson Revision Notes: Tides, Moon Phases, and Gravitational Pull

---

### Core Concepts

* **Spring Tides:** When the Sun, Moon, and Earth are lined up in a straight line (occurring at the time of a **New Moon** or **Full Moon**), the gravitational pull of the Sun adds to the gravitational pull of the Moon. This creates extra-high high tides and very low low tides, resulting in the maximum difference in height between low and high tide.
* **Neap Tides:** When the Sun and Moon are positioned at right angles to each other (occurring during the **first quarter** and **third quarter** moon phases), the Sun's gravitational pull partially cancels out the Moon's gravitational pull, resulting in smaller tides.

---

### Did You Know?

* Earth's only natural satellite is simply called **the Moon** because people did not know other moons existed until Galileo Galilei discovered four moons orbiting Jupiter in 1610. 
* Other moons in our solar system are given names so they won't be confused with each other.

# Lesson Revision Notes: Tides and Shoreline Ecosystems

## Core Concepts

### Tides and the Moon
* The Moon's gravitational pull creates oceanic tides.
* **Neap Tides:** Occur when the difference in height between the low and high tide is at its minimum.
* The Moon contributes more to the Earth's tides than the Sun because it is much closer to Earth. If there were no Moon, Earth's tides would be about one-third of their current height.
* *Note on Orbit:* The Moon's orbit is gradually increasing, meaning the Moon is slowly moving away from Earth. Consequently, ancient tides were much higher than they are today, and they will continue to decrease in the future.

---

### The Effects of Tides on Shoreline Ecosystems

* **Intertidal Zone:** The region of the beach located between the high tide and low tide levels. It represents a harsh environment for marine plants and animals.
* **Environmental Challenges in the Intertidal Zone:**
  * **Wave Action:** During storms, heavy surf can batter organisms and wash them away unless they can withstand the force.
  * **Exposure to Air and Temperature Extremes:** Organisms are submerged underwater during high tide but exposed to air during low tide. They face hot summer temperatures and cold winter temperatures, requiring adaptations to survive wide thermal ranges (though some stay underwater in small rock pools that do not empty when the tide goes out).
  * **Salinity Fluctuations:** Animals exposed to air at low tide may be soaked in fresh rainwater, yet they are submerged in salty seawater at high tide. They must tolerate rapidly changing salt concentrations.

---

### Adaptations of Intertidal Organisms

Different organisms have developed unique adaptations to survive this tough environment:
* **Crabs:** Burrow into the damp sand to hide during low tide.
* **Kelp and other seaweeds:** Covered with a thick layer of slime to prevent them from drying out when exposed to the air.

---

## Vocabulary / New Words

* **Ecosystem:** A biological community of interacting organisms and their physical environment.
* **Intertidal zone:** The area of the shoreline that is above water at low tide and submerged under water at high tide.

# Revision Notes: Organisms in the Intertidal Zone

## Core Concepts & Observations

The intertidal zone is the area that is above water at low tide and underwater at high tide. Organisms living in this environment face harsh conditions, including extreme temperature fluctuations, wave action, and changing salinity levels.

*   **Mussels:** Grow in dense clusters on rocks to withstand strong wave action.
*   **Crabs:** Often hide in the sand or under rocks to protect themselves from predators and harsh sunlight during low tide.
*   **Seals:** Use the intertidal zone to rest, breed, and raise pups while navigating dynamic water conditions.
*   **Mudskippers:** Unique fish adapted to walk or skip on land using modified pectoral fins.

## Did You Know?

Sea anemones look like plants with flowers, but they are actually animals. Their tentacles contain a poison that paralyses their food (small fish and shrimps) when touched.

#### Key Concepts
* The Moon orbits the Earth once every $27.3$ days. The Moon also spins on its own axis once every $27.3$ days. Due to both these time periods being the same, we only ever see one side of the Moon from Earth.
* Gravity is a force that acts between all objects with mass. The size of the force acting on the objects is proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to their distance from each other.
* The Earth's gravity is responsible for holding the Moon in orbit around the Earth.
* The Moon's gravitational pull is mainly responsible for the tides on Earth.
* Neap tides occur when the Sun and Moon are at $90^\circ$ to each other.
* Spring tides occur when the Sun and Moon are in line with each other.
* The rise and fall of the tides affects marine life living along shorelines. They have adapted to this harsh environment in many ways to prevent themselves from drying out and from being washed away by strong waves.

## 1. Orbital Relationships
* **Moon is Earth's Natural Satellite:** The Moon revolves around the Earth, and is held in an orbit around the Earth.
* **Earth revolves around the Sun:** The Earth revolves around the Sun, and is held in an orbit around the Sun.

## 2. Gravity and Attraction
* **Gravity:** Gravity is an attraction (pull) between all objects.
* **Factors affecting gravitational force:**
  * **Mass of objects:** If objects are the same distance apart, then an increase in mass results in a stronger pull.
  * **Distance between objects:** If objects have the same mass, the distance between them affects how strongly they attract one another.

## 3. The Moon, Earth, and Tides
* **Gravity by the Sun and Earth:** Gravity is exerted by the Sun on the Earth, and by the Earth on the Sun.
* **Moon's gravitational pull on Earth:** The Moon pulls on water on Earth.
* **Result:** This gravitational pull results in **tides**.

# 16 Relationship of the Moon to the Earth

## Chapter Overview
* **Duration:** 2 weeks
* **Prior Knowledge:** In Grade 4, learners covered basic facts about the Moon (lack of air and water, size relative to the Earth, position relative to the Sun, and moon phases). In Grade 6, learners studied the Moon's motion in space (revolving around the Earth while rotating on its spin axis).
* **Core Concepts:** 
  * Phases of the Moon related to the relative positions of the Earth, Moon, and Sun.
  * The concept of gravity and the influence of the Moon's and Sun's gravitational pulls on the Earth's oceans resulting in tides.

### Main Aims
* The Moon is smaller than the Earth and orbits around the Earth in $27.3$ days as the Earth revolves around the Sun.
* The Moon is held in orbit around the Earth by the force of gravity. Similarly, the Earth and all other planets in the solar system are held in orbit around the Sun by the force of gravity.
* All masses experience the force of gravity, and the size of the force exerted depends upon the mass of the objects and their distance from each other.
* The combined gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun on the Earth's oceans causes ocean tides.

## A note about lunar phases

Learners will most likely be familiar with the change in the Moon's appearance over the course of a month, the lunar phases. Each lunar phase cycle from New Moon to New Moon takes $29.5$ days, which is slightly longer than the Moon takes to complete one revolution around the Earth ($27.3$ days). This is because during the $27.3$ days it takes for the Moon to revolve around the Earth, the Earth is moving along in its orbit. In order for the Moon to appear at the same phase as viewed by an observer on Earth it needs to travel slightly further than $360$ degrees around the Earth and in order for it to be aligned such that there is a New Moon it takes about an extra $2$ days.

`bit.ly/16C8LQB` shows a nice animation demonstrating the difference between the orbital period of the Moon which defines the sidereal month ($27.3$ days) and the lunar phase cycle which defines the synodic month ($29.5$ days).

Observers on Earth see the same phase wherever they are positioned on Earth. However, the phases (apart from New Moon and Full Moon) look different to an observer in the Northern and Southern hemispheres. We in the Southern Hemisphere view the Moon "upside down". This is important to note because $99\%$ of all textbooks and online references include the Northern Hemisphere view of the phases and this is not what learners will see when they view the Moon for themselves. An awareness of this is crucial to reduce learner confusion when looking online and in generic textbooks.

# Investigating the Phases of the Moon

## Overview & Background
In this activity, you will model the Moon in its orbit around the Earth and observe the changing phases of the Moon. 

* **The Sun:** Represented by an unshaded lamp placed in the centre of the room.
* **The Earth:** Represented by the person holding the pencil and paper.
* **The Moon:** Represented by the styrofoam ball on a stick.

> **Note on Southern Hemisphere Observations:** The first and third quarter appearances listed below are specific to the Southern Hemisphere.

### Key Moon Phases
* **New Moon:** Entirely dark
* **Full Moon:** Entirely lit
* **Crescent Moon:** Mostly dark (less than half lit)
* **Gibbous Moon:** Mostly lit
* **First Quarter Moon:** Left half lit
* **Third Quarter Moon:** Right half lit

---

## Materials Needed
* Pencil ($2$ per pair)
* One lamp that can shine in all directions (a lamp base with a bare $100$ to $150\text{ Watt}$ bulb and no lampshade)
* Styrofoam balls ($1$ per pair)
* Black plastic bags (and tape) or curtains to darken the classroom
* Sheet of paper ($1$ per pair)

---

## Instructions

1. **Work in pairs:** One learner holds the ball in place while the other draws observations. 
2. **Set up the Sun:** Place the lamp in the centre of the room. Use only one bright lamp to avoid unwanted shadowing effects.
3. **Darken the room:** Use dark plastic bags taped to the windows or close the curtains.
4. **Prepare the Moon:** Stick one of the pencils into your styrofoam ball so you can hold it up by the pencil end.
5. **Form a circle:** Learners must stand in a circle around the central light, with partners next to each other.
6. **Position yourselves:** Directly face the light. One partner holds the ball at arm's length, slightly above their head (representing the Earth). Ensure the ball is held high enough so it does not cast shadows over the ball. *Note: We assume the Earth remains in the same spot while the Moon orbits around it.*
7. **Observe illumination:** If you are holding the ball, move it slowly from left to right (keeping your body still) and observe how much of it is lit by the light. Swap roles with your partner to try it as well.
8. **Reference the board:** Look at the different phases of the Moon drawn on the blackboard (New Moon, Full Moon, First Quarter, Third Quarter).
9. **Model New Moon:** Position the ball until, from your point of view, it looks completely in shadow. This represents a **New Moon**.
10. **Record observations:** The partner not holding the ball should draw the relative positions of the Sun, Moon, and Earth, and write down the corresponding Moon phase.
11. **Model Full Moon:** Swap roles and position the ball such that it is fully lit, looking like a **Full Moon**. Record the relative positions.
12. **Repeat for all phases:** Complete the activity for all the phases listed in step 8.
13. **Find the Crescent:** Look at the crescent moon on the blackboard and determine what positions you can place the ball to see it lit up like a crescent.

## Tides and Shoreline Ecosystems

### Understanding Tides: Key Concepts and Observations

Ensure that learners can still find the sides of low and high tides even though they are less extreme.

1. Two
2. Under the Moon and on the side of the Earth directly opposite the Moon.
3. They remain under the Moon and directly opposite the Moon.
4. The high and low tide are on different parts of the surface of Earth now.
5. The Sun.
6. Yes, but the difference between high and low tide would not be as extreme. Since the Sun is so far away, the Sun's gravitational pull would give the tides only a third of their height.

In this activity, we have ignored the motion of the Moon revolving around the Earth. The time of high tide changes each day because the Moon is moving around the Earth. If we had no Moon, the tide due to the Sun alone would occur at essentially the same time every day. This activity also ignores the friction between the water and solid Earth as it spins, which causes the tidal bulges (piled up ocean bulges) to lie ahead of the Earth-Moon line in the direction of the Earth's rotation.

The actual times of low and high tides at a particular place on Earth are influenced not only by the Earth's spin but also by the Moon's motion around the Earth in its orbit. As the Earth spins, the Moon also travels around the Earth. The Moon rises about an hour later each day, and high (and low) tides also occur roughly an hour later each day.
