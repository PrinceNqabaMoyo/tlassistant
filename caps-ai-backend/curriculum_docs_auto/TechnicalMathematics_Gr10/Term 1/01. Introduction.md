# Introduction to accounting

## Basic principles of accounting

The main aim of any business is to make a profit. The business uses funds to buy the assets that will help the business to generate income in its business operations. The owners can provide the funds (owners' equity) or the funds can come from other sources (liabilities). This leads to the accounting equation:

$$\text{ASSETS} = \text{OWNER'S EQUITY} + \text{LIABILITIES}$$
$$A = OE + L$$

Owners or managers need information to be able to see if their business operations for a specific period have resulted in a profit or loss. The purpose of Accounting is to make the necessary information available in a structured manner. The structure of the information must be the same for every business and also for each accounting period. This makes it easier to understand and analyse the information and forms a basis for decisions.

## GAAP concepts

The Generally Accepted Accounting Principles/Practices provide some guidelines for setting out the information. The following six concepts apply:

* **Historical cost concept:** Assets are recorded at the original cost and the values are not changed as market value changes.
* **Prudence concept:** When a transaction can be recorded in more than one way, it should be recorded in the more conservative manner.
* **Materiality concept:** If an amount or transaction is insignificant or it will make no real difference, principles could be ignored.
* **Business entity concept:** The transactions of the business are kept separate from the personal transactions of the owner.
* **Going concern concept:** We assume that the business will continue with operations to realise its goal.
* **Matching concept:** Expenses paid to earn income are recorded in the same period as the income earned.

Other concepts that GAAP identifies in this group:

* **Consistency concept:** Use the same method of accounting within a given period and year after year.
* **Accrual basis:** Treat the values earned during a specific period as well as values used in a specific period as income and expenses to calculate the profit for that period.

## Role of Subsidiary Journals and Ledger Accounts

* **Subsidiary Journals:** Do not provide enough information on their own to calculate profit or loss.
* **Ledger Accounts:** The information from subsidiary journals is posted to ledger accounts, where all changes in the value of assets, liabilities, owner's equity, income, or expenses are recorded.
* **Double-Entry System:** The business uses the double-entry system to record information in the ledger accounts. Each accounting entry involves at least two accounts: if you debit one account, you credit another account. 
* **T-Account Rules:** 
  * Make **debit entries** on the left side of the T-account.
  * Make **credit entries** on the right side of the T-account.

## Owner's Equity

Owner's equity is the amount the business owes to the owner or the owner's interest in the business. Remember that the main aim of a business is to make a profit. The owners are entitled to the profit as they usually provide most of the funds. They take the risk and should get some return on their investment. 

The profit is calculated in two parts:

$$\text{Gross profit} = (\text{Sales} - \text{Debtors allowances}) - \text{Cost of sales}$$

$$\text{Net profit} = \text{Gross profit} + \text{Income} - \text{Expenses}$$

### Effect on Owner's Equity

* Sales and Income increase profit and thus increase owner's equity.
* Cost of sales and Expenses decrease profit and thus decrease owner's equity.

The table summarises the ledger entries:

| | Account to debit | Account to credit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Assets** | Increase | Decrease |
| **Owner's equity** | Decrease | Increase |
| **Income/Sales/Profit** | Decrease | Increase |
| **Expenses/Cost of sales/Loss** | Increase | Decrease |
| **Liabilities** | Decrease | Increase |

Accounts in the General Ledger are divided into the Balance Sheet accounts section and the Nominal accounts section for a specific purpose:

| Section | Accounts | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Balance Sheet accounts section** | Capital, Drawings, Assets, Liabilities | To determine financial position of the business in the financial statements (Balance Sheet) |
| **Nominal accounts section** | Sales, Cost of sales, Income, Expenses | To determine the result of operations (profit or loss) in the financial statements (Income Statement) |

A **Trial Balance** is a list of all the General Ledger accounts with their balances (values) and proves that the total amount of debit balances is equal to the total amount of credit balances. When you prepare the Trial Balance, the accounts will appear in the same sections and same order as in the General Ledger. At the end of the accounting period you can use the information in the Trial Balance after making certain adjustments to prepare the **financial statements**. The financial statements provide the results of business operations: profit or loss (Income Statement) as well as the financial position of the business (Balance Sheet).

## Forms of ownership

The accounting cycle and the accounting equation, the basic principles of accounting, apply to all businesses. It does not matter if it is a sole trader, partnership or company. Owners are entitled to the profit and owner's equity is still the amount that the business owes to the owner/owners or the owners' interest in the business.

The main differences are the distribution of the profit and the structure of the owner's equity.

In Grade 11 the focus is on sole traders and partnerships.

---

| Form of ownership | Structure of owner's equity |
| :--- | :--- |
| Sole trader | Capital + Net profit - Drawings |
| Partnership | Capital <br> Current accounts of partners (Earnings - Drawings) |

## Financial statements

The business needs to sort and organise information from the transactions in a way that has meaning to other users. The financial statements represent the transactions for a specific financial year.

The financial statements of the business for each financial year show the 'story' of the business. This enables the owner or owners to analyse the performance of the business and make the right decisions.
