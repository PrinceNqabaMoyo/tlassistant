# 3. Series and Parallel Circuits

## Key Questions
* Are there different types of electric circuits?
* If all the light bulbs in a house are part of the same circuit, how can you switch one light off without the rest also turning off?
* What is a series circuit?
* What is a parallel circuit?
* What happens when you connect more components in series or in parallel?

---

## New Words
* **Series:** Components arranged one after another in a single pathway.
* **Ammeter:** A device used to measure electric current.
* **Ampere:** The unit of measurement for electric current (A).
* **Resistance:** The opposition to the flow of electric current.

---

## 3.1 Series Circuits

A **series circuit** is defined as a circuit in which there is only one pathway for the electric current to follow. In this configuration, components are arranged one after another in a single continuous loop.

### Ammeter
An **ammeter** is a measuring instrument used to determine the magnitude of electric current flowing through a circuit. 

* **Connection:** To function correctly, an ammeter must be connected in **series** within the circuit.
* **Unit:** The current is measured in **amperes (A)**.

**2. Resistance of an Ammeter**
Do you think that an ammeter would have a high resistance or a low resistance to the current? Explain your choice.

*   **Answer:** An ammeter must have a **very low resistance**.
*   **Reasoning:** Because an ammeter is connected in series with the components it is measuring, a high resistance would significantly reduce the total current flowing through the circuit, thereby changing the very value it is intended to measure.

### Key Concept: Resistance and Current
As more resistors (bulbs) are added in series, the total resistance ($R_{total}$) of the circuit increases. According to Ohm's Law, where $I = \frac{V}{R}$, as the total resistance increases, the current strength ($I$) decreases.

### KEY CONCEPTS: SERIES CIRCUITS

#### Definition and Structure
A **series circuit** is a circuit where there is only one pathway for electrons to move through.

#### Key Characteristics
*   **Current Strength:** The current strength is the same at all points in a series circuit because there is only one pathway.
*   **Effect of Adding Resistors:** If you add more resistors in series, the current in the whole circuit **decreases**.

---

### ELECTRON FLOW IN CONDUCTORS

#### Electron Movement
*   **In a normal conductor:** Electrons in a metal drift in various different directions.
*   **In a closed circuit:** When a cell is used as an energy source, electrons begin to move towards the positive terminal of the battery.
*   **Synchronicity:** When the circuit is closed, all electrons move in the same general direction at the same time. This is why a light bulb turns on immediately when the switch is closed.
*   **Rate of Flow:** The rate at which electrons move is determined by the resistance of the conductor.

### 3.2 Parallel Circuits

**Definition:**
A **parallel circuit** is a circuit that offers more than one pathway for electrons to follow. When components are connected in this manner, we say they are connected **in parallel**.

**Key Characteristics:**
*   **Multiple Paths:** Unlike a series circuit, the current splits into different branches.
*   **Independent Paths:** Each branch provides a separate route for the current to flow through the components (e.g., light bulbs).

### Investigation: Parallel Circuits

**Objective:** To understand how parallel circuits function and to determine the effect of adding resistors in parallel on the total current strength in a circuit.

#### Investigation Details
*   **Aim:** To investigate the effect of adding resistors in parallel on the current strength.
*   **Hypothesis:** Formulate a prediction regarding how the addition of parallel resistors will change the current measured in the circuit.

---

### Key Concepts in Circuitry

#### 1. Current Strength ($I$)
Current is the rate of flow of charge in a circuit, measured in Amperes ($A$). In a circuit, an ammeter is connected in series to measure this flow.

#### 2. Resistors in Parallel
When resistors are connected in parallel, the total resistance of the circuit ($R_{total}$) decreases. According to Ohm's Law:
$$V = I \cdot R$$
Where:
*   $V$ = Potential difference (Voltage) in Volts ($V$)
*   $I$ = Current in Amperes ($A$)
*   $R$ = Resistance in Ohms ($\Omega$)

For resistors in parallel, the equivalent resistance is calculated as:
$$\frac{1}{R_{total}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{R_n}$$

#### 3. Effect on Current
As more resistors are added in parallel, the total resistance of the circuit decreases. Given a constant voltage source, the total current ($I$) drawn from the source will increase:
$$I_{total} = \frac{V}{R_{total}}$$

### Parallel Circuits: Key Concepts

#### Characteristics of Parallel Circuits
*   **Multiple Pathways:** There is more than one pathway for the electric current to follow.
*   **Current Division:** The current divides between the different branches. If the resistors (e.g., torch bulbs) in each branch are identical, the current is divided equally between them.
*   **Total Current:** If you add more resistors in parallel, the total current supplied by the cell increases.

#### Analogy: The School Hall
To understand why current divides, imagine a school hall with only one exit. When students leave, it takes time because they must exit one by one. If a second, identical door is opened, the speed at which students exit increases because some students use the first door while others use the second. 

Similarly, in a parallel circuit, as electrons approach a junction, they split between the available pathways.

#### Mathematical Relationships in Parallel Circuits
In the circuit diagram provided, the current measured at different points follows these relationships:

*   $A_1 = A_4$
*   $A_1 = A_2 + A_3$
*   $A_4 = A_2 + A_3$

### Introduction to Electrical Conductivity
While we have studied how resistors and cells behave in series and parallel circuits, we must also consider the material of the conductors themselves.

*   **Resistance in Conductors:** All conductors possess some degree of resistance to the flow of electric current.
*   **Conductivity Variation:** Different metals have different levels of resistance. Some metals are better conductors of electricity than others, meaning they offer less resistance to the flow of charge.

### 3.3 Other Output Devices

#### Definition: Output Devices
Output devices are components in an electrical circuit that use electrical energy to perform a function. While light bulbs are a common example, there are many other types of output devices.

#### LEDs (Light-Emitting Diodes)
LEDs are small, widely used electronic devices that emit light. Unlike traditional incandescent bulbs, they do not contain a filament.

**Key Characteristics of LEDs:**
*   **Durability:** Because they lack a filament, they do not "burn out" in the traditional sense and have a much longer lifespan.
*   **Efficiency:** They generate less heat than incandescent bulbs and consume significantly less electricity.
*   **Applications:** They are commonly found in:
    *   Electronic timepieces (digital clocks/watches).
    *   High-definition televisions.
    *   Modern home lighting (replacing traditional bulbs).

# Energy Transfers and Sankey Diagrams

## Introduction
In an electrical system, energy is transferred from an input source to various outputs. We can represent these energy transfers visually and proportionally using a **Sankey diagram**.

---

## Key Concepts

### 1. Energy System Components
*   **Input Energy:** The total energy supplied to the system.
*   **Useful Output Energy:** The energy that performs the intended function of the device.
*   **Wasted Output Energy:** Energy that is dissipated (usually as heat or sound) and does not contribute to the intended function.

### 2. Sankey Diagram
A Sankey diagram is a flow diagram in which the width of the arrows is proportional to the quantity of energy being represented.

---

## Worked Example: Kettle Energy Transfer
In a kettle, $2000\text{ J}$ of electrical energy is supplied. 
*   $1400\text{ J}$ is converted into **Heat energy** (useful).
*   $600\text{ J}$ is converted into **Sound energy** (wasted).



![Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Birth-life-and-the-death-of-a-star.pdf-0023-03.png](images/Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Birth-life-and-the-death-of-a-star.pdf-0023-03.png)
> **[Diagram Context - Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Birth-life-and-the-death-of-a-star.pdf-0023-03.png]**

It features five stellar representations with corresponding text labels and temperatures: Blue-white (40 000 °C), White (10 000 °C), Yellow (6 000 °C), Orange (4 500 °C), and Red (3 000 °C), beneath the heading "The temperature of a star determines its colour". Conceptually, the diagram conveys to high school learners that hotter stars emit shorter wavelengths appearing blue-white, while cooler stars emit longer wavelengths appearing red, supporting the study of electromagnetic radiation and stellar characteristics in Earth and Space Sciences.



---

## Take Note
*   Energy is measured in **joules (J)**.
*   The total input energy must equal the sum of the output energies:
    $$\text{Input Energy} = \text{Useful Output Energy} + \text{Wasted Output Energy}$$
    $$2000\text{ J} = 1400\text{ J} + 600\text{ J}$$

### Energy Efficiency: LED vs. Incandescent Bulbs

#### Concept: Sankey Diagrams
A Sankey diagram is a visual representation of energy flow. The width of the arrows is proportional to the amount of energy. It helps us distinguish between **useful energy output** and **wasted energy output**.

The efficiency of a device is calculated using the following formula:
$$\text{Efficiency} = \left( \frac{\text{Useful Energy Output}}{\text{Total Energy Input}} \right) \times 100\%$$

---

**1. LED Sankey Diagram**
*   **Input Energy:** $100\text{ J}$
*   **Useful Energy (Light):** $75\text{ J}$
*   **Wasted Energy (Heat):** $100\text{ J} - 75\text{ J} = 25\text{ J}$

## 1. Overview of Circuits
Circuits can have various **output devices**, such as:
* Alarms
* LEDs
* Motors

## 2. Circuit Types and Characteristics

### Series Circuits
* **Pathway(s):** Only one pathway for current to flow.
* **Current:** The current is the same everywhere in the circuit.
* **Adding Resistors:** When you add resistors in series, the total current decreases.
* **Observation:** The decrease in current can be observed in the **bulb brightness** (it becomes dimmer).

### Parallel Circuits
* **Pathway(s):** Two or more pathways (current splits between branches).
* **Current:** The total current increases when more branches are added.
* **Adding Resistors:** When you add resistors in parallel, the total current increases.
* **Observation:** The increase in current can be observed in the **bulb brightness** (it becomes brighter).

## 3. Summary Comparison Table

| Feature | Series Circuit | Parallel Circuit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Number of pathways** | Only one | Two or more |
| **Current behavior** | Same everywhere | Splits between branches |
| **Effect of adding resistors** | Current decreases | Current increases |
| **Effect on bulb brightness** | Decreases (dimmer) | Increases (brighter) |

## 4. Key Concepts
* **Resistor:** A component that opposes the flow of electric current.
* **Current ($I$):** The rate of flow of charge through a conductor.
* **Circuit:** A closed loop through which current can flow.

### Investigation: The Effect of Adding Bulbs in Series on Current

#### Key Concepts
*   **Independent Variable:** The number of bulbs added to the circuit.
*   **Dependent Variable:** The current strength (measured by an ammeter) and the brightness of the bulbs.
*   **Hypothesis:** A prediction of how the dependent variable changes in response to the independent variable (e.g., "As the number of resistors increases, the current strength decreases").
*   **Qualitative Data:** Descriptive observations, such as "bright," "brighter," or "brightest."
*   **Quantitative Data:** Numerical measurements, such as ammeter readings in Amperes ($A$).

#### Experimental Notes
*   **Equipment Calibration:** School laboratory ammeters and voltmeters may be uncalibrated or poorly serviced, leading to slightly inaccurate results.
*   **Circuit Consistency:** Ensure all torch bulbs have the same resistance.
*   **Switch:** A switch is not essential for this investigation and may be omitted.
*   **Thermal Effects:** If bulbs are allowed to heat up significantly between measurements, their resistance will increase, which may affect the accuracy of the downward trend.

#### Example Results Table
| Number of bulbs in series | Brightness of bulbs | Reading on ammeter ($A$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | brightest | $0,15$ |
| 2 | bright | $0,07$ |
| 3 | dimmest | $0,05$ |

#### Observations and Conclusions
1.  **Trend:** As the number of bulbs in a series circuit increases, both the ammeter reading and the bulb brightness decrease.
2.  **Current in Series:** If all bulbs glow with the same brightness, it indicates that they all experience the same current, confirming that current is constant throughout a series circuit.

## 2. Brightness Analysis (Series Circuits)
*   **Ranking:** Brightest, bright, dim.
*   **Explanation:** The first circuit contains the least resistance, resulting in the highest current and the brightest bulb. The third circuit has the highest resistance due to two resistors connected in series with the light bulb. 
*   **Key Principle:** As more resistors are connected in series, the total resistance increases, which causes the current to decrease.

## 3. Brightness Analysis (Parallel Circuits)
*   **Ranking:** Dimmest, bright, brightest.
*   **Explanation:** The third circuit contains the brightest bulb because adding resistors in parallel lowers the overall resistance of the circuit. A lower resistance allows for a greater current, making the bulb shine brighter. The first circuit is the dimmest because it lacks parallel branches, offering the highest resistance.

## 4. Complex Circuit Analysis
For a circuit containing both series and parallel components:
*   **a)** The circuit features series components (cell and bulb A) and a parallel branch (bulbs B and C).
*   **b)** Bulb A is the brightest; Bulbs B and C have equal brightness to each other.
*   **c)** If switch S is opened:
    *   Bulb C will not glow.
    *   Bulbs A and B will have equal brightness, but will be dimmer than when the switch was closed.
    *   With the switch open, A and B are in series with no parallel branch. This increases the overall resistance of the circuit, resulting in a smaller current.

## 5. Current Distribution Rules
*   **a)** $A_1 = A_4$: The total current flows through the circuit at both of these points.
*   **b)** $A_1 = A_2 + A_3$: The current splits between parallel branches in a circuit.
