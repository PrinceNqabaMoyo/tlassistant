## 1.1 Elements and Compounds

### Vocabulary
*   **Compound:** A substance formed when two or more chemical elements are chemically bonded together.
*   **Crystal lattice:** A symmetrical three-dimensional structural arrangement of atoms, ions, or molecules inside a crystalline solid.
*   **Element:** A pure substance consisting only of atoms that all have the same numbers of protons in their atomic nuclei.
*   **Diatomic:** Consisting of two atoms.
*   **Molecule:** A group of atoms bonded together, representing the smallest fundamental unit of a chemical compound that can take part in a chemical reaction.

### The Particles that Make Up Compounds
The particles of a compound always consist of two or more atoms. These atoms combine in different ways:

1.  **Molecules:** A cluster of atoms that stick together in a specific way. For example, water ($H_2O$) consists of molecules where two hydrogen atoms are bonded to one oxygen atom.
2.  **Crystal Lattice:** A regular, repeating pattern of atoms or ions arranged in a specific structure.

**Key Concept:** The molecules of a compound always consist of two or more *different* kinds of atoms.

---

### Compounds and Crystal Lattices

#### 1. Crystal Lattices
Compounds that form crystal lattices consist of many atoms, but they always combine in a fixed ratio. 

*   **Example:** Sodium chloride (table salt).
*   **Structure:** The smallest 'unit' that is repeated in the crystal consists of one sodium (Na) atom and one chlorine (Cl) atom.
*   **Formula:** The formula $\text{NaCl}$ represents one 'formula unit' of sodium chloride.

#### 2. Mixtures vs. Compounds
A compound is not simply a mixture of elements. In a mixture, elements exist independently, whereas in a compound, they are chemically bonded in specific ratios.

#### 3. Diatomic Molecules
Elements are made up of only one kind of atom. Some elements exist as **diatomic molecules**.

*   **Definition:** Diatomic means 'consisting of two atoms'.
*   **Common Examples:** 
    *   Hydrogen: $\text{H}_2$
    *   Nitrogen: $\text{N}_2$
    *   Oxygen: $\text{O}_2$
    *   Fluorine: $\text{F}_2$
    *   Chlorine: $\text{Cl}_2$
    *   Bromine: $\text{Br}_2$
    *   Iodine: $\text{I}_2$

### The Atoms in Molecules and Lattices are Combined in a Fixed Ratio

Compounds are defined by the specific, fixed ratio in which their constituent atoms combine. If this ratio changes, the substance changes, resulting in different chemical and physical properties.

#### Examples of Fixed Ratios

*   **Water ($H_2O$):** Every water molecule consists of exactly one oxygen atom ($O$) combined with two hydrogen atoms ($H$).

### Each Compound has a Unique Name and Formula

Chemical formulas provide a shorthand way to represent the composition of a compound, indicating the types of atoms present and the ratio in which they are combined.

*   **Water:** Represented by the formula $H_2O$. The subscript "2" indicates that there are two hydrogen atoms for every one oxygen atom.
*   **Carbon Dioxide:** Represented by the formula $CO_2$.

### The Atoms in a Compound are Held Together by Chemical Bonds

When atoms combine to form molecules, they are held together by an attractive force known as a **chemical bond**.

---

### Compounds Form During Chemical Reactions

In a chemical reaction, atoms in molecules rearrange themselves to form new molecules. This process involves:
1. Existing bonds between atoms breaking.
2. Atoms rearranging into new groups.
3. New bonds forming.

#### Key Terminology
*   **Reactants:** The elements or compounds present to the left of the arrow in a chemical reaction (the starting materials).
*   **Product:** The new compound formed, shown to the right of the arrow.

### A Compound has a Chemical Formula

Each compound is represented by a unique **chemical formula**. The formula uses subscripts to indicate the number of atoms of a particular element present in one molecule of that compound.

#### Example: Water ($H_2O$)
*   The subscript **2** after $H$ indicates there are two Hydrogen atoms in one molecule of water.
*   The $O$ has no subscript, which by convention means there is exactly one Oxygen atom.



![Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Acids-bases-and-the-pH-value.pdf-0003-01.png](images/Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Acids-bases-and-the-pH-value.pdf-0003-01.png)


#### Mathematical Representation of Ratios
The formula $H_2O$ (technically $H_2O_1$) represents a ratio of atoms:
*   **Ratio of Hydrogen to Oxygen:** $2:1$

**Note:** By scientific convention, we do not write the number $1$ as a subscript in chemical formulae. Therefore, $H_2O$ is the correct notation.

### 1.2 The Periodic Table

The Periodic Table is a chart used by scientists to organize and list all known elements.

#### Key Definitions (New Words)
*   **Periodic Table:** A tabular display of all known chemical elements.
*   **Symbol (or element symbol):** A unique abbreviation used to represent an element.
*   **Atomic number:** A unique number assigned to each element based on its position in the table.
*   **Unique:** Being the only one of its kind.

#### Summary of the Periodic Table
1.  **Arrangement:** All known elements are arranged in the Periodic Table.
2.  **History:** The version used today was originally proposed by Dmitri Mendeleev in the 1800s.
3.  **Ordering:** Elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number, starting with Hydrogen ($H$) in the top left-hand corner.
4.  **Classification:** An element's position indicates its classification:
    *   **Metals:** Found on the left-hand side.
    *   **Non-metals:** Found on the far right-hand side.
    *   **Semi-metals:** Found in the region between metals and non-metals.
5.  **Identification:** Each element is identified by:
    *   A unique name.
    *   A unique chemical symbol.
    *   A unique atomic number.

#### Properties of Elements
*   **Metals:** Usually shiny, ductile, and malleable. Most are solids at room temperature and have high melting and boiling points.
*   **Non-metals:** Can be solids, liquids, or gases at room temperature. They have a great variety of properties depending on their state.
*   **Semi-metals:** All are solids at room temperature and possess a combination of metallic and non-metallic properties.

### Understanding the Periodic Table

#### Key Concepts
The Periodic Table is a tabular display of chemical elements. Each element is represented by a "tile" containing specific information. The two most common pieces of information found on every tile are:

1.  **The Chemical Symbol:** A unique one- or two-letter code used to identify the element (e.g., $C$ for Carbon, $Na$ for Sodium).
2.  **The Atomic Number:** A whole number that represents the number of protons found in the nucleus of an atom of that element.

*   **Protons:** Since the atomic number is $6$, the atom has **6 protons**.

#### Periodic Table Reference
The Periodic Table organizes elements based on their atomic structure. Below is a summary of the information structure provided in the standard Periodic Table:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Atomic Number** | Located at the top of the tile; indicates the number of protons. |
| **Chemical Symbol** | Located in the center of the tile; represents the element name. |
| **Element Name** | Often written below the symbol (depending on the version of the table). |
| **Groups** | Vertical columns (numbered 1–18) representing elements with similar properties. |
| **Periods** | Horizontal rows representing the number of electron shells. |

# The Periodic Table of Elements

## Overview
The Periodic Table is a structured arrangement of all known chemical elements. Elements are organized in a specific order to help scientists understand their properties and relationships.

## Information Contained in Element Tiles
Each element is represented by a tile. While some versions only show the chemical symbol, others provide additional data:

1.  **Atomic Number:** The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
2.  **Chemical Symbol:** A unique one- or two-letter abbreviation for the element (e.g., $\text{Cu}$ for copper).
3.  **Element Name:** The full name of the element.
4.  **Atomic Mass:** The average mass of an atom of that element (e.g., $63.5$ for copper).

## Arrangement of Elements
Elements are arranged according to a specific logic:

*   **Increasing Atomic Number:** Elements are placed in order of increasing atomic number.
    *   **Hydrogen ($\text{H}$):** Has the smallest atomic number ($1$) and is located at the top left.
    *   **Heaviest Elements:** Elements with the largest atomic numbers are found at the bottom of the table.
*   **Regional Grouping:** The table is divided into regions, often color-coded, to distinguish between different types of elements such as metals, non-metals, and semi-metals.

## Key Vocabulary
*   **Metal:** Elements that are typically shiny, conductive, and malleable.
*   **Non-metal:** Elements that lack metallic properties; often gases or brittle solids.
*   **Semi-metal (Metalloid):** Elements with properties intermediate between metals and non-metals.
*   **Group:** A vertical column in the periodic table.
*   **Period:** A horizontal row in the periodic table.
*   **Electrons:** Negatively charged subatomic particles.
*   **Neutrons:** Neutral subatomic particles found in the nucleus.
*   **Protons:** Positively charged subatomic particles found in the nucleus.

# The Periodic Table: Structure and Classification

## 1. Classification of Elements
The Periodic Table categorizes elements based on their physical and chemical properties. These are summarized as follows:

*   **Metals:** Located on the left and center of the Periodic Table. In the provided diagram, these are represented in **blue**.
*   **Non-metals:** Located in a small, triangular region at the top right-hand side of the table. In the provided diagram, these are represented in **red**.
*   **Semi-metals (Metalloids):** Elements that possess both metallic and non-metallic properties. They form a diagonal strip separating the metals from the non-metals. In the provided diagram, these are represented in **yellow**.

> **TAKE NOTE:** Semi-metals are also sometimes referred to as **metalloids**.

# The Periodic Table: Structure and Symbols

## 1. Structure of the Periodic Table

The Periodic Table is organized into a grid system that helps scientists categorize elements based on their properties.



![Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Acids-bases-and-the-pH-value.pdf-0009-00.png](images/Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Acids-bases-and-the-pH-value.pdf-0009-00.png)
> **[Diagram Context - Textbook_NaturalScience_Gr9_Acids-bases-and-the-pH-value.pdf-0009-00.png]**
> 1. A row of colorful test tubes, each containing a different colored liquid.



### Groups
*   **Definition:** The vertical columns of the Periodic Table are called **groups**.
*   **Numbering:** Groups are numbered from 1 to 18, starting from the left.
*   **Note:** In older textbooks, you may encounter a different, more complex numbering system.

### Periods
*   **Definition:** The horizontal rows of the Periodic Table are called **periods**.
*   **Orientation:** The first period is located at the very top of the table.

---

## 2. Names and Chemical Symbols

Every element has a unique name and a unique chemical symbol. When writing or identifying these symbols, follow these five standard rules:

1.  Every element has its own, unique symbol.
2.  The symbol is usually (but not always) the first one or two letters of the element's name.
3.  The first letter of the symbol is **always** a capital letter.
4.  If the symbol has two letters, the second letter is **always** a small letter.
5.  Some elements have symbols derived from their Latin names.

### Learning Goal
As a student, you are expected to memorize the names and chemical symbols of the first 20 elements of the Periodic Table.

### The Periodic Table: Groups and Properties

#### Why are there 18 groups?
The Periodic Table contains exactly 18 groups because of how electrons are distributed within an atom. These "electron patterns" repeat after every 18th element. You will study this in greater detail in Grade 10 Physical Sciences.

#### Properties of Elements in the Same Group
Elements within the same group often share similar physical and chemical properties because they possess similar "electron patterns," which dictate how they behave in chemical reactions.

#### Alkali Metals (Group 1)
*   **Definition:** The metals found in Group 1 are known as **alkali metals**.
*   **Note:** Hydrogen is placed in Group 1 due to its electron pattern, but it is classified as a non-metal.
*   **Physical Properties:** Alkali metals are soft, dull-grey metals.
*   **Chemical Properties:** They react in a very specific, vigorous way with water.

**Example: Reaction of Lithium with Water**
When a piece of lithium is placed in water, it reacts immediately. The chemical equation for this reaction is:

$$2\text{Li} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{LiOH} + \text{H}_2$$

*   **Observation:** Lithium metal is typically stored in oil to prevent it from reacting with moisture in the air. It floats in the bottle because it is less dense than the oil.

### Chemical Reactions of Alkali Metals with Water

#### 1. The Reaction of Lithium with Water
When lithium metal reacts with water, it produces hydrogen gas ($H_2$) and lithium hydroxide. The reaction releases heat, which can cause the hydrogen gas to ignite.

*   **Chemical Equation:** $2Li + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2LiOH + H_2$
*   **Word Equation:** Lithium + Water $\rightarrow$ Lithium hydroxide + Hydrogen

#### 2. Reactivity Trends in Group 1 Metals
Elements in the same group of the periodic table exhibit similar chemical properties. As you move down Group 1, the reactivity of the metals increases.

**Sodium ($Na$):**
Sodium is more reactive than lithium. It moves rapidly on the surface of water and bursts into flame immediately upon contact.
*   **Chemical Equation:** $2Na + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH + H_2$

**Potassium ($K$):**
Potassium is even more reactive than sodium. It reacts violently and explodes upon contact with water.
*   **Chemical Equation:** $2K + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2KOH + H_2$

# 1.3 Names of Compounds

### New Words
*   **IUPAC:** International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry.
*   **IUPAC system:** A standardized set of rules for naming chemical compounds.
*   **Systematic name:** A name assigned based on a specific set of rules.
*   **Suffix:** A letter or group of letters added to the **end** of a word.
*   **Prefix:** A letter or group of letters added to the **beginning** of a word.

---

### The Importance of Unique Names
Just as every element has a unique name to avoid confusion, every chemical compound must have a unique name. 

**Example:**

*   **$CO_2$ (Carbon dioxide):** A different gas.

If both were simply called "carbon oxide," it would be dangerous because their properties (such as toxicity) differ significantly.

---

### Chemical Formulae and Naming
*   **Formulae:** Chemical formulae are combinations of element symbols (e.g., $NaCl$ consists of Sodium ($Na$) and Chlorine ($Cl$)).
*   **Naming Convention:** When naming compounds, the names of the elements are combined. Often, the name of the element listed last is slightly modified.
*   **The IUPAC System:** To prevent confusion, the **International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC)** developed a systematic way to name compounds based on the elements they contain and how they are combined.

---

### Naming Compounds with Two Elements
At this level, we focus on naming compounds that consist of two elements. We categorize them into types to determine the naming rule.

#### Type 1: Compounds that contain a metal and a non-metal
The rule for naming these compounds is:
1.  The **metal** name comes first.
2.  The **non-metal** name comes second.
3.  The ending of the non-metal name is changed to the suffix **-ide**.

**Example:**
*   Sodium + Chlorine $\rightarrow$ Sodium chlor**ide**

> **TAKE NOTE**
> *   A **suffix** is something placed at the **end** of a word.
> *   A **prefix** is something placed at the **beginning** of a word.

# Type 2: Compounds that contain only non-metals

Naming compounds consisting only of non-metals requires following three specific rules.

### Rule 1: Element Order and Suffixes
1. The name of the element further to the left on the Periodic Table is written first.
2. The name of the element further to the right on the Periodic Table follows.
3. The ending of the second element is replaced with the suffix **-ide**.

**Examples of suffix changes:**
* Oxygen becomes **oxide**
* Fluorine becomes **fluoride**
* Chlorine becomes **chloride**

### Compounds

*   **Definition:** A compound consists of atoms of two or more different kinds that are chemically bonded in a fixed ratio.
*   **Chemical Bonds:** The atoms within a molecule are held together by special attractions known as chemical bonds.
*   **Chemical Reactions:**
    *   Compounds can be formed and broken down through chemical reactions.
    *   A **decomposition reaction** is a specific type of chemical reaction where a compound is broken down into simpler compounds or elements.
*   **Separation:** Compounds cannot be separated by physical processes. They can only be separated into their constituent elements or simpler compounds through chemical processes.

---

### The Periodic Table

*   **Arrangement:** Elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number, starting with the lightest element (Hydrogen: $H$) in the top left-hand corner.
*   **Classification by Position:**
    *   **Metals:** Found on the left-hand side of the table.
    *   **Non-metals:** Found on the far right-hand side of the table.
    *   **Semi-metals:** Found in the region between the metals and non-metals.
*   **Identification:** Each element is uniquely identified by:
    1.  A unique name.
    2.  A unique chemical symbol.
    3.  A unique atomic number.
*   **Structure:**
    *   **Groups:** The vertical columns of the Periodic Table (there are 18 groups). Elements in the same group exhibit similar chemical behaviour and properties.
    *   **Periods:** The horizontal rows of the Periodic Table (there are 7 periods).
*   **Data Provided:** Most versions of the Periodic Table provide the element symbol, atomic number, and atomic mass for each element.

---

### Names and Formulae

*   **Uniqueness:** Every compound has a unique name and chemical formula.
*   **Formula Purpose:** The formula indicates which elements are present in the compound and the ratio in which atoms have combined to form one molecule of that compound.
*   **Naming Rules:** There are specific conventions for naming compounds that account for the number of atoms of each type present in a single molecule.

## 2. Atomic Structure and Notation
Each element block on the Periodic Table typically contains:
1.  **Atomic Number:** The smaller number (represents the number of protons).
2.  **Atomic Mass:** The larger number (often rounded).

### Important Distinction: Mass Number vs. Average Atomic Mass
It is a common misconception to label the "larger number" on the Periodic Table as the *Mass Number*.

*   **Mass Number:** Defined as the sum of protons and neutrons in a specific atom.
*   **Isotopes:** Variants of a chemical element that have the same number of protons and electrons but a different number of neutrons.
*   **Average Atomic Mass:** Because elements exist as a mixture of isotopes in nature, the value on the Periodic Table is the weighted average of the masses of all naturally occurring isotopes. 

**Instructional Note:** Use the term **"atomic mass"** rather than "mass number" when referring to the value found on the Periodic Table to ensure scientific accuracy.

1.  **Symbolic Representation:** Using chemical formulae (e.g., $H_2O$, $CO_2$).
2.  **Sub-microscopic Representation:** Using molecular diagrams to visualize the arrangement of atoms.

### Practical Application
Learners are encouraged to use physical materials (such as plasticine or play dough) to construct molecular models. This reinforces the ability to switch between symbolic notation and 3D spatial understanding.

### Play Dough Recipe
This recipe is provided for reference in the study of Matter and Materials.

**Ingredients:**
* 2 cups flour
* 2 cups warm water
* 1 cup salt
* 2 tablespoons vegetable oil
* 1 tablespoon cream of tartar (optional, for improved elasticity)
* Food colouring (various colours)

**Method:**
1. Combine all ingredients and stir over low heat until the mixture thickens to a consistency resembling mashed potatoes.
2. Once the dough pulls away from the sides and forms a clump in the centre, remove from heat and allow to cool. If the dough remains sticky, continue cooking for a longer duration.
3. Turn the dough onto a clean surface and knead until smooth. Divide into balls.
4. Create a small depression in the centre of each ball, add food colouring, and work the colour through the dough until the desired intensity is reached.

### Understanding the Atom (Revision)

An atom is defined by its subatomic particles. For a neutral atom:
1. The **Atomic Number** indicates the number of protons.
2. **Protons** are positively charged.
3. **Electrons** are negatively charged. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.
4. **Nucleus**: Protons and neutrons are clustered in the centre.
5. **Electron Cloud**: Electrons occupy a large space or cloud around the nucleus.

#### Worked Example: Oxygen
*   **Atomic number**: 8 (Therefore, 8 protons).
*   **Atomic mass**: 15.999 (Rounded to 16).
*   **Neutron calculation**: $\text{Number of neutrons} = \text{Atomic mass} - \text{Atomic number}$
*   $\text{Number of neutrons} = 16 - 8 = 8$
*   **Electrons**: 8 (Since the atom is neutral).

### Alkali Metals

The metals in Group 1 of the periodic table are known as **alkali metals**. The lightest member of this group is Lithium (Li).

#### Properties and Handling
*   **Storage**: Alkali metals are stored in oil rather than water. They react vigorously with water and slowly with oxygen in the air.
*   **Density**: Lithium is the lightest metal in the universe. Because it is less dense than oil, it floats when placed in a bottle of oil. This demonstrates the particle model of matter regarding the density of different materials.

### 6.2 Elements and Compounds

This section serves as a revision of fundamental concepts regarding the structure of compounds.

#### Types of Compound Structures
Compounds are classified into two main structural types based on the elements they contain:

1.  **Molecules:** Formed when a compound consists entirely of non-metals.
    *   *Examples:* $CO_2$, $H_2O$, $NH_3$.
2.  **Lattices (Ionic Compounds):** Formed when a compound consists of a metal and a non-metal.
    *   *Process:* Metal and non-metal atoms exchange electrons to form ions. Due to electrostatic attraction between opposite charges, these ions pack together in vast, three-dimensional crystal structures.
    *   *Example:* $NaCl$ (Sodium chloride) consists of a regular arrangement of sodium and chloride ions in a $1:1$ ratio.

### 6.3 Names of Compounds

Chemical compounds are categorized based on the types of elements they contain. Understanding these categories helps in naming the compounds correctly.

#### Key Concepts
*   **Ionic Compounds:** Compounds formed between a metal and a non-metal. These do not form molecules, but rather structures called "formula units" (lattices).
*   **Covalent Compounds:** Compounds formed between two or more non-metals. These exist as discrete molecules.

### Guidelines for Drawing Molecular Structures

When representing molecules visually, keep the following principles in mind:

*   **Central Atom Placement:** The atom that appears first in the chemical name or formula should be placed at the centre of the molecule. All other atoms must be bonded to this central atom.
*   **Relative Sizes:** While exact sizes are not critical, atoms of elements located higher up on the Periodic Table generally have a smaller atomic radius than those located lower down.
*   **Colour Coding:** Atoms of the same element should be represented using the same colour. The specific choice of colour is not important.
