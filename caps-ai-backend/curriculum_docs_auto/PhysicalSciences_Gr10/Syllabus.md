#### 2. Content & Cognitive Scope
*   **Core Concept:** Matter is composed of particles; observable characteristics of matter are determined by its reactivity.

    *   Observing, describing, classifying, and using materials.
    *   Applying a macroscopic view to material properties.
    *   Interpreting safety data and ingredient lists on consumer products.

    *   This topic serves as a revision of Grade 9 content.
    *   Emphasis is placed on the practical application of material properties (e.g., conductivity, density, malleability).
    *   Integration of indigenous knowledge systems (contrasting modern food preservation with traditional methods).

*   **Pacing:** The topic is allocated 2 hours of instructional time.
*   **Instructional Strategy:** 
    *   Teachers should ensure learners can link theoretical properties (e.g., melting points) to real-world objects.
    *   Encourage the use of product labels to foster scientific literacy regarding chemical compounds in food and medicine.
    *   Ensure the transition from Grade 9 to Grade 10 is seamless by focusing on the revision of these foundational concepts.

#### 4. Assessment Matrix
*   **Informal Assessment:** The provided activity ("What materials are products made of?") serves as an informal assessment opportunity to gauge learner understanding of material classification and properties.
*   **Cognitive Levels:** The activities focus on **Knowledge** (identifying properties) and **Routine Procedures** (classifying materials based on observation).

*   **Classification:** Ability to categorize substances based on physical and chemical properties.

**Cognitive Levels:**
*   **Knowledge:** Recalling definitions of elements, compounds, and mixtures.
*   **Routine Procedures:** Performing standard chromatography experiments and identifying substances using the periodic table.
*   **Complex Procedures:** Analyzing experimental data (melting/boiling points) to determine purity.
*   **Problem Solving:** Differentiating between complex mixtures and justifying classifications through scientific reasoning.

#### 4. Mathematical & Scientific Notation
The following concepts are foundational to the curriculum:
*   **Purity Criteria:** 
    *   Pure substances exhibit constant physical properties: $T_{melting} = \text{constant}$ and $T_{boiling} = \text{constant}$.
    *   Mixtures exhibit a range of temperatures during phase changes.
*   **Chromatography:** The retention factor ($R_f$) is a key concept for future quantitative analysis:
    $$R_f = \frac{\text{distance traveled by solute}}{\text{distance traveled by solvent}}$$

#### 4. Mathematical & Scientific Representations

*   **Kinetic Molecular Theory:** The behavior of particles is governed by the relationship between kinetic energy and intermolecular forces.
*   **Phase Change Data:**
    *   Temperature ($T$) as a function of time ($t$): $T = f(t)$
    *   Heating curve slope represents the rate of energy transfer: $\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta t}$
*   **Microscopic Modeling:**
    *   Solid: Particles vibrate about fixed positions.
    *   Liquid: Particles move past one another with limited range.
    *   Gas: Particles move randomly with high kinetic energy.

---

#### 4. Mathematical Formulas & Notation

*   **Atomic Number ($Z$):** Number of protons.
*   **Mass Number ($A$):** Sum of protons and neutrons.
    $$A = Z + N$$
    *(Where $N$ is the number of neutrons)*
*   **Charge Calculation:**
    $$Q_{net} = (\text{protons}) - (\text{electrons})$$
*   **Isotope Notation:**
    $${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$$
    *(Where $\text{X}$ is the element symbol)*

## 4. Mathematical Representation

The principle of superposition for two pulses with displacements $y_1$ and $y_2$ at a specific point is expressed as:

$$y_{resultant} = y_1 + y_2$$

*   **Constructive Interference:** Occurs when pulses are on the same side of the equilibrium position, resulting in a larger amplitude:
    $$A_{total} = A_1 + A_2$$
*   **Destructive Interference:** Occurs when pulses are on opposite sides of the equilibrium position, resulting in a reduced amplitude:
    $$A_{total} = |A_1 - A_2|$$

### Physical Sciences Grade 10: Electrostatics (Curriculum Specification)

#### 1. Annual Teaching Plan (ATP) & Timelines

#### 2. Content & Cognitive Scope
The following core concepts and skills are required for mastery within the "Two kinds of charge" sub-topic:

*   **Core Concepts:**
    *   Atomic composition: All materials contain positive charges (protons) and negative charges (electrons).
    *   Neutrality: An object with an equal number of electrons and protons is neutral (net charge = 0).
    *   Charge states: 
        *   Positively charged objects are electron-deficient.
        *   Negatively charged objects have an excess of electrons.
    *   Charging mechanisms: Charging by contact (or rubbing) – tribo-electric charging.

*   **Cognitive Levels & Depth of Treatment:**
    *   **Knowledge:** Recall that matter consists of protons and electrons.
    *   **Routine Procedures:** Identify whether an object is neutral, positively charged, or negatively charged based on electron/proton count.
    *   **Conceptual Understanding:** Explain the process of tribo-electric charging (transfer of electrons via contact/rubbing).

#### 3. Guidelines for Teachers
*   **Terminology:** It is pedagogically sound to refer to the two types of charge as "positive" and "negative" because their sum results in a net charge of zero (neutrality).
*   **Misconception Management:** Ensure learners understand that all objects contain both positive and negative charges. An object is only considered "charged" when there is an imbalance (either an excess or a deficiency of electrons).

#### 4. Mathematical Representation
While this specific section focuses on conceptual foundations, the underlying principle of net charge ($Q_{net}$) is defined as:

$$Q_{net} = \sum q_i$$

Where:
*   $Q_{net}$ is the total net charge.
*   $q_i$ represents the individual charges of protons ($+$) and electrons ($-$).

For a neutral object:
$$n_{protons} = n_{electrons} \implies Q_{net} = 0$$

# Lesson: Basic Stoichiometric Calculations

## Overview
Stoichiometry is the study of the quantitative relationships between the amounts of reactants used and the products formed in a chemical reaction. This lesson covers the fundamental calculations required to determine yields based on mass, moles, concentration, and volume.

---

## Core Concepts and Formulas

### 1. The Mole Concept
The mole ($n$) is the standard unit for the amount of substance.
*   **Mass to Moles:** $n = \frac{m}{M}$
    *   $n$: number of moles (mol)
    *   $m$: mass of the substance (g)
    *   $M$: molar mass (g·mol$^{-1}$)

### 2. Concentration
Concentration ($C$) is defined as the amount of solute per unit volume of solution.
*   **Formula:** $C = \frac{n}{V}$
    *   $C$: concentration (mol·dm$^{-3}$)
    *   $n$: number of moles (mol)
    *   $V$: volume (dm$^{3}$)

---

## Determining Theoretical Yield

The theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product that can be produced from a given amount of reactant, assuming the reaction goes to completion.

### Procedure for Calculation:
1.  **Balance the Equation:** Ensure the chemical equation follows the Law of Conservation of Mass.
2.  **Convert to Moles:** Use the given mass of the reactant to find the number of moles ($n = \frac{m}{M}$).
3.  **Use Mole Ratios:** Use the stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced equation to find the moles of the product.
    *   If the ratio of Reactant A to Product B is $a:b$, then:
        $$n_{product} = n_{reactant} \times \left( \frac{b}{a} \right)$$
4.  **Convert to Mass:** Convert the moles of product back into mass ($m = n \times M$).

#### 2. Mathematical Specifications (Kinematic Equations)

The following equations are prescribed for solving problems involving motion in one dimension:

| Variable | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| $v_i$ or $u$ | Initial velocity |
| $v_f$ or $v$ | Final velocity |
| $a$ | Acceleration |
| $\Delta x$ or $s$ | Displacement |
| $\Delta t$ or $t$ | Time interval |

**Standard Kinematic Equations:**
$$v_f = v_i + a\Delta t \quad \text{or} \quad v = u + at$$
$$\Delta x = v_i\Delta t + \frac{1}{2}a(\Delta t)^2 \quad \text{or} \quad s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$$
$$v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2a\Delta x \quad \text{or} \quad v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$
$$\Delta x = \left( \frac{v_i + v_f}{2} \right) \Delta t \quad \text{or} \quad s = \left( \frac{v + u}{2} \right)t$$

#### 3. Instructional Guidelines for Teachers
*   **Graphical Interpretation:** When teaching the area under a velocity-time graph, use concrete examples. For instance, represent displacement as the area of a square (e.g., a car traveling at $20\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ for $3\text{ s}$ covers $60\text{ m}$).
*   **Problem-Solving Strategy:** 
    *   Explicitly teach problem-solving strategies.
    *   Require students to include a sketch of the physical situation.
