# Electric Circuits

## Potential Difference and EMF

In a complete electric circuit, charge requires a force to move it. This force is provided by the battery, which performs work on the charge to move it through the circuit. A battery possesses the potential to drive charge around a closed circuit by converting its stored energy into electrical energy.

### Definition: Potential Difference

Potential difference is defined as the work done per unit charge.

**Formula:**
$$V = \frac{W}{q}$$

**Units:**
*   **Quantity:** Potential difference ($V$)
*   **Unit name:** volt
*   **Unit symbol:** $V$
*   **Definition:** One volt is defined as one joule per coulomb ($1\text{ J}\cdot\text{C}^{-1}$).

## EMF (Electromotive Force)

### Definitions
*   **EMF:** The maximum amount of work per coulomb of charge that a battery can do to drive charge from one terminal, through the circuit, to the other terminal.
*   **Electrical Potential Difference:** Also known as **voltage**.

### Measuring EMF vs. Terminal Potential Difference

| Condition | Measurement Type | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Not in a complete circuit** | EMF | Measures the maximum potential difference across the battery terminals. |
| **In a complete circuit** | Terminal Potential Difference | Measures the potential difference across the battery when current is flowing. |

*   **Note:** The terminal potential difference is not identical to the EMF. The difference represents the work done to drive charge through the battery itself.

## Current

### Flow of Charge
Current describes the amount of charge that moves past a fixed point in a circuit in one second. Charges are present in wires, but they require a battery to push them through the circuit.

When one charge moves, it pushes the charges next to it, causing them to move as well. This is similar to a tube of marbles or a train with connected carriages: if you push a marble into one end of the tube, a marble must exit the other side immediately. Similarly, when a battery is connected, all charges in the circuit begin to move instantaneously.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Definition: Current
Current is defined as the rate at which charges move past a fixed point in a circuit.

| Quantity | Unit Name | Unit Symbol |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Current ($I$) | Ampere | A |

*   **Note:** The unit of current, the ampere (A), is defined as one coulomb per second.

---

### Historical Fact: The Direction of Current
Benjamin Franklin made an early assumption regarding the direction of charge flow when rubbing smooth wax with rough wool. He hypothesized that charges flowed from the wax to the wool (positive to negative). 

*   **The Reality:** We now know that electrons have a negative charge and that objects labeled "negative" actually have an excess of electrons. 
*   **The Convention:** By the time the true direction of electron flow was discovered, the convention of "positive" and "negative" was already so well-established in the scientific community that it was never changed. Consequently, current is still discussed in terms of this original convention.

## Electric Current
Electric current is represented by the symbol $I$ and is measured in amperes (A). One ampere is defined as one coulomb of charge moving past a point in one second ($C \cdot s^{-1}$).

The relationship between current, charge, and time is given by the formula:
$$I = \frac{Q}{\Delta t}$$

### Direction of Current
*   **Diagrammatic representation:** Current flow is indicated on circuit diagrams using arrows.
*   **Conventional Current:** By convention, charge is said to flow from the positive terminal of a battery to the negative terminal.

### High Voltage and Safety
If the voltage is sufficiently high, current can be driven through materials that are normally insulators (such as the gas inside a plasma ball). In a plasma ball, the voltage is extremely high, but the resulting current is very low, which makes it safe to touch.

### Question
An amount of charge equal to $45\text{ C}$ moves past a point in a circuit in $1\text{ second}$, what is the current in the circuit?

### Solution

**Step 1: Analyse the question**

$$I = 45\text{ A}$$

**Step 3: Quote the final result**
The current is $45\text{ A}$.

## Worked Example: Calculating Current

**Given:**
*   Charge of an electron = $1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$
*   Number of electrons = $95$
*   Time interval ($\Delta t$) = $\frac{1}{10} \text{ s}$

### Step 2: Determine the total charge ($Q$)
We know that each electron carries a charge of $1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$. Therefore, the total charge is:

$$Q = 95 \times 1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$$
$$Q = 1,52 \times 10^{-17} \text{ C}$$

### Step 3: Determine the current ($I$)
Using the formula for current:

$$I = \frac{Q}{\Delta t}$$

Substitute the values:

$$I = \frac{1,52 \times 10^{-17} \text{ C}}{\frac{1}{10} \text{ s}}$$
$$I = \frac{1,52 \times 10^{-17} \text{ C}}{1} \times \frac{1}{\frac{1}{10} \text{ s}}$$
$$I = \frac{1,52 \times 10^{-17} \text{ C}}{1} \times \frac{10}{1 \text{ s}}$$
$$I = 1,52 \times 10^{-16} \text{ C} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$$
$$I = 1,52 \times 10^{-16} \text{ A}$$

### Step 4: Quote the final result
The current is $1,52 \times 10^{-16} \text{ A}$.

## What causes resistance?

On a microscopic level, electrons moving through a conductor collide or interact with the particles of the conductor (metal). During these collisions, the electrons transfer kinetic energy, causing them to lose speed. This process is what we define as resistance.

The transferred kinetic energy causes the resistor to heat up. A practical example of this is a cellphone charger, which becomes warm during use because the internal circuits contain resistors.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Definition: Resistance
Resistance is the property that slows down the flow of charge in a circuit.

*   **Quantity:** Resistance ($R$)
*   **Unit:** ohm
*   **Unit symbol:** $\Omega$

The unit is defined as a volt per ampere of current:
$$1\ \Omega = 1\ \frac{\text{volt}}{\text{ampere}}$$

---

## Conductors and Resistance

All conductors possess some degree of resistance. For instance, a standard piece of wire has less resistance than a lightbulb filament, but both still offer resistance to the flow of current.

### The Lightbulb Filament
A lightbulb consists of a very thin wire (the filament) surrounded by a glass housing. Because the filament is very thin, it has high resistance. This high resistance causes electrons to transfer a significant amount of their kinetic energy into heat. The resulting heat energy is sufficient to make the filament glow white-hot, which produces light.

In contrast, the wires connecting the lamp to the battery do not get noticeably warm. This is because they are thicker (have a larger cross-section) and therefore have much lower resistance.

---

### FACT: Efficiency in Lighting
Fluorescent lightbulbs are more efficient than traditional incandescent lightbulbs. They do not rely on thin wires; instead, they use specific gases that glow when an electric current flows through them. Because these gases offer much less resistance than a thin filament, they are more energy-efficient.

## Physical Attributes Affecting Resistance [NOT IN CAPS]
The physical dimensions of a resistor directly influence its total resistance:

*   **Length ($L$):** Increasing the length of a resistor increases its resistance. If the length is increased by a certain factor, the resistance increases by the same factor.
*   **Cross-sectional Area ($A$):** Increasing the width or height (the area through which current flows) provides a larger pathway for electrons. If the cross-sectional area is increased by a factor, the resistance typically decreases by the same factor.

### Mathematical Relationship
For a single resistor, these relationships are summarized by the proportionality:
$$R \propto \frac{L}{A}$$

Where:
*   $R$ = Resistance
*   $L$ = Length
*   $A$ = Cross-sectional area

## Why do batteries go flat?
Batteries store **chemical potential energy**. When connected in a circuit, a chemical reaction occurs inside the battery, converting this chemical potential energy into electrical energy, which drives the flow of electrons.

All circuit components (conducting leads, resistors, lightbulbs) possess resistance. As charge flows, electrical energy is converted into heat and light. Because energy is conserved, a battery "goes flat" once all its stored chemical potential energy has been converted into other forms of energy.

# Series Resistors

When resistors are connected in series within a circuit, they exhibit specific physical characteristics regarding current, voltage, and resistance.

## Key Characteristics of Series Circuits

*   **Current:** There is only one path for the current to flow. This ensures that the current is the same at every point in the circuit.
*   **Voltage:** The voltage is divided across the resistors. The total potential difference (voltage) across the battery is equal to the sum of the voltages across each individual resistor in the series:
    $$V_{battery} = V_1 + V_2 + \dots$$
*   **Resistance:** The total resistance to the flow of current increases. The total resistance ($R_S$) is calculated as the sum of the individual resistances:
    $$R_S = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$$

## Potential Difference in Series
When resistors are placed one after the other, a potential difference exists across each resistor. The total potential difference across the set of resistors is the sum of the potential differences across each individual resistor:
$$V_{battery} = V_1 + V_2 + \dots$$

Resistors in series are often referred to as **voltage dividers**. This is because the total voltage supplied by the source (the battery) is divided amongst the individual resistors in the series circuit.

## Parallel Resistors

When resistors are added in parallel to a circuit, the following principles apply:

*   **Current Splitting:** There are multiple paths for current to flow, which ensures that the current splits across the different paths.
*   **Voltage Consistency:** The voltage is the same across all parallel resistors and is equal to the voltage across the battery:
    $$V_{battery} = V_1 = V_2 = V_3 \dots$$
*   **Total Resistance:** The total resistance to the flow of current decreases. The total resistance ($R_P$) is calculated using the following formula:
    $$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots$$

### Key Concept: Potential Difference in Parallel
When resistors are connected in parallel, the start and end points for all resistors are the same. These points share the same potential energy; therefore, the potential difference between them remains constant regardless of what components are placed in between. You can ignore other components between these two points when calculating the potential difference.

## Potential Difference in Parallel Circuits

### Key Concept: Constant Voltage in Parallel
When resistors are connected in parallel, the potential difference (voltage) across each branch remains the same, provided there are no other components (like resistors) between the measurement points and the source.

*   **Observation:** If you measure the potential difference between two points connected by a wire with no components, the reading will be identical regardless of where on that wire you place your probes.
*   **Rule:** All points on a continuous wire that have no circuit components between them are at the same potential.

The following diagrams demonstrate that adding more resistors in parallel does not change the potential difference measured across the parallel network, assuming the battery remains the same.

### Measurement Principles
When analyzing circuits with parallel components:
1.  **Wire Equivalence:** Points $A, C,$ and $E$ are electrically equivalent because they are connected by a wire with no components. Similarly, points $B, D,$ and $F$ are equivalent.
2.  **Voltage Consistency:** Measuring the potential difference between $A-B$, $C-D$, or $E-F$ will yield the same result.
3.  **Energy:** Because there are no components between these points, there is no change in potential energy, resulting in a constant voltage reading of $V = 5\text{ V}$ in the provided example.

## Resistors in Parallel

When resistors are connected in parallel, the total resistance of the circuit decreases as more paths are provided for the current to flow. The total resistance is **not** the simple sum of the individual resistances.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### The Parallel Resistance Formula

For any number of resistors connected in parallel, the total resistance ($R_P$) is calculated using the following formula:

$$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots$$

### Derivation for Two Resistors

When a circuit contains exactly two resistors ($R_1$ and $R_2$) in parallel, we can simplify the general formula by adding the fractions:

1. Start with the general formula:
   $$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$$

2. Find a common denominator by multiplying each term to match the denominators:
   $$\frac{1}{R_P} = \left( \frac{1}{R_1} \times \frac{R_2}{R_2} \right) + \left( \frac{1}{R_2} \times \frac{R_1}{R_1} \right)$$

3. Combine the fractions:
   $$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{R_2}{R_1 R_2} + \frac{R_1}{R_1 R_2}$$

4. Final expression for the sum:
   $$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{R_2 + R_1}{R_1 R_2}$$

## Calculating Total Resistance for Resistors in Parallel

When resistors are connected in parallel, the total resistance ($R_P$) is calculated using the reciprocal formula.

### Step 1: The General Formula
For any number of resistors in parallel, the relationship is:
$$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots$$

### Step 2: Applying the Principle (Worked Example)
For a circuit with three resistors ($R_1, R_2, R_3$), the formula expands to:
$$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}$$

**Algebraic Manipulation:**
To solve for $R_P$, we find a common denominator:
$$\frac{1}{R_P} = \left(\frac{1}{R_1} \times \frac{R_2 R_3}{R_2 R_3}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{R_2} \times \frac{R_1 R_3}{R_1 R_3}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{R_3} \times \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 R_2}\right)$$

$$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{R_2 R_3 + R_1 R_3 + R_1 R_2}{R_1 R_2 R_3}$$

Inverting the fraction to solve for $R_P$:
$$R_P = \frac{R_1 R_2 R_3}{R_2 R_3 + R_1 R_3 + R_2 R_3}$$

**Numerical Calculation:**
Using values of $15\,\Omega$, $7\,\Omega$, and $3\,\Omega$:
$$R_P = \frac{(15\,\Omega)(7\,\Omega)(3\,\Omega)}{(7\,\Omega)(3\,\Omega) + (15\,\Omega)(3\,\Omega) + (7\,\Omega)(15\,\Omega)}$$
$$R_P = \frac{315\,\Omega^3}{21\,\Omega^2 + 45\,\Omega^2 + 105\,\Omega^2}$$
$$R_P = \frac{315\,\Omega^3}{171\,\Omega^2}$$
$$R_P = 1,84\,\Omega$$

### Step 3: Quote the Final Result
The total resistance of the resistors in parallel is $1,84\,\Omega$. 

*Note: This result is logical because adding an additional parallel path reduces the overall resistance of the circuit.*

# Chapter 17 | Summary: Electric Circuits

## Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Electromotive Force (emf):** The potential difference across the terminals of a battery when it is **not** in a complete circuit. Measured in volts (V).
*   **Terminal Potential Difference:** The potential difference across the terminals of a battery when it is in a complete circuit. Measured in volts (V).
*   **Voltage:** A measure of work required/done to move a certain amount of charge. Equivalent to $J \cdot C^{-1}$.
*   **Current:** The rate at which charge moves/flows. Measured in amperes (A), which is equivalent to $C \cdot s^{-1}$.
*   **Conventional Current:** Flows from the positive terminal of a battery, through a circuit, to the negative terminal.
*   **Resistance:** A measure of how much work must be done for charge to flow through a circuit element. Measured in ohms ($\Omega$), which is equivalent to $V \cdot A^{-1}$. Resistance depends on the material and physical characteristics (length and cross-sectional area) of the element.

## Circuit Components and Measurement

*   **Ammeter:** Measures current; must be connected in **series**.
*   **Voltmeter:** Measures potential difference (voltage); must be connected in **parallel**.

## Resistor Configurations

### Resistors in Series
*   Current is constant through resistors in series.
*   Resistors in series act as **voltage dividers** (the sum of the voltages equals the voltage across the entire set).
*   **Total Resistance Formula:**
    $$R_S = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$$

### Resistors in Parallel
*   Voltage is constant across resistors in parallel.
*   Resistors in parallel act as **current divides** (the sum of the current through each branch equals the total current).
*   **Total Resistance Formula:**
    $$\frac{1}{R_P} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots$$
*   **For two parallel resistors:**
    $$R_P = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

### Series and Parallel Circuits: Worked Examples

#### 1. Voltage in a Series Circuit
**Problem:** If the total voltage ($V_s$) is $12\text{ V}$ and the voltage across two resistors is $3\text{ V}$ and $2\text{ V}$ respectively, what is the voltage across the unknown resistor?

**Solution:**
In a series circuit, the total voltage is the sum of the individual voltages:
$$V_s = V_1 + V_2 + V_{unknown}$$
$$12\text{ V} = 3\text{ V} + 2\text{ V} + V_{unknown}$$
$$V_{unknown} = 7\text{ V}$$

#### 2. Total Resistance in a Parallel Circuit
**Problem:** There are 3 resistors in parallel with resistances of $3\text{ }\Omega$, $4\text{ }\Omega$, and $11\text{ }\Omega$. What is the total resistance ($R_p$) of the parallel combination?

**Solution:**
The formula for resistors in parallel is:
$$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}$$

Using the common denominator formula:
$$R_p = \frac{R_1 R_2 R_3}{R_1 R_2 + R_2 R_3 + R_1 R_3}$$
$$R_p = \frac{(3)(4)(11)}{(3)(4) + (4)(11) + (3)(11)}$$
$$R_p = \frac{132}{12 + 44 + 33}$$
$$R_p = \frac{132}{89}$$
$$R_p \approx 1,48\text{ }\Omega$$

#### 4. Finding an Unknown Resistor in Parallel
**Problem:** The total resistance of two resistors in parallel is $3\text{ }\Omega$. If one resistor has a resistance of $5\text{ }\Omega$, what is the resistance of the other resistor?

**Solution:**
Using the parallel resistance formula:
$$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$$
$$\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{R_2}$$
$$\frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5}$$
$$\frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{5 - 3}{15}$$
$$\frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{2}{15}$$
$$R_2 = \frac{15}{2} = 7,5\text{ }\Omega$$

### Worked Example: Calculating an Unknown Resistor in Parallel

Given a parallel circuit where the total equivalent resistance $R_P = 3\,\Omega$ and one resistor $R_1 = 5\,\Omega$, calculate the value of the second resistor $R_2$.

**Formula:**
$$R_P = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

**Step-by-step Calculation:**
1. Rearrange the formula to solve for $R_2$:
   $$R_P(R_1 + R_2) = R_1 R_2$$
   $$R_p R_1 + R_p R_2 - R_1 R_2 = 0$$
   $$R_2(R_p - R_1) = -R_p R_1$$
   $$R_2 = \frac{-R_p R_1}{R_p - R_1}$$

2. Substitute the known values:
   $$R_2 = \frac{-(3)(5)}{3 - 5}$$
   $$R_2 = \frac{-15}{-2}$$
   $$R_2 = 7,5\,\Omega$$
