# Chapter 21: Motion in One Dimension

## Introduction
Motion in one dimension describes how objects move along a straight line, such as cars on a straight road or trains on straight tracks. To describe this motion accurately, we use three primary features:

1.  **Position and Displacement:** Describes an object's location and any change in that location.
2.  **Speed and Velocity:** Describes how fast an object is moving, with velocity also specifying the direction of motion.
3.  **Acceleration:** Describes how quickly an object's speed and velocity are changing.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Fact Box
*   **Jerk:** The term used to describe how fast the acceleration of an object is changing.

---

## Reference Frame
To describe motion effectively, we must define an object's **position**. Stating that an object is "here" or "there" is insufficient. We must use **reference points** (known locations) to specify exactly where an object is located.

## 1. Defining Location
To describe a location precisely, one must establish a **reference point** and a **coordinate system**. 

*   **Reference Point:** A specific location used as a starting point for measurements (e.g., a classroom door).
*   **Coordinate System:** A set of directions (e.g., inside/outside, left/right, forward/backward) used to define position relative to the reference point.

### Definition: Frame of Reference
A **frame of reference** is a reference point combined with a set of directions.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

## 2. 1-Dimensional Coordinate Systems
In a 1-dimensional coordinate system, we define one direction as the **positive (+)** direction. The opposite direction is then automatically the **negative (-)** direction.

*   **Origin:** The point where the coordinate system begins (the reference point).
*   **Notation:** Positions are often denoted using vectors, such as $\vec{x}_i$.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->
*Figure 21.1: Graphical representation of a 1-dimensional frame of reference.*

## 3. Key Principles
*   **Consistency:** You can define different frames of reference for the same physical problem. Regardless of the frame chosen, the physical results (the outcome of the motion) will remain the same.
*   **Application:** For example, if a boy is standing inside a moving train, both an observer on the platform and the boy can define their locations using different reference points and directions, yet both will accurately describe the motion of the train.

## 1. Frame of Reference
Motion is relative to the observer. A **frame of reference** is a system used to describe the position and motion of an object. To define a frame of reference, you must establish:
*   **An origin:** The point from which measurements are taken (e.g., where you are standing on a platform).
*   **A positive direction:** A chosen direction that is considered positive (e.g., to your right). The opposite direction is then considered negative.

### Example
If you are standing on a platform and a train moves from left to right:
*   From your frame of reference, the train (and a boy inside it) is moving in the positive direction.
*   From the boy's frame of reference (inside the train), he is not moving; he is stationary relative to the train.

## Distance vs. Displacement

*   **Distance:** The total length of the path travelled between a starting point and a finishing point.
*   **Displacement:** The change in an object's position.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Definition: Displacement
Displacement is a vector quantity that points from the initial position ($\vec{x}_i$) to the final position ($\vec{x}_f$).

| Quantity | Unit Name | Unit Symbol |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Displacement ($\Delta\vec{x}$) | metre | m |

---

## Calculating Displacement

Displacement is defined as the final position minus the initial position. Because it has both magnitude and direction, it is a vector.

The formula for displacement is:
$$\Delta\vec{x} = \vec{x}_f - \vec{x}_i$$

### Key Concepts
*   **The $\Delta$ (Delta) Symbol:** In physics, $\Delta$ is used as a shorthand for "final minus initial".
*   **Vector Nature:** Displacement represents the vector you add to the initial position vector to reach the final position vector.
*   **Path Independence:** Displacement does not depend on the actual path taken; it only depends on the starting and ending positions.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

## Tip: Understanding Terminology
*   **Initial:** Refers to the start of the situation or problem.
*   **Final:** Refers to the end of the situation or problem.
*   **Negative Results:** It is common for the final position to be smaller than the initial position, resulting in a negative displacement. This is mathematically correct and indicates a change in direction.

---

## Worked Example
If you roll a ball $5\text{ m}$ along a floor in a straight line:
*   **Initial position ($\vec{x}_i$):** $0\text{ m}$
*   **Direction of motion:** Positive
*   **Displacement ($\Delta\vec{x}$):** $5\text{ m}$

## Worked Example: Calculating Distance and Displacement

Consider a street with houses arranged in a line, each separated by $100\text{ m}$.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Scenario:** Komal walks to Kevin's house, then walks to the school.

### 1. Analysis
*   **Distance:** Komal covers $400\text{ m}$ to Kevin's house and $500\text{ m}$ from Kevin's house to the school.
    *   Total distance = $400\text{ m} + 500\text{ m} = 900\text{ m}$.
*   **Displacement:** Displacement only considers the starting position and the end position.
    *   Starting point: Komal's house.
    *   End point: School.
    *   Displacement = $100\text{ m}$ towards the school.

### 2. Mathematical Calculation
To calculate these values, we define a reference point (Komal's house) and a positive direction (towards Kevin's house). Consequently, the direction towards the school is negative.

| Measurement | Calculation | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Distance ($D$)** | $400\text{ m} + 500\text{ m}$ | $900\text{ m}$ |
| **Displacement ($\Delta\vec{x}$)** | $\vec{x}_f - \vec{x}_i = -100\text{ m} + 0\text{ m}$ | $-100\text{ m}$ |

*   **Interpretation of the result:** The displacement is $-100\text{ m}$. The negative sign indicates that the displacement is $100\text{ m}$ in the direction opposite to the one chosen as positive at the start of the calculation. Therefore, the displacement is $100\text{ m}$ towards the school.

### Speed and Velocity

#### Definition: Average Speed
Average speed is the total distance ($D$) travelled divided by the time ($\Delta t$) taken for the journey.

*   **Quantity:** Average speed ($v_{av}$)
*   **Unit Name:** Metre per second
*   **Unit Symbol:** $\text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$

#### Definition: Average Velocity
Average velocity is the change in position of a body divided by the time it took for the displacement to occur.

*   **Quantity:** Average velocity ($\vec{v}_{av}$)
*   **Unit Name:** Metre per second
*   **Unit Symbol:** $\text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$

#### Key Concepts
*   **Difference between Distance and Displacement:** It is important to note that average speed can be a very large number while the average velocity is zero, depending on the path taken.
*   **Rate of Change:** Average velocity is defined as the rate of change of position. It indicates how much an object's position changes per unit of time.
*   **Vector Nature:** Velocity is a vector quantity. We use the symbol $\vec{v}_{av}$ to represent average velocity.

# Chapter 21: Motion in One Dimension

## 1. Average Velocity
Average velocity is defined as the change in position (displacement) divided by the change in time.

**Formula:**
$$\vec{v}_{av} = \frac{\Delta\vec{x}}{\Delta t}$$

*   **Units:** $\text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$
*   **Direction:** Velocity is a vector. A positive velocity indicates motion in the chosen positive direction, while a negative velocity indicates motion in the opposite direction.

## 2. Average Speed
Average speed is defined as the total distance travelled divided by the total time taken. Since distance and time are scalars, speed is also a scalar quantity.

**Formula:**
$$v_{av} = \frac{D}{\Delta t}$$

*   **Units:** $\text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$

    $$\Delta\vec{x} = 0\text{ m}$$

### Acceleration

#### Definition: Average Acceleration
Average acceleration is the change in average velocity divided by the time taken.

*   **Quantity:** Average acceleration ($\vec{a}_{av}$)
*   **Unit name:** metre per second squared
*   **Unit symbol:** $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$

Acceleration is a measure of how fast the velocity of an object changes in time. If we have a change in velocity ($\Delta\vec{v}$) over a time interval ($\Delta t$), then the average acceleration ($\vec{a}_{av}$) is defined as:

$$\text{average acceleration (in m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}\text{)} = \frac{\text{change in velocity (in m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\text{)}}{\text{change in time (in s)}}$$

The mathematical formula is:

$$\vec{a}_{av} = \frac{\Delta\vec{v}}{\Delta t}$$

**Note:** We only deal with problems involving constant acceleration. This means that the average acceleration and the instantaneous acceleration are the same.

## Acceleration

Acceleration is defined as the rate of change of velocity. It is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction.

### Formula
The magnitude of acceleration is calculated using the following formula:
$$a = \frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{\Delta t}$$

### Key Concepts
*   **Nature of Acceleration:** Acceleration describes how the motion of an object changes. It does not provide information about how fast an object is moving or its specific direction on its own.
*   **Signs of Acceleration:** Like velocity, acceleration can be positive or negative.
    *   If velocity and acceleration have the **same sign**, the object is **speeding up**.
    *   If velocity and acceleration have **opposite signs**, the object is **slowing down**.

> **Tip:** Avoid using the word *deceleration* to refer to negative acceleration. Deceleration usually implies slowing down, but an object can slow down with either positive or negative acceleration depending on the direction of its velocity. Always consider the sign of the velocity relative to the acceleration to determine if an object is slowing down.

## Worked Example: Acceleration

### Question
A car accelerates uniformly from an initial velocity of $2\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ to a final velocity of $10\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ in $8\text{ seconds}$. It then slows down uniformly to a final velocity of $4\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ in $6\text{ seconds}$. Calculate the acceleration of the car during the first $8\text{ seconds}$ and during the last $6\text{ seconds}$.

### Solution

**Part 1: First 8 seconds**
*   Initial velocity ($v_i$) = $2\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$
*   Final velocity ($v_f$) = $10\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$
*   Time ($\Delta t$) = $8\text{ s}$

$$a = \frac{v_f - v_i}{\Delta t}$$
$$a = \frac{10 - 2}{8}$$
$$a = \frac{8}{8} = 1\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$$

**Part 2: Last 6 seconds**
*   Initial velocity ($v_i$) = $10\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$
*   Final velocity ($v_f$) = $4\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$

### Chapter 21: Motion in One Dimension

#### Historical Context
Galileo Galilei of Pisa, Italy, was the first to determine the correct mathematical law for acceleration: the total distance covered, starting from rest, is proportional to the square of the time. He concluded that objects retain their velocity unless a force—often friction—acts upon them, refuting the Aristotelian hypothesis that objects "naturally" slow down and stop unless a force acts upon them. This principle was incorporated into Newton’s laws of motion (1st law).

## Acceleration
Acceleration ($\vec{a}$) is defined as the rate of change of velocity. It is calculated using the following formula:

$$\vec{a} = \frac{\Delta\vec{v}}{t}$$

Where $\Delta\vec{v}$ is the change in velocity, defined as $\Delta\vec{v} = \vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i$. Substituting this into the formula gives:

$$\vec{a} = \frac{\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i}{t}$$

By rearranging this formula to solve for the final velocity ($\vec{v}_f$), we get:

$$\vec{v}_f = \vec{v}_i + \vec{a}t$$

---

## Derivation of Displacement from Velocity vs. Time Graphs
For **uniformly accelerated motion**, the velocity vs. time graph is a straight line. The displacement of an object can be calculated by finding the area under this graph.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

To find the total displacement, we calculate the area under the graph by splitting the shaded region into a rectangle and a triangle.

### 1. Area of the Triangle ($\text{Area}_{\triangle}$)
The triangle represents the change in velocity over time.
$$\text{Area}_{\triangle} = \frac{1}{2}b \times h$$
$$\text{Area}_{\triangle} = \frac{1}{2}t \times (\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i)$$
$$\text{Area}_{\triangle} = \frac{1}{2}\vec{v}_f t - \frac{1}{2}\vec{v}_i t$$

### 2. Area of the Rectangle ($\text{Area}_{\square}$)
The rectangle represents the initial velocity maintained over time.
$$\text{Area}_{\square} = \ell \times b$$
$$\text{Area}_{\square} = t \times \vec{v}_i$$
$$\text{Area}_{\square} = \vec{v}_i t$$

---

### Chapter 21: Motion in One Dimension

#### Displacement Equations
Displacement can be calculated using the area under a velocity-time graph, represented as the sum of a rectangle and a triangle:

$$\text{Displacement} = \text{Area}_{\square} + \text{Area}_{\triangle}$$
$$\Delta \vec{x} = v_i t + \frac{1}{2}v_f t - \frac{1}{2}v_i t$$
$$\Delta \vec{x} = \left( \frac{v_i + v_f}{2} \right) t$$

---

#### Derivation of Equation 21.3
This equation is derived by eliminating the final velocity ($v_f$) from the standard kinematic equations. 

Starting with the definition of final velocity:
$$\vec{v}_f = \vec{v}_i + \vec{a}t$$

Substituting this into the displacement equation:
$$\Delta \vec{x} = \left( \frac{\vec{v}_i + (\vec{v}_i + \vec{a}t)}{2} \right) t$$
$$\Delta \vec{x} = \frac{2\vec{v}_i t + \vec{a}t^2}{2}$$
$$\Delta \vec{x} = \vec{v}_i t + \frac{1}{2}\vec{a}t^2$$

---

#### Derivation of Equation 21.4
This equation is derived by eliminating the time variable ($t$) from the displacement equation. 

From the definition of acceleration, we know:
$$t = \frac{\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i}{a}$$

Substituting this expression for $t$ into the displacement equation will yield the final kinematic relationship.

---

### Derivation of the Equation of Motion (Independent of Time)

The following derivation shows the relationship between final velocity, initial velocity, acceleration, and displacement without requiring the time variable:

$$\Delta \vec{x} = \vec{v}_i \left( \frac{\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i}{\vec{a}} \right) + \frac{1}{2} \vec{a} \left( \frac{\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i}{\vec{a}} \right)^2$$
$$\Delta \vec{x} = \frac{\vec{v}_i \vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i^2}{\vec{a}} + \frac{1}{2} \vec{a} \left( \frac{\vec{v}_f^2 - 2\vec{v}_i \vec{v}_f + \vec{v}_i^2}{\vec{a}^2} \right)$$
$$\Delta \vec{x} = \frac{\vec{v}_i \vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i^2}{\vec{a}} + \frac{\vec{v}_f^2 - 2\vec{v}_i \vec{v}_f + \vec{v}_i^2}{2\vec{a}}$$
$$2\vec{a}\Delta \vec{x} = -2\vec{v}_i^2 + \vec{v}_f^2 + \vec{v}_i^2$$
$$\vec{v}_f^2 = \vec{v}_i^2 + 2\vec{a}\Delta \vec{x}$$

#### Step 2: Calculate the distance AB (Reaction Distance)
Before the brakes are applied, the truck travels at a constant velocity. Since there is no acceleration, we use the standard velocity formula:

$$\vec{v}_f = \vec{v}_i + at$$

$$0 = 10\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1} + (-1,25\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2})t$$
$$-10\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1} = (-1,25\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2})t$$

**Result:** It takes $8\text{ seconds}$ for the truck to stop after the brakes are applied.

*   **Displacement ($\Delta \vec{x}$):** How far an object is from the reference point. It is the shortest distance between the object and the reference point. It has magnitude and direction, making it a **vector**.
*   **Distance ($D$):** The length of the path travelled from the starting point to the end point. It has magnitude only, making it a **scalar**.
*   **Speed ($v$):** The distance covered ($D$) divided by the time taken ($\Delta t$):
    $$v = \frac{D}{\Delta t}$$
*   **Average velocity ($\vec{v}_{av}$):** The displacement ($\Delta \vec{x}$) divided by the time taken ($\Delta t$):
    $$\vec{v}_{av} = \frac{\Delta \vec{x}}{\Delta t}$$
*   **Instantaneous speed:** The speed at a specific instant in time.
*   **Instantaneous velocity:** The velocity at a specific instant in time.

#### Problem 8: Motion of a Car and a Motorcycle
A car, travelling at constant velocity, passes a stationary motorcycle at a traffic light. As the car overtakes the motorcycle, the motorcycle accelerates uniformly from rest for $10\text{ s}$.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**(a) Find the magnitude of the constant velocity of the car.**
The car's velocity ($v$) is the gradient of the displacement-time graph:
$$v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{300\text{ m}}{10\text{ s}} = 30\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

**(b) Show by calculation that the acceleration of the motorcycle for the first $10\text{ s}$ is $7,5\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$.**
Using the equation of motion $\Delta x = v_i \Delta t + \frac{1}{2} a (\Delta t)^2$:
Given $v_i = 0\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$, $\Delta x = 375\text{ m}$, and $\Delta t = 10\text{ s}$:
$$375 = 0(10) + \frac{1}{2} a (10)^2$$
$$375 = 50a$$
$$a = \frac{375}{50} = 7,5\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$$

**(c) Calculate how long it will take the motorcycle to catch up with the car (point X).**
At point X, the displacements are equal ($x_{car} = x_{motorcycle}$):
$$v_{car} \cdot t = \frac{1}{2} a t^2$$
$$30t = \frac{1}{2} (7,5) t^2$$
$$30 = 3,75t$$
$$t = \frac{30}{3,75} = 8\text{ s}$$

**(d) How far behind the motorcycle will the car be after $15\text{ seconds}$?**
*   **Car displacement at $15\text{ s}$:** $x_c = 30 \times 15 = 450\text{ m}$
*   **Motorcycle displacement at $15\text{ s}$:**
    *   First $10\text{ s}$: $x = 375\text{ m}$
    *   From $10\text{ s}$ to $15\text{ s}$ ($5\text{ s}$ duration), the motorcycle moves at constant velocity $v_f = v_i + a\Delta t = 0 + (7,5)(10) = 75\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.
    *   Distance covered in last $5\text{ s}$: $d = v \cdot t = 75 \times 5 = 375\text{ m}$.
    *   Total $x_m = 375 + 375 = 750\text{ m}$.
*   **Difference:** $750\text{ m} - 450\text{ m} = 300\text{ m}$. The car is $300\text{ m}$ behind.

The direction of motion is relative to a chosen positive direction. If the direction from the left pole to the right pole is defined as positive, motion from the right pole to the left pole is considered negative.

### Lesson: Unit Conversions and Uniform Acceleration

#### 1. Unit Conversion Concepts
To convert units (such as $\text{km/h}$ to $\text{m/s}$), we use conversion factors where the units we wish to remove are placed in the opposite position (numerator vs. denominator) to cancel them out.

**Key Conversion Factors:**
*   $1 \text{ km} = 1000 \text{ m}$
*   $1 \text{ hour} = 60 \text{ minutes} = 3600 \text{ seconds}$

**Worked Example: Converting Speed**
To convert $120 \text{ km/h}$ to $\text{m/s}$:
$$\frac{120 \text{ km}}{1 \text{ hour}} \times \frac{1000 \text{ m}}{1 \text{ km}} \times \frac{1 \text{ hr}}{60 \text{ min}} \times \frac{1 \text{ min}}{60 \text{ sec}} = 33,33 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

---

#### 2. Calculating Time and Distance
When an object moves at a constant velocity, the time taken to cover a specific distance is calculated using:
$$t = \frac{d}{v}$$

**Worked Example: Rabbit crossing a freeway**
*   **Distance:** The freeway has 3 lanes, each $3 \text{ m}$ wide. Total distance $d = 3 \times 3 \text{ m} = 9 \text{ m}$.
*   **Speed:** The rabbit's speed is $2,78 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.
*   **Calculation:**
$$t = \frac{9 \text{ m}}{2,78 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}} = 3,24 \text{ s}$$

---

#### 3. Uniform Acceleration
Acceleration ($a$) is defined as the rate of change of velocity over time. 

**Formula:**
$$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_f - v_i}{t_f - t_i}$$

Where:
*   $v_f$ = final velocity
*   $v_i$ = initial velocity
*   $\Delta t$ = change in time

**Exercise 21-4: Worked Example**
An athlete accelerates uniformly from an initial velocity of $0 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ to a final velocity of $4 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ in $2$ seconds. Calculate the acceleration.

**Solution:**
1. Identify variables: $v_i = 0 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$, $v_f = 4 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$, $\Delta t = 2 \text{ s}$.
2. Apply the formula:
$$a = \frac{4 - 0}{2 - 0}$$
$$a = \frac{4}{2}$$
$$a = 2 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$$

#### Problem 12: Average Speed vs. Instantaneous Speed
A traffic officer monitors a valley. A car enters $1 \, km$ to the left and leaves $1 \, km$ to the right. A white Toyota enters at 11:01:30 and leaves at 11:02:42. The officer records the car's speed as $140 \, km \cdot hr^{-1}$.

A bus on a straight road starts from rest at a bus stop and accelerates at $2 \, m \cdot s^{-2}$ until it reaches a speed of $20 \, m \cdot s^{-1}$. b) What is the average velocity of the bus during the trip?

#### 1. Visual Representation

#### 2. Part (a): Total Time Calculation
To find the total time, we must calculate the time taken for each of the three phases of the journey.

**Phase 1: Acceleration**

$$20 = 0 + (2)t$$

#### 3. Part (b): Average Velocity Calculation
Average velocity is defined as the total displacement divided by the total time:
$$v_{avg} = \frac{\Delta x_{total}}{\Delta t_{total}}$$

*   **Phase 1 (Acceleration):**

### Motion Analysis: Calculating Total Displacement and Average Velocity

This section demonstrates how to calculate the total displacement and average velocity of an object (a bus) moving through three distinct phases of a journey.

#### 1. Displacement Calculations

**Phase 1:**
The initial displacement is given as:
$$\Delta x = +100\,m$$

**Phase 2:**
The bus travels at a constant speed of $20\,m\cdot s^{-1}$ for $20\,s$.

$$\text{Total } \Delta x = 100\,m + 400\,m + 50\,m = +550\,m$$

Average velocity is defined as the total displacement divided by the total time taken. 

*Note: Based on the provided calculation, the total time used is $35\,s$.*

$$\text{Average velocity} = \frac{\text{Total Displacement}}{\text{Total Time}}$$
$$\text{Average velocity} = \frac{550}{35} = 15,71\,m\cdot s^{-1}$$
