## Rounding Off
In science, writing numbers to an infinite or extremely high number of decimal places is impractical and often unnecessary. Rounding off allows us to approximate numbers to a manageable level of precision.

### The Rounding Process
To round a decimal number to a specific number of decimal places:

1. **Count:** Count the required number of decimal places from the decimal point.
2. **Mark:** Place a vertical bar ($|$) after the last digit you wish to keep.
3. **Evaluate:** Look at the first digit to the right of the bar ($|$).
4. **Decide:**
    * **Round up:** If the first digit after the bar is greater than 5, increase the final kept digit by 1.
    * **Round down:** (Implied) If the digit is less than 5, the final kept digit remains the same.

#### Worked Example
To round $2,6525272$ to three decimal places:
* **Step 1:** Count three places: $2,652$
* **Step 2:** Mark with a bar: $2,652|5272$
* **Step 3:** The first digit after the bar is $5$.

### Scientific Notation

Scientific notation is a compact way to represent very large or very small numbers using the general form:

$$N \times 10^n$$

#### Key Definitions
*   **$N$**: A decimal number between $0$ and $10$ (rounded to a few decimal places).
*   **$n$**: The **exponent**, which must be an integer.

#### Rules for the Exponent ($n$)
*   If $n > 0$: The decimal place in $N$ moves to the **right**.
    *   Example: $3,24 \times 10^3 = 3\,240$
*   If $n < 0$: The decimal place in $N$ moves to the **left**.
    *   Example: $3,24 \times 10^{-3} = 0,00324$

#### Converting to Scientific Notation
To convert a number:
1.  Determine the value of $N$ by placing the decimal comma so that the number is between $1$ and $10$.
2.  Count the number of places the decimal comma moved to determine the value of $n$.

**Example: Speed of Light**
*   Value: $299\,792\,458 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$
*   To two decimal places: $N = 3,00$
*   The decimal moved $8$ places to the left, so $n = 8$.
*   Result: $3,00 \times 10^8 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$

## Addition and Subtraction in Scientific Notation

To add or subtract numbers in scientific notation, follow these steps:

1.  **Make all the exponents the same:** Adjust the numbers so that the power of $10$ is identical for all terms.
2.  **Carry out the addition and subtraction:** Add or subtract the coefficients ($N$ parts) while keeping the common exponent.
3.  **Write the final answer:** Re-attach the common exponent to the result.

### Worked Example
**Problem:** Calculate $1,99 \times 10^{-26} + 0,199 \times 10^{-25} - 1,67 \times 10^{-27}$

**Solution:**
*   **Step 1:** Make exponents the same (using $10^{-25}$ as the base):
    *   $1,99 \times 10^{-26} = 0,199 \times 10^{-25}$
    *   $1,67 \times 10^{-27} = 0,0167 \times 10^{-25}$
*   **Step 2:** Add and subtract the coefficients:
    *   $0,199 + 0,199 - 0,0167 = 0,3813$
*   **Step 3:** Final answer:
    *   $0,3813 \times 10^{-25}$

*Note: The same process applies to positive exponents. For example:*
$5,1 \times 10^3 + 4,2 \times 10^4 = 0,51 \times 10^4 + 4,2 \times 10^4 = 4,71 \times 10^4$

## Units

Units are essential for providing context to measurements. Without a unit, a numerical value is incomplete (e.g., "2 wide and 6 long" is ambiguous; "2 metres wide and 6 metres long" is precise). All physical quantities, such as time, temperature, and distance, require units.

### Definition: Physical Quantity
A **physical quantity** is anything that can be measured. Examples include length, temperature, distance, and time.

### Systems of Units
There are several established systems of units used in science:
*   **SI units** (Système International)
*   **c.g.s units** (centimetre-gram-second)
*   **Imperial units**
*   **Natural units**

## SI Units

In this course, we use SI units, which are the internationally agreed-upon units for scientific measurement.

> **DEFINITION: SI Units**
> The name *SI units* comes from the French *Système International d'Unités*, which means *international system of units*.

### Base SI Units
There are seven base SI units. All other physical quantities have units that can be derived from these seven. They are called "base units" because none of them can be expressed as a combination of the other six. They function similarly to the 26 letters of the alphabet, which can be combined to form many different words.

| Base quantity | Name | Symbol |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| length | metre | m |
| mass | kilogram | kg |
| time | second | s |
| electric current | ampere | A |
| temperature | kelvin | K |
| amount of substance | mole | mol |
| luminous intensity | candela | cd |

### 1.3 Alternative Unit Systems

Beyond the standard SI units, other systems exist for specific scientific or historical contexts:

*   **c.g.s. Units:** In this system, the metre is replaced by the centimetre and the kilogram by the gram. Because these base units change, all derived units (such as force and work) are also different. This system is primarily used in astrophysics and atomic physics.
*   **Imperial Units:** Historically derived from royal decrees, these units (e.g., pounds, miles, gallons, yards) are still used by the Americans and British. Because these units vary by region, they make scientific communication difficult, which served as the primary motivation for adopting the internationally agreed-upon SI system.
*   **Natural Units:** A sophisticated system where fundamental quantities (such as the speed of light) are set equal to $1$. All other physical quantities are then built from these fundamental constants. This system is used in high-energy physics and quantum mechanics.

---

### Combinations of SI Base Units

While SI base units are the standard, many combinations are given special names to make calculations easier. It is always correct to reduce these back to their base units.

#### Key Examples of Derived Units

#### Prefixes of Base Units
In science, prefixes are used with units to represent powers of $10$. 

*   **The Kilogram Exception:** The kilogram ($\text{kg}$) is the only SI base unit that already contains a prefix. It is equal to $1000\text{ g}$ or $1 \times 10^{3}\text{ g}$. The prefix "k" (kilo) replaces the $10^{3}$ factor.
*   **Case Sensitivity:** The case of the prefix symbol is critical. 
    *   **Uppercase:** Used for exponents greater than one (e.g., $\text{M}$ for mega, which is $10^{6}$).
    *   **Lowercase:** Used for exponents less than one (e.g., $\text{m}$ for milli, which is $10^{-3}$).

> **TIP: Writing Units**
> When writing combinations of base SI units, place a dot ($\cdot$) between the units to indicate that different base units are used. For example, the symbol for metres per second is correctly written as $\text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$, and not as $\text{ms}^{-1}$ or $\text{m/s}$. While other options may be accepted in some contexts, $\text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$ is the standard format used in this curriculum.

## Changing the Subject of a Formula

In science, it is often necessary to rearrange equations to solve for a specific variable. This process is known as changing the subject of a formula.

### 1. Moles
The standard equation to calculate the number of moles ($n$) from mass ($m$) and molar mass ($M$) is:
$$n = \frac{m}{M}$$

To solve for the molar mass ($M$), we rearrange the equation:
1. Multiply both sides by $M$: $nM = m$
2. Divide both sides by $n$: $M = \frac{m}{n}$

To solve for mass ($m$):
$$m = nM$$

### 2. Energy of a Photon
The equation for the energy of a photon ($E$) using Planck's constant ($h$), the speed of light ($c$), and wavelength ($\lambda$) is:
$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

To solve for the speed of light ($c$):
1. Multiply both sides by $\lambda$: $E\lambda = hc$
2. Divide both sides by $h$: $c = \frac{E\lambda}{h}$

Using the same method, we can derive formulas for other variables:
*   To find wavelength: $\lambda = \frac{hc}{E}$
*   To find Planck's constant: $h = \frac{E\lambda}{c}$

## Rate, Proportion, and Ratios

In science, we frequently analyze how one quantity relates to another or how a quantity changes over a period of time. This requires an understanding of rates, proportions, and ratios.

### Rate
A rate describes how a quantity changes in relation to another quantity, typically time.

---

### 1.3 Skills for Science: Rates, Ratios, and Proportions

#### Rates
The rate at which an event occurs is defined as the number of times it happens over a specific period of time. A rate is always expressed as a change per unit of time.

*   **Rate of change of velocity:** $\frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{t}$
*   **Rate of change of concentration:** $\frac{\Delta C}{t}$

*Note: The symbol $\Delta$ (delta) represents a "change in".*

---

#### Ratios and Fractions
*   **Fractions:** A number representing a part of a whole, written as $\frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ is the numerator and $b$ is the denominator.
*   **Ratios:** Used to describe the relative size of one quantity compared to another (e.g., $2:1$ or $4:3$). Ratios can also be expressed as fractions or percentages (fractions with a denominator of $100$).

---

#### Proportion
Proportion describes the relationship between values or constants.

##### 1. Directly Proportional
Two values are directly proportional when a change in one leads to the same type of change in the other (a "more-more" relationship).

*   **Mathematical notation:** $y \propto x$
*   **Equation:** $y = kx$ (where $k$ is the proportionality constant).
*   **Graphical representation:** A straight-line graph passing through the origin $(0; 0)$.

*   **Mathematical notation:** $y \propto \frac{1}{x}$
*   **Equation:** $y = \frac{k}{x}$

## Experiments: The Scientific Method
When a scientist performs an experiment, they follow a structured process known as the **scientific method**.

### The Scientific Process
1. **Observe:** Identify an event and formulate an answerable question about it.
2. **Hypothesis:** Create a theory about the event that provides a sensible result.
3. **Design:** Plan an experiment to test the theory. This involves:
    * **Fixed factors:** Variables that will not change during the experiment.
    * **Independent variable:** The variable that you set or change.
    * **Dependent variable:** The variable that you measure.
4. **Collect and Interpret:** Gather data accurately and analyze what it means.
5. **Conclusion:** Draw conclusions based on the experimental results.
6. **Evaluate:** Decide whether the hypothesis is correct or incorrect.
7. **Verify:** Repeat the experiment or have others repeat it to confirm the results.

### Reporting Practical Work
In your studies, you will often be provided with the first three steps (Observation, Hypothesis, and Design) and required to complete the final four. To ensure your work is understandable and repeatable by others, always use the following standard layout:

*   **Aim:** A brief sentence describing the purpose of the experiment.
*   **Apparatus:** A list of the equipment used and a sketch of the setup.

## General Safety Rules

The following are the essential guidelines and rules to observe when working in a laboratory:

1. **Personal Responsibility:** You are responsible for your own safety as well as the safety of others in the laboratory.
2. **No Consumption:** Do not eat or drink in the laboratory. Do not use laboratory glassware to eat or drink from.
3. **Conduct:** Always behave responsibly. Do not run around or play practical jokes.
4. **Accidents:** In case of accidents or chemical spills, call your teacher at once.
5. **Waste Disposal:** Always check with your teacher how to dispose of waste. Chemicals should not be disposed of down the sink.
6. **Authorized Experiments:** Only perform the experiments that your teacher instructs you to. Never mix chemicals for fun.
7. **Supervision:** Never perform experiments alone.
8. **Chemical Safety:** Always check the safety data of any chemicals you are going to use.
9. **Follow Instructions:** Follow the given instructions exactly. Do not mix up steps or try things in a different order.
10. **Caution:** Be alert and careful when handling chemicals, hot glassware, etc.
11. **Cleanup:** Ensure all Bunsen burners are turned off at the end of the practical and all chemical containers are sealed.
12. **Dilution Rule:** Never add water to acid. Always add the acid to water.
13. **Glassware Safety:** Never heat thick glassware as it will break (e.g., do not heat measuring cylinders).
14. **Smelling Chemicals:** When you are smelling chemicals, place the container on a laboratory bench and waft the fumes toward you (do not inhale directly).

#### Unit Conversion: Speed
**Problem:** The top speed of the Concorde is $844\text{ km}\cdot\text{hr}^{-1}$. Convert this speed to $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.

**Solution:**
To convert from $\text{km}\cdot\text{hr}^{-1}$ to $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$, divide the value by $3,6$:
$$844\text{ km}\cdot\text{hr}^{-1} = (844 \div 3,6)\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1} = 234,44\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$

---

#### Unit Conversion: Temperature
**Problem:** The boiling point of water is $100^\circ\text{C}$. What is the boiling point of water in Kelvin?

**Solution:**
To convert from Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$) to Kelvin ($\text{K}$), add $273$:
$$100^\circ\text{C} = (100 + 273)\text{K} = 373\text{ K}$$
