# Chapter 10: Sound

## Introduction
Our sense of hearing allows us to perceive a wide range of sounds and determine their direction. Sound is generated by a disturbance in the air that creates a pulse. When this pulse reaches the ear, it causes the eardrum to vibrate, which the brain interprets as sound. A continuous train of these pulses is referred to as a **wave**.

*   **Definition:** Sound is a longitudinal, pressure wave consisting of alternating regions of high and low pressure in the air.

---

## Sound Waves
Sound waves are created by vibrations. A common example used to study these waves is a **tuning fork**.

### How Sound Waves are Formed
1.  **Vibration:** When a tuning fork vibrates, it pushes against the air particles in front of it.
2.  **Compression:** As air particles are pushed together, a region of high pressure called a **compression** is formed.
3.  **Rarefaction:** The particles behind the compression move further apart, creating a region of low pressure called a **rarefaction**.
4.  **Propagation:** As particles continue to push against each other, the disturbance travels through the air as a wave.

### Key Characteristics
*   **Pressure Waves:** Because sound involves variations in air pressure, sound waves are classified as pressure waves.
*   **Medium Dependency:** Sound waves travel faster in media where particles are closer together (such as solids or liquids compared to gases), as the particles can transfer the energy of the vibration more efficiently.

## Transmission of Sound
Sound waves require a medium (a substance) to travel through. The speed at which sound travels depends on the density of the medium:
*   **Solids:** Sound travels fastest because particles are closest together.
*   **Liquids:** Sound travels faster than in air.
*   **Gases (Air):** Sound travels slowest because particles are further apart.

# Chapter 10: Sound - Speed of Sound

## Factors Affecting the Speed of Sound

The speed of sound is not constant; it is influenced by the physical properties of the medium through which it travels.

### 1. The Medium (State of Matter)
Sound travels at different speeds depending on the density and arrangement of particles in a medium:
*   **Solids:** Sound travels fastest here because particles are packed closely together, allowing for efficient energy transfer.
*   **Liquids:** Sound travels slower than in solids.
*   **Gases:** Sound travels slowest in gases as particles are further apart.

### 2. Temperature
The speed of sound is directly proportional to the temperature of the medium. As a substance is heated, its particles gain more kinetic energy and vibrate or move faster. This increased motion allows sound waves to be transmitted more quickly.

### 3. Pressure
Sound waves are pressure waves. Therefore, the speed of sound is influenced by the ambient pressure of the medium. For example, at sea level, where air pressure is higher, sound travels faster than it does at high altitudes (such as on a mountain) where the air pressure is lower.

---

## Key Fact
The speed of sound in air, at sea level, at a temperature of $21^{\circ}\text{C}$ and under normal atmospheric conditions, is $344 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.

## Measuring the Speed of Sound

### Concept
The speed of sound can be measured by utilizing the significant difference between the speed of light and the speed of sound. 
*   **Speed of light:** $\approx 300\,000\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$
*   **Speed of sound:** $\approx 300\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$

Because light travels almost instantaneously, the time delay between seeing an event (like a flash) and hearing the sound allows us to calculate the speed of sound using the distance and time elapsed.

### Experimental Method
**Apparatus:** Stopwatch

**Procedure:**
1. Find a location with a known, precise, straight-line distance between two points (e.g., an athletics track).
2. One person stands at the first point to produce a loud sound.
3. A second person stands at the second point with a stopwatch.
4. The person with the stopwatch starts the timer when they see the sound being produced and stops it when they hear the sound. Repeat this process several times to ensure accuracy.

### Data Collection and Calculation
To calculate the speed of sound ($v$), use the following formula:
$$v = \frac{D}{t}$$

Where:
*   $v$ = speed in $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$
*   $D$ = distance in $\text{m}$
*   $t$ = time in $\text{s}$

## Reflection and Echoes

### Definition
When sound waves collide with an object, they are reflected. This occurs because the individual particles oscillating about their equilibrium position collide with the surface of the object and bounce back, causing the wave to change direction.

### Echoes
*   **In small spaces:** Reflections occur at every surface, but they are too numerous and mixed up for the human ear to distinguish individual sounds.
*   **In large, open spaces:** In an empty school hall or near a large mountain cliff, the reflected sound wave travels back to the listener. If the delay is sufficient, the listener hears the sound again a split second later. This phenomenon is known as an **echo**.

---

## SONAR

### Definition
**SONAR** is an acronym for **So**und **N**avigation **A**nd **R**anging. It is a technology used primarily by ships to determine the depth of the ocean.

### How it Works
1.  A sound wave is transmitted from the ship toward the seabed.
2.  The wave hits the seabed and is reflected back to the ship.
3.  Because the speed of sound in water is known and the time lapse between sending and receiving the signal is measured, the distance to the bottom can be calculated.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Mathematical Principle
To calculate the depth ($d$) of the ocean, we use the relationship between speed ($v$), time ($t$), and distance. Since the sound must travel to the seabed and back to the ship, the total distance traveled is $2d$.

The formula is:
$$v = \frac{2d}{t}$$

Rearranging to solve for depth ($d$):
$$d = \frac{v \times t}{2}$$

Where:
*   $d$ = depth of the ocean (m)
*   $v$ = speed of sound in water ($\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$)
*   $t$ = total time taken for the echo to return (s)

## Example 1: SONAR

### Question
A ship sends a signal to the bottom of the ocean to determine the depth of the ocean. The speed of sound in sea water is $1450\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$. If the signal is received $1,5\text{ seconds}$ later, how deep is the ocean at that point?

### Solution

**Step 1: Identify what is given and what is being asked:**

*   $s = 1450\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$
*   $t = 1,5\text{ s}$ (total time for the signal to travel there and back)
*   $\therefore t = 0,75\text{ s}$ (time for one way)
*   $d = ?$

**Step 2: Calculate the distance:**

$$\text{Distance} = \text{speed} \times \text{time}$$
$$D = s \times t$$
$$D = 1450\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1} \times 0,75\text{ s}$$
$$D = 1087,5\text{ m}$$

## Loudness

The **loudness** or volume of a sound is determined by the **amplitude** of the sound wave.

*   **Larger amplitude:** Results in a louder sound.
*   **Smaller amplitude:** Results in a softer sound.

### Key Principles:
1.  **Energy Transfer:** The vibration of a source sets the amplitude of a wave. A more energetic vibration transmits more energy into the medium, resulting in a larger amplitude where molecules move back and forth more vigorously.
2.  **Ear Sensitivity:** Loudness is also dependent on the sensitivity of the ear. The human ear is more sensitive to certain frequencies than others. Therefore, the perceived volume depends on both the physical amplitude of the wave and whether the frequency falls within a range where the ear is highly sensitive.

# Chapter 10: Sound - The Oscilloscope

## 1. The Oscilloscope
An oscilloscope is an electronic instrument used to visualize electrical signals. In the context of sound, a microphone picks up sound waves and converts them into electrical signals, which are then displayed on the oscilloscope screen.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Key Controls
To effectively display the characteristics of a sound wave, the following controls are used:

*   **Amplitude Adjustment:** Controls the vertical scale of the display. It determines how "tall" a given voltage appears on the screen. This allows the user to view both very large and very small signals on the same screen.
*   **Frequency (Time) Adjustment:** Controls the horizontal scale of the display. It determines how much time is represented by a certain distance across the screen. This allows the user to view signals that change very quickly or very slowly.
*   **Triggering and Channels:** Used to stabilize the signal and select the input source for the display.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

## 2. Important Note on Wave Representation
> **Note:** The display of the oscilloscope shows a **transverse wave pattern**. It is important to understand that this does not mean sound waves themselves are transverse. Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave; the oscilloscope simply displays the fluctuating pressure as a transverse wave pattern for easier analysis.

## 3. Practical Application
Learners can experiment with different amplitudes and frequencies using a function generator. By observing the waveform picked up by the microphone, you can see the direct impact that changes in sound properties have on the visual representation of the wave.

## Applications of Ultrasound

Ultrasound is widely used in industrial and medical fields. Its ability to create images relies on the **reflection and transmission of waves** at boundaries between different substances.

### Medical Imaging
When an ultrasound wave travels through the human body, it encounters boundaries between different materials (e.g., bone and muscle, or muscle and fat). At these boundaries, part of the wave is reflected and part is transmitted. These reflected rays are detected and processed to construct an image.
*   **Usage:** Visualising muscle, soft tissue, and organs.
*   **Benefits:** It is a safe, non-invasive method for looking inside the human body, commonly used during pregnancy.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Therapeutic and Industrial Uses
*   **Biological Tissue:** Ultrasound sources can generate local heating, which is applied in physical therapy and cancer treatment.
*   **Medical Procedures:** Focused ultrasound sources are used to break up kidney stones.
*   **Ultrasonic Cleaners:** These operate at frequencies between $20\text{ kHz}$ and $40\text{ kHz}$. They are used to clean jewellery, lenses, watches, dental instruments, and industrial parts.
    *   **Mechanism:** The cleaning action is caused by the energy released from the collapse of millions of microscopic bubbles in the cleaning fluid.

> **FACT:** Ultrasound generator/speaker systems marketed to frighten away rodents and insects lack scientific evidence. Controlled tests show that rodents quickly learn that these speakers are harmless.

## The Physics of Hearing

The human ear is divided into three main sections: the outer, middle, and inner ear.

### The Journey of a Sound Wave
1.  **Pinna:** The outermost part of the ear collects and focuses sound waves.
2.  **Ear Canal:** The sound wave travels through the canal until it reaches the eardrum.
3.  **Eardrum:** Pressure fluctuations from the sound wave cause the eardrum to vibrate.
4.  **Middle Ear:** Three small bones—the **malleus** (hammer), the **incus** (anvil), and the **stapes** (stirrup)—transmit these vibrations.
5.  **Auditory Nerve:** The signal is transmitted to the brain.

## The Inner Ear and Sound Transmission
The process of hearing involves the transmission of sound signals through the ear structures to the **elliptical window**, which marks the beginning of the inner ear.

*   **Transmission:** Sound waves are transmitted through the fluid contained within the inner ear.
*   **Interpretation:** These signals are interpreted as sounds by the brain.
*   **Structure of the Inner Ear:**
    *   **Semicircular canals:** Responsible for detecting quick movements and maintaining body balance.
    *   **Cochlea:** A fluid-filled structure involved in hearing.
    *   **Auditory nerve:** Transmits electrical impulses from the cochlea to the brain.

## Hearing Safety and Damage Prevention
Sound intensity is measured in decibels (dB). Exposure to high-intensity sound can lead to permanent hearing loss.

### Key Safety Thresholds
*   **Threshold of Pain:** Certain sounds exceed the threshold of pain and can cause immediate damage to the ear.
*   **Long-term Damage:** Exposure to sounds at or above $80\text{ dB}$ can cause cumulative damage to hearing over time.

### Protective Measures
To protect your hearing, consider the following strategies:
1.  **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Wear earplugs or ear muffs in loud environments.
2.  **Limit Exposure:** Reduce the amount of time spent in high-decibel environments.
3.  **Increase Distance:** Increase the physical distance between yourself and the source of the sound.

### 3. Conceptual Analysis

**Question 5**
X and Y are different wave motions. In air, X travels much faster than Y but has a much shorter wavelength. Which types of wave motion could X and Y be?

*   **Concept:** The relationship between wave speed ($v$), frequency ($f$), and wavelength ($\lambda$) is given by the formula:
    $$v = f \cdot \lambda$$
*   **Analysis:** Since $X$ travels faster than $Y$ ($v_X > v_Y$) but has a shorter wavelength ($\lambda_X < \lambda_Y$), the frequency of $X$ must be significantly higher than the frequency of $Y$ to satisfy the wave equation.

The frequency ($f$) of a wave is the reciprocal of the period ($T$). The period is the time taken for one complete cycle.

*   $v$ is measured in meters per second ($\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$).
*   $f$ is measured in Hertz (Hz).
*   $\lambda$ is measured in meters (m).

### Physics Revision: Properties of Sound Waves

#### 1. Speed of Sound and Mach Numbers
The speed of an object relative to the speed of sound is often described using the Mach number.
*   **Subsonic:** Speed is less than the speed of sound ($Mach < 1$).
*   **Sonic:** Speed is equal to the speed of sound ($Mach = 1$).
*   **Supersonic:** Speed is greater than the speed of sound ($Mach > 1$).
*   **Hypersonic:** Speed is significantly greater than the speed of sound (typically $Mach > 5$).

#### 2. Comparison: Sound Waves vs. Light Waves
| Feature | Sound Waves | Light Waves |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nature** | Mechanical wave | Electromagnetic wave |
| **Medium** | Requires a medium (solid, liquid, gas) | Can travel through a vacuum |
| **Origin** | Produced by vibrating objects | Produced by oscillating electric/magnetic fields |

#### 3. Factors Affecting the Speed of Sound
The speed of sound depends on the medium through which it travels. Generally, sound travels fastest in solids, followed by liquids, and slowest in gases. This is due to the density and elastic properties of the particles in the medium.
*   **Order of speed:** $v_{solid} > v_{liquid} > v_{gas}$
*   **Example:** At the same temperature, sound travels faster in **rock** (solid) than in **milk** (liquid), **oxygen** (gas), or **sand** (granular solid/air mixture).

#### 4. Wave Velocity and Wavelength
The velocity of a wave ($v$) is determined by the product of its frequency ($f$) and its wavelength ($\lambda$):
$$v = f \cdot \lambda$$

**Key Concept:** When a wave travels through a specific medium (like nitrogen gas) at a constant temperature, its **velocity remains constant**, regardless of changes in wavelength or frequency. If the wavelength changes, the frequency adjusts proportionally to keep the velocity constant.

$$\lambda = \frac{344}{10}$$
$$\lambda = 34,4\text{ m}$$

**Example 23: Ocean Depth Calculation**
*Problem:* A ship sends a signal to determine ocean depth. The signal returns $2,5\text{ seconds}$ later. If sound travels at $1450\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ in sea water, how deep is the ocean?
*Solution:*
The sound travels to the bottom and back, so we use half the time:
$$D = v \times t$$
$$D = 1450 \times \left(\frac{2,5}{2}\right)$$
$$D = 1812,5\text{ m}$$

**Example 24: Echo from a Cliff**
*Problem:* A person shouts at a cliff and hears an echo $1\text{ second}$ later. If the speed of sound is $344\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$, how far away is the cliff?
*Solution:*
First, calculate the total distance the sound traveled:
$$D_{\text{total}} = v \times t$$
$$D_{\text{total}} = 344 \times 1 = 344\text{ m}$$
Since the sound traveled to the cliff and back, the distance to the cliff is half of the total distance:
$$D_{\text{cliff}} = \frac{D_{\text{total}}}{2}$$
$$D_{\text{cliff}} = \frac{344}{2}$$
$$D_{\text{cliff}} = 172\text{ m}$$

### Revision Notes: Wave Properties and Definitions

Understanding the fundamental characteristics of waves is essential in physics. Below is a summary of key wave terminology and their definitions.

#### Core Concepts

*   **Wave Speed ($v$):** The distance a wave travels per unit of time. It is calculated as:
    $$v = f \cdot \lambda$$
    *(where $f$ is frequency and $\lambda$ is wavelength)*
*   **Frequency ($f$):** A measure of how often a single wavelength passes a specific point. It is measured in Hertz (Hz).
*   **Period ($T$):** The time taken for one complete wavelength to pass a specific point. It is the reciprocal of frequency:
    $$T = \frac{1}{f}$$
*   **Wavelength ($\lambda$):** The distance between two consecutive points on a wave that are in phase (e.g., crest to crest).
*   **Amplitude ($A$):** The maximum displacement from the equilibrium position. It is defined as half the difference between the high point (crest) and the low point (trough) of a wave.
*   **Longitudinal Waves:** Waves where the medium moves in the same direction as the wave propagation (e.g., sound waves).
*   **Transverse Waves:** Waves where the medium moves in a direction perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation.
