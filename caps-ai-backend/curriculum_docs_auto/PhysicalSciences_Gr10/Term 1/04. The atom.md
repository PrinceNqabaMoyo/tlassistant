## Models of the Atom

### Historical Context
The scientific understanding of the atom has evolved over a long period, with scientists building upon the ideas of their predecessors.

*   **Origin:** The concept of the atom was first proposed by Greek philosophers **Democritus** and **Leucippus** in the fifth century BC.
*   **Etymology:** The Greek word $\alpha\tau o\mu o\nu$ (atom) means **indivisible**, as they believed atoms could not be broken into smaller pieces.
*   **Modern Understanding:** Atoms consist of a **positively charged nucleus** at the centre, surrounded by **negatively charged electrons**.

---

### Definition: Model
> A **model** is a representation of a system in the real world. Models help us to understand systems and their properties.

### Understanding Atomic Models
*   An **atomic model** represents what the structure of an atom *could* look like based on observed behaviours.
*   It is important to note that a model is not necessarily an exact or "true" picture of the atom.
*   **Limitations:** Models are often simplified versions of reality. Just as a toy car represents a real vehicle but is smaller and simpler, an atomic model provides a useful approximation while acknowledging that it may not be 100% accurate. It is essential to recognize these limitations to avoid developing misconceptions.

## 1. Dalton's Model of the Atom
John Dalton proposed that all matter is composed of very small particles called **atoms**. 

*   **Historical Context:** This concept was inspired by the ancient Greeks (notably Democritus), who proposed that matter is composed of small, indivisible objects.
*   **Key Characteristic:** In Dalton's model, atoms were considered indivisible (they could not be divided).
*   **Limitations:** When Dalton proposed this model, electrons and the atomic nucleus were unknown.

## 2. Thomson's Model of the Atom
Following the discovery of the electron by J.J. Thomson in 1897, it was realized that atoms were made of even smaller particles.

### The "Plum Pudding" Model (1904)
Because the atomic nucleus had not yet been discovered, Thomson proposed the "plum pudding model":
*   **Structure:** The atom consists of negative electrons floating in a "soup" of positive charge.
*   **Analogy:** Similar to plums or raisins in a fruit cake.

| :--- | :--- |
| **Cubic Model** | Electrons were imagined to lie at the corners of a cube. |
| **Saturnian Model** | Electrons were imagined to orbit a very big, heavy nucleus. |

## 3. The Discovery of Radiation
The next advancement in understanding atomic structure came from the study of **radiation**:
*   **Discovery:** Marie and Pierre Curie discovered that some elements (radioactive elements) emit particles capable of passing through matter, similar to X-rays.
*   **Impact:** This discovery allowed Ernest Rutherford, in 1911, to revise the model of the atom, leading to further developments in atomic theory.

## Rutherford's Model of the Atom
Rutherford’s experiments led to a significant shift in the understanding of atomic structure. His model proposed that the atom consists of a tiny, dense, positively charged core known as the **nucleus**, which is surrounded by lighter, negatively charged **electrons**.

*   **The Planetary Model:** This model is often compared to a mini solar system, where electrons orbit the nucleus in a manner similar to planets orbiting the sun.

*Figure 4.3: Rutherford's model of the atom*

## Bohr's Model of the Atom
While Rutherford's model was revolutionary, it had limitations. Specifically, it could not explain why atoms only emit light at certain specific wavelengths or frequencies.

*   **The Bohr Solution:** Niels Bohr addressed this by proposing that electrons do not orbit randomly. Instead, they are restricted to **certain special orbits** at specific **energy levels** around the nucleus.

*Figure 4.4: Bohr's model of the atom*

## James Chadwick and the Neutron
In 1920, Rutherford predicted the existence of an additional particle within the nucleus alongside the proton. 

*   **The Reasoning:** If the nucleus contained only positively charged protons, the repulsive forces between these like-charged particles would cause the nucleus to break apart. To maintain an electrically neutral and stable nucleus, a neutral particle was required.
*   **The Discovery:** In 1932, James Chadwick experimentally confirmed this prediction by discovering the **neutron**.

# How heavy is an atom?

## The Problem with Kilograms
Determining the mass of a single atom in kilograms results in extremely small numbers that are difficult to work with.
*   **Mass of a carbon atom:** $\approx 1,99 \times 10^{-26} \text{ kg}$
*   **Mass of a hydrogen atom:** $\approx 1,67 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$

## The Atomic Mass Unit (amu)
To simplify calculations, scientists use the **atomic mass unit (amu)**, abbreviated as **u**.

### The Carbon Standard
Scientists use carbon as the standard to determine atomic mass:
*   Carbon is assigned an atomic mass of exactly $12,0 \text{ u}$.
*   Atomic mass is a **relative mass**; it describes the mass of an atom compared to the mass of a carbon atom, rather than its actual mass in kilograms.

# Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

## Overview
Radioactive elements emit various particles, including positively charged alpha ($\alpha$) particles. Ernest Rutherford conducted a series of experiments to determine the location of positive charge within an atom by bombarding thin gold foil with these $\alpha$ particles.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Figure 4.5:** Rutherford's gold foil experiment. Figure (a) shows the path of the $\alpha$ particles after hitting the gold sheet. Figure (b) shows the arrangement of atoms in the gold sheets and the path of the $\alpha$ particles in relation to them.

## Experimental Setup
*   **Source:** A beam of $\alpha$ particles was directed at thin sheets of gold.
*   **Detection:** A screen made of zinc sulphide was placed behind the gold sheets to detect where the $\alpha$ particles landed.
*   **Scientific Reasoning:**
    *   Rutherford knew that electrons were too small to significantly affect the path of the massive $\alpha$ particles.
    *   He reasoned that the positively charged **protons** would be responsible for repelling the positively charged $\alpha$ particles.

## Observations and Results
Rutherford compared his findings against the existing Thomson model of the atom:

| Path | Description | Observation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Path A** | Particles pass through the foil undisturbed. | Most particles followed this path. |
| **Path B** | Particles are slightly deflected. | Some particles followed this path. |
| **Path C** | Particles are reflected by the positive nucleus. | Very few particles followed this path. |

## Conclusion
*   If Thomson's model had been correct, most particles should have followed Path C.
*   Because most particles passed straight through (Path A), Rutherford concluded that the atom is mostly empty space.
*   The small number of deflections and reflections (Paths B and C) suggested that the positive charge is concentrated in a very small, dense region at the center of the atom, which he identified as the **nucleus**.

## Introduction
Atoms consist of a positively charged **nucleus** surrounded by one or more negatively charged **electrons** that orbit the nucleus. To understand atomic structure, we must define the properties of electrons, protons, and neutrons.

---

## The Electron
The electron is a very tiny particle.

*   **Mass:** $9,11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}$
*   **Electric Charge:** One unit of negative electric charge ($-1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$)

> **FACT:** Scientists believe that the electron can be treated as a **point particle** or **elementary particle**, meaning that it cannot be broken down into anything smaller.

---

## The Nucleus
Unlike the electron, the nucleus can be broken down into smaller building blocks called **protons** and **neutrons**. Together, these are referred to as **nucleons**.

### The Proton
*   **Electric Charge:** One unit of positive electric charge ($+1,6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$).
*   **Mass:** $1,6726 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$ (approximately $10\,000$ times heavier than an electron).
*   **Atomic Neutrality:** Atoms are electrically neutral. Therefore, the number of protons in the nucleus must equal the number of electrons orbiting the nucleus to balance the total charge to zero.

### The Neutron
*   **Electric Charge:** Electrically neutral (carries no charge).
*   **Mass:** $1,6749 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$ (slightly heavier than a proton).

---

## Summary of Atomic Mass
When discussing the atomic mass of an atom, we are primarily referring to the combined mass of the protons and neutrons (the nucleons).

### Atomic Number and Atomic Mass Number

The chemical properties of an element are determined by the charge of its nucleus, which is defined by the number of protons.

#### 1. Atomic Number ($Z$)
**Definition:** The number of protons in an atom.

*   The atomic number is an integer ranging from $1$ to approximately $118$.
*   It can be found on the periodic table.

#### 2. Atomic Mass Number ($A$)
**Definition:** The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.

*   The mass of an atom depends on the total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons).

---

### Standard Notation
The atomic number ($Z$) and the mass number ($A$) are indicated using standard notation. For example, carbon is represented as:
$$_6^{12}\text{C}$$

---

### Fact: The Island of Stability
*   Currently, element $118$ is the highest atomic number for an element.
*   Elements with high atomic numbers (from about $93$ to $118$) are unstable and break apart within seconds of being formed.
*   Scientists hypothesize an "island of stability" where elements with even higher atomic numbers may exist without breaking apart immediately.

## Standard Notation of Nuclides
To represent an atom, we use the following standard notation:

$${}_{Z}^{A}X$$

Where:
*   $X$: The chemical symbol of the element.
*   $A$: The mass number (number of nucleons: protons + neutrons).
*   $Z$: The atomic number (number of protons).

### Calculating Neutrons
The number of neutrons ($N$) in a nucleus is calculated by finding the difference between the mass number and the atomic number:
$$N = A - Z$$

**Example:** Iron ($Fe$) with 26 protons and 30 neutrons is written as:
$${}_{26}^{56}Fe$$
Here, $Z = 26$ and $A = 56$. Therefore, $N = 56 - 26 = 30$.

## Ions
A neutral atom has an equal number of protons and electrons. When an atom gains or loses electrons, it becomes an **ion** (a charged atom).

*   **Gaining electrons:** The atom becomes more **negative** (e.g., $Cl^-$ or $F^-$).
*   **Losing electrons:** The atom becomes more **positive** (e.g., $Na^+$ or $Li^+$).

*Note: Changing the number of electrons does not change the type of element.*

### Worked Example: Determining Atomic Composition (Sodium)

To determine the composition of a neutral atom, follow these steps:

*   **Step 3: Find the number of electrons**

## 4.5 Isotopes

### Concept: What are Isotopes?
The chemical properties of an element are determined by the number of protons and electrons within the atom. Because neutrons do not affect these properties, adding or removing neutrons from the nucleus does not change the element's identity or its position in the periodic table.

*   **Etymology:** The word "isotope" comes from the Greek words *isos topos*, meaning "same place."
*   **Definition:** Isotopes are atoms that have the same number of protons (same atomic number, $Z$), but a different number of neutrons (different $N$), resulting in a different mass number ($A$).

### Stability and Notation
While the chemical properties of isotopes are identical, their nuclear stability may differ. Isotopes are typically written in the format **E - A**, where:
*   **E** = Element symbol
*   **A** = Atomic mass

**Example:**
*   **Chlorine-35:** 17 protons and 18 neutrons (Atomic mass = 35 u)
*   **Chlorine-37:** 17 protons and 20 neutrons (Atomic mass = 37 u)

---

### Worked Example 2: The relative atomic mass of an isotopic element

**Question:**
The element chlorine has two isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. The abundance of these isotopes when they occur naturally is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Calculate the average atomic mass.

**Solution:**

To calculate the average atomic mass, we use the weighted average based on their natural abundance:

$$\text{Average atomic mass} = (\text{mass}_1 \times \text{abundance}_1) + (\text{mass}_2 \times \text{abundance}_2)$$

| Isotope | Mass (u) | Abundance (%) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Chlorine-35 | 35 | 75% (0,75) |
| Chlorine-37 | 37 | 25% (0,25) |

**Calculation:**

$$\text{Average atomic mass} = (35 \times 0,75) + (37 \times 0,25)$$
$$\text{Average atomic mass} = 26,25 + 9,25$$
$$\text{Average atomic mass} = 35,5 \text{ u}$$

## Worked Example: Calculating Average Relative Atomic Mass

To calculate the average relative atomic mass of an element, you must account for the abundance of its naturally occurring isotopes.

**Problem:** Calculate the average relative atomic mass for chlorine.

**Solution:**

*   **Step 1:** Calculate the mass contribution of chlorine-35.
    Given that $75\%$ of chlorine atoms have a mass of $35\text{ u}$:
    $$\text{Contribution of Cl-35} = \left( \frac{75}{100} \times 35 \right) = 26,25\text{ u}$$

*   **Step 2:** Calculate the mass contribution of chlorine-37.
    Given that $25\%$ of chlorine atoms have a mass of $37\text{ u}$:
    $$\text{Contribution of Cl-37} = \left( \frac{25}{100} \times 37 \right) = 9,25\text{ u}$$

*   **Step 3:** Add the two values to arrive at the average relative atomic mass of chlorine.
    $$\text{Relative atomic mass of chlorine} = 26,25\text{ u} + 9,25\text{ u} = 35,5\text{ u}$$

## Electronic Configuration

### The Energy of Electrons
Electrons within an atom possess the same charge and mass, but they differ in their energy levels. 
*   **Lowest Energy:** Electrons found closest to the nucleus, where the attractive force of the positively charged nucleus is strongest.
*   **Higher Energy:** Electrons found further away from the nucleus, as they have enough energy to overcome the attractive force of the nucleus.

---

### Electron Arrangement
Electrons are arranged in specific energy levels (or shells) around the nucleus of an atom. These levels are numbered $1, 2, 3$, etc.

**Rules for Electron Capacity:**
*   **Energy Level 1:** Can hold a maximum of $2$ electrons.
*   **Energy Level 2:** Can hold a maximum of $8$ electrons.
*   **Energy Level 3:** Can hold a maximum of $8$ electrons.

*   **Neutral Atom:** Contains $3$ electrons.

### Atomic Orbitals
Within each energy level, electrons move in **orbitals**. An orbital defines the spaces or regions where electrons move.

> **DEFINITION: Atomic orbital**
> An atomic orbital is the region in which an electron may be found around a single atom.

#### Energy Level Structure
*   **First energy level:** Contains one $s$ orbital.
*   **Second energy level:** Contains one $s$ orbital and three $p$ orbitals.
*   **Third energy level:** Contains one $s$ orbital, three $p$ orbitals, and five $d$ orbitals.

Within each energy level, the $s$ orbital is at a lower energy than the $p$ orbitals.

> **NOTE**
> Each block in Figure 4.9 is able to hold two electrons. This means that the $s$ orbital can hold two electrons, while the $p$ orbital can hold a total of six electrons, two in each of the three blocks.

### Electron Configuration
The electron configuration of an element is the arrangement of the electrons in the shells and subshells. Guidelines for determining electron configuration include:

*   **Orbital Capacity:** Each orbital can only hold two electrons. Electrons that occur together in an orbital are called an **electron pair**.
*   **Energy Preference:** An electron will always try to enter an orbital with the lowest possible energy.

### Key Principles of Electron Filling
*   **Energy Preference:** Electrons occupy lower energy orbitals before occupying higher energy orbitals.

### Definition: Electron Configuration
**Electron configuration** is the arrangement of electrons in an atom, molecule, or other physical structure.

## Aufbau Diagrams

An element's electron configuration can be represented using **Aufbau diagrams** (also known as energy level diagrams). An Aufbau diagram uses arrows to represent electrons.

---

### The Aufbau Principle

The term "Aufbau" is German for "building up." In chemistry, this principle describes the process of determining the electron configuration of an atom by filling orbitals in order of increasing energy.

#### Steps for Electron Configuration
1. Determine the total number of electrons in the atom.
2. Fill the $s$ orbital in the first energy level ($1s$ orbital) with the first two electrons.
3. Fill the $s$ orbital in the second energy level ($2s$ orbital) with the next two electrons.
4. Place one electron in each of the three $p$ orbitals in the second energy level ($2p$ orbitals). If electrons remain, return to the $2p$ orbitals to pair them up.
5. Continue this process through successive energy levels until all electrons are placed.

*Analogy:* Filling orbitals is similar to passengers boarding a bus. People prefer to sit in empty seats alone before being forced to share a seat with someone else. Sharing a seat represents a higher energy state.

---

### Hund’s Rule and Pauli’s Exclusion Principle

#### Hund’s Rule
Electrons prefer to occupy an empty subshell rather than sharing one. When filling subshells, place one electron in each subshell individually before going back to pair them up.

#### Pauli’s Exclusion Principle
Electrons possess a property known as "spin." Two electrons in the same subshell cannot have the same spin. Consequently, when representing electrons in orbitals, they are drawn as one arrow pointing up ($\uparrow$) and one arrow pointing down ($\downarrow$).

Spectroscopic notation provides a concise way to describe the energy levels, orbitals, and the number of electrons within an atom.

*   **Format:** The number represents the energy level, the letter represents the orbital type, and the superscript represents the number of electrons in that orbital.
*   **Example (Lithium):** $1s^2 2s^1$

### 4.6 Chemical Reactivity and Stability

#### Key Concepts
*   **Interaction of Atoms:** When atoms interact, it is primarily their **valence electrons** (electrons in the outermost shell) that determine how they react.
*   **Stability:** An atom is at its most stable (unreactive) when its electron orbitals are full. Conversely, an atom is least stable (reactive) when its valence electron orbitals are not full.
*   **Chemical Behaviour:** Valence electrons are responsible for an element's chemical behaviour. Elements with the same number of valence electrons often exhibit similar chemical properties.
*   **The Octet Rule:** Elements tend to react to achieve the most stable electron configuration, which is that of a noble gas. A full energy level typically contains 8 electrons. This set of 8 is known as an **octet**.

### Calculating Relative Atomic Mass and Understanding Isotopes

#### 1. Calculating Relative Atomic Mass
To calculate the relative atomic mass of an element from a sample containing different isotopes, you multiply the mass of each isotope by its percentage abundance (expressed as a fraction) and sum the results.

**Worked Example:**
If a sample contains $79\%$ Mg-24, $10\%$ Mg-25, and $11\%$ Mg-26, calculate the relative atomic mass.

*   **Contribution from Mg-24:** $\frac{79}{100} \times 24 = 18,96 \text{ u}$
*   **Contribution from Mg-25:** $\frac{10}{100} \times 25 = 2,5 \text{ u}$
*   **Contribution from Mg-26:** $\frac{11}{100} \times 26 = 2,86 \text{ u}$

**Total Relative Atomic Mass:**
$18,96 \text{ u} + 2,5 \text{ u} + 2,86 \text{ u} = 24,3 \text{ u}$

### Revision Notes: Atomic Structure and Properties

#### 1. Fundamental Components of an Atom
An atom is the basic unit of matter. It consists of three primary subatomic particles:
*   **Protons:** Positively charged particles located in the nucleus.
*   **Neutrons:** Electrically neutral particles located in the nucleus.
*   **Electrons:** Negatively charged particles that orbit the nucleus.

**Concept Check:**
*   **Question:** What are the three basic components of an atom?
*   **Answer:** Protons, neutrons, and electrons.

#### 2. Electrical Charge of an Atom
In its standard state, an atom is electrically **neutral**. This is because the number of positively charged protons in the nucleus is exactly balanced by the number of negatively charged electrons orbiting the nucleus.

#### 3. Rutherford’s Scattering Experiment
Ernest Rutherford’s gold foil experiment involved firing alpha particles (which are positively charged) at a thin sheet of gold. 
*   The alpha particles were deflected by the positive charge of the atomic nuclei.
*   If **neutrons** were used instead of alpha particles, they would not be deflected by the nucleus because neutrons carry **no electrical charge**. Since they are neutral, they do not experience electrostatic repulsion or attraction from the nucleus.

#### 4. Understanding Isotopes and Notation
Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. 

The notation for an isotope is written as:
$$_Z^A X$$

Where:
*   $A$ = Mass number (protons + neutrons)
*   $Z$ = Atomic number (number of protons)
*   $X$ = Chemical symbol

**Worked Example: Analyzing $^{234}_{92}U$**
*   **Atomic Number ($Z$):** $92$ (This identifies the element as Uranium).
*   **Mass Number ($A$):** $234$.
*   **Number of Neutrons:** Calculated as $A - Z = 234 - 92 = 142$.

**Comparison Table: Isotope Properties**

| Property | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Atomic Number** | Defines the element; determines the number of protons and electrons. |
| **Mass Number** | The sum of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. |
| **Isotope Relationship** | Same atomic number ($Z$), different mass number ($A$). |
| **Electron Configuration** | Determined by the number of protons (atomic number). |

*Note: Atoms with the same atomic number ($Z$) have the same electron configuration, regardless of the number of neutrons.*
