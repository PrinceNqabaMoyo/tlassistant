# 6 Scale, Maps and Plans

## 6.1 Introduction and Key Concepts

Maps and plans are visual representations of the real world around us—for example, a school, a town, a movie theatre, or a shopping centre. They are tools that help us find our way around a new environment or locate a specific place, such as a shop in a mall or a seat in a sports stadium.

### Learning Objectives
In this chapter, you will learn how to:
* Use the **number scale** and the **bar scale**, and understand the advantages and disadvantages of both, including what happens when maps are resized.
* Estimate **actual distance** or length when given a scale map.
* Calculate **scaled measurements** when given the actual distance or length.
* Read maps and seating plans to describe the position of an object in relation to its surroundings.
* Find locations and follow or develop directions for travelling between two or more locations.

---

> **DEFINITION: Scale**
> 
> The scale of a map is a ratio of the distance on the map to the actual distance on the ground or in real life. 
> 
> For example, a number scale of $1 : 100$ means that $1$ unit on the map represents $100$ units on the ground or in reality.
> 
> $$1 \text{ cm on the map} = 100 \text{ cm on the ground}$$
> $$1 \text{ cm on the map} = 1 \text{ m on the ground}$$

---

## 6.2 Number and Bar Scales

### Introduction to Number and Bar Scales

The two primary types of scales used in geography and technical drawings are the **number scale** and the **bar scale**.

#### 1. Number Scale
The number scale is expressed as a ratio, such as $1 : 50$. This indicates that $1$ unit of measurement on the map represents $50$ of the same units on the ground.

*   $1 \text{ cm}$ on the map represents $50 \text{ cm}$ on the ground.
*   $1 \text{ m}$ on the map represents $50 \text{ m}$ on the ground.

**Procedure for using a number scale:**
1. Measure the distance between two points on the map using a ruler.
2. Multiply that measurement by the "real" part of the scale ratio (the second number).
3. Convert the final answer to a suitable unit (e.g., from centimeters to meters or kilometers).

$$\text{Actual Distance} = \text{Map Distance} \times \text{Scale Factor}$$

#### 2. Bar Scale
The bar scale is a visual representation of distance. It looks like a small ruler printed on the map.

**Example of a bar scale:**

| $0\text{ m}$ | $10\text{ m}$ | $20\text{ m}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| [Black Segment] | [White Segment] | |

To use a bar scale, you measure the length of the bar with a ruler to determine how many centimeters on the map represent the distance indicated (e.g., if the bar from $0$ to $10\text{ m}$ measures $2\text{ cm}$, then $2\text{ cm} = 10\text{ m}$ in reality).

---

Each piece or segment of the bar represents a given distance, as labelled underneath. To use the bar scale, you need to measure how long one segment of the bar is on your ruler. You must then measure the distance on the map in centimetres; calculate how many segments of the bar graph it works out to be (the total distance measured; divided by the length of one segment); and then multiply it by the scale underneath.

So, if $1\text{ cm}$ on the bar represents $10\text{ m}$ on ground, and the distance you measure on the map is $3\text{ cm}$ ($3\text{ cm} \div 1\text{ cm} = 3$ segments) then the real distance on the ground is $3 \times 10\text{ m} = 30\text{ m}$.

# 6.2. Number and bar scales

### Worked Examples: Calculating Real Distance

To calculate the real-life distance from a map, follow this rule:
**Multiply your scaled measurement by the "real" number in the scale ratio.**

**Example 1 (continued)**
a) Calculating the width:
$$5\text{ cm} \times 500 = 2500\text{ cm}$$
$$2500\text{ cm} \div 100 = 25\text{ m}$$
The field is $25\text{ m}$ wide.

b) On the map, the field is $10\text{ cm}$ long:
$$10\text{ cm} \times 500 = 5000\text{ cm}$$
$$5000\text{ cm} \div 100 = 50\text{ m}$$
The field is $50\text{ m}$ long.

**Example 2**
On the map, the science classroom building is $5\text{ cm}$ long:
$$5\text{ cm} \times 500 = 2500\text{ cm}$$
$$2500\text{ cm} \div 100 = 25\text{ m}$$
The science classrooms are $25\text{ m}$ long.

**Example 3**
The blue dotted line is $6\text{ cm}$ long on the map:
$$6\text{ cm} \times 500 = 3000\text{ cm}$$
$$3000\text{ cm} \div 100 = 30\text{ m}$$
Zuki walked $30\text{ m}$ from the tuckshop to his maths classroom.

***

> **NOTE:**
> Although we say we are measuring distance on a map and calculating real distance, in reality we are approximating or estimating distance because the measurements you get using a scale and conversion are accurate only to the nearest metre or centimetre. For example, using the above measurement of how far Zuki walked from the tuckshop to his maths classroom, by our calculations he walked $30\text{ m}$, whereas if we were to measure this distance exactly on the ground, using a measuring tape, he may actually have walked $30\text{ m}$ and $10\text{ cm}$.

### Key Concepts: Resizing and Scale Accuracy

#### 1. Resizing with a Number Scale
If we resize a map that has a number scale on it, the number scale becomes **incorrect**. 

**Example:**
If a map is $10\text{ cm}$ wide when printed, and the number scale is $1 : 10$, then $1\text{ cm}$ on the map represents $10\text{ cm}$ on the ground. However, if we reprint the map larger so it is now $15\text{ cm}$ wide, our scale will still be $1 : 10$ according to the printed text, but now:
$$1,5\text{ cm} \text{ represents } 10\text{ cm} \text{ on the ground}$$
$$(1,5 \times 10 = 15\text{ cm} = \text{width of map})$$
Consequently, any scale calculations will now be wrong. When resizing maps that use the number scale, it is important to know that the scale changes with the map. This is a significant **disadvantage** to using the number scale.

#### 2. Resizing with a Bar Scale
If we resize a map that has a bar scale on it, the size of the bar scale will be resized along with the map. Therefore, it will remain **accurate**. This is a major **advantage** to using the bar scale.

---

### Comparison of Scale Types

| Scale Type | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Number Scale** ($1 : 50$) | Only one distance needs to be measured. Calculations are usually simple. | Becomes inaccurate if the map is resized (enlarged or reduced). |
| **Bar Scale** | Remains accurate even if the map is resized, as the bar grows or shrinks with the image. | Requires measuring both the segment length and the map distance. Calculations can be more complicated. |

**Note:** To use a bar scale, you must first determine how many "segments" fit into the distance measured on the map before multiplying by the real-world value of a segment.

## Activity 6 – 4: Drawing a Scaled Map

### Core Concept: Using a Number Scale
A number scale (e.g., $1 : 50$) tells us the relationship between the dimensions on a drawing and the actual dimensions in real life. 
*   The first number ($1$) represents the distance on the **map/plan**.
*   The second number ($50$) represents the **actual distance** in real life.
*   A scale of $1 : 50$ means that $1 \text{ unit}$ on the drawing represents $50 \text{ units}$ in reality.

### Procedure: Calculating Scaled Dimensions
To draw an accurate floor plan, follow these steps:
1.  **Convert all measurements to the same unit** (usually centimeters, as you will use a ruler to draw).
2.  **Divide the actual measurement** by the scale factor.
    $$\text{Scaled Dimension} = \frac{\text{Actual Dimension}}{\text{Scale Factor}}$$

### Worked Example: Bedroom Floor Plan
**Scenario:**
*   **Room dimensions:** $3,5 \text{ m}$ by $4 \text{ m}$.
*   **Single bed:** $92 \text{ cm}$ by $188 \text{ cm}$.
*   **Bedside table:** $400 \text{ mm}$ square.
*   **Scale:** $1 : 50$.

**Step 1: Convert and Calculate Dimensions**

| Item | Actual Dimension (Original) | Actual Dimension (in cm) | Calculation ($\div 50$) | Scaled Dimension (to draw) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Room Length | $4 \text{ m}$ | $400 \text{ cm}$ | $400 \div 50$ | $8 \text{ cm}$ |
| Room Width | $3,5 \text{ m}$ | $350 \text{ cm}$ | $350 \div 50$ | $7 \text{ cm}$ |
| Bed Length | $188 \text{ cm}$ | $188 \text{ cm}$ | $188 \div 50$ | $3,76 \text{ cm}$ |
| Bed Width | $92 \text{ cm}$ | $92 \text{ cm}$ | $92 \div 50$ | $1,84 \text{ cm}$ |
| Bedside Table | $400 \text{ mm}$ | $40 \text{ cm}$ | $40 \div 50$ | $0,8 \text{ cm}$ ($8 \text{ mm}$) |

**Step 2: Drawing the Plan**
Using a ruler, draw a rectangle of $8 \text{ cm} \times 7 \text{ cm}$ to represent the room. Place the bed and bedside table inside the room using their calculated scaled dimensions.

### Worked Example: Calculating Capacity

**Question:**
Calculate the total seating capacity of The Hillvale Theatre based on the seating plan provided.

**Solution:**
To find the total capacity, add the total seats in the Stalls to the total seats in the Balcony.

$$Total = \text{Stalls Total} + \text{Balcony Total}$$

$$Total = [19 + (6 \times 20) + 19 + 18] + [19 + 20 + 15 + 16 + 5 + 5]$$
$$Total = 176 + 80$$
$$Total = 256$$

The Hillvale Theatre has a total capacity of $256$ seats.
