In this chapter, we will cover:

## The Probability Scale

To describe the likelihood of an event, we use a probability scale. This is a continuous line ranging from "Impossible" to "Certain."

* **Impossible:** An event that will never happen (e.g., having 8 days in one week).
* **Certain:** An event that will definitely happen (e.g., having a Monday in a week).

Between these two extremes, we use descriptive terms: very unlikely, unlikely, even chance, likely, and very likely.

### Numerical Representation
We can assign numerical values to these probabilities. While word descriptions represent a range of values, the mathematical scale is defined as follows:

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

| Description | Numerical Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Impossible | $0$ |
| Very unlikely | $0,2$ |
| Unlikely | $0,4$ |
| Even chance | $0,5$ |
| Likely | $0,6$ |
| Very likely | $0,8$ |
| Certain | $1$ |

**Note:** Each word description covers a range of continuous probabilities on the scale; they do not always match a single specific number exactly.

## 7.2 Prediction

### Games of Chance

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

Probability games, such as those using coins and dice, help us to understand probability better. These games work with random events and are a useful way to learn how to use probabilities to predict outcomes.

#### Definition: Frequency
**Frequency** is the number of times that a specific event occurs.

---

### Probability: Key Concepts

#### Definitions
*   **Random:** When something happens without being made to happen on purpose.
*   **Trial:** A test. Examples include throwing a dice or tossing a coin.
*   **Fair:** Treated equally, without having an advantage or disadvantage.
*   **Theoretical probability:** The calculated probability, not the actual result.

---

### Random Events and Equal Chances
Games of chance are useful for learning probability because of two main factors:
1.  **Randomness:** Events cannot be deliberately influenced (assuming the game is fair).
2.  **Equal Chance:** Each possible outcome has the same probability of occurring.

#### Examples of Theoretical Probability
*   **Tossing a coin:** There is a $50\%$ or $0,5$ or $\frac{1}{2}$ chance of getting heads, and a $50\%$ or $0,5$ or $\frac{1}{2}$ chance of getting tails.
*   **Rolling a dice:** There is a $1$ in $6$ chance of throwing a $1, 2, 3, 4, 5,$ or $6$.

---

### Relative Frequency
When you perform a probability experiment (e.g., tossing a coin multiple times), you find the **relative frequency** of each outcome.

**Example:**
If you toss a coin $10$ times and get Heads $3$ times, the relative frequency is:
$$\text{Relative Frequency} = \frac{3}{10}$$

---

### Events vs. Outcomes
*   **Outcome:** The result of a single trial.
    *   *Example:* Rolling a $6$ on a dice.
*   **Event:** A collection of one or more outcomes.
    *   *Example:* Rolling an even number on a dice. This event consists of the outcomes $\{2, 4, 6\}$.

### 7.3 Fair and Unfair Games

**Concept:** A game is considered **fair** if all participants have an equal chance of winning and losing. 

**Note:** Many games of chance are intentionally designed to be **unfair**. These games are weighted to give one player a better statistical chance of winning than the other. This is often done to keep the "loser" playing, as occasional wins provide enough confidence to continue the game.

---

### Understanding Fair and Unfair Games

A **fair game** is defined as a game where there is an equal chance of winning or losing. When you understand the probability of an event, you can predict the likelihood of winning. If the rules of a game are changed, the fairness of the game may be altered.

### 7.4 Single and Combined Outcomes

#### Single Outcomes
A single outcome refers to the result of one event. For example, when flipping a coin, there are two possible outcomes: Heads or Tails.

*   Probability of getting Heads: $P(\text{Heads}) = \frac{1}{2}$
*   Probability of getting Tails: $P(\text{Tails}) = \frac{1}{2}$

To represent these probabilities visually, we use a **tree diagram**.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

### Probability: Tree Diagrams and Combined Outcomes

#### 1. Basic Tree Diagrams
A tree diagram is a visual tool used to represent the possible outcomes of an experiment and their associated probabilities.

*   **Tossing a coin:** There are two possible outcomes, Heads ($H$) or Tails ($T$), each with a probability of $\frac{1}{2}$.
    <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

*   **Rolling a dice:** There are six possible outcomes ($1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$), each with a probability of $\frac{1}{6}$.
    <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

#### 2. Combined and Compound Events
When we perform more than one experiment or use more than one object, we create **combined outcomes**.

*   **Combined Event:** An event where two or more separate experiments are performed together (e.g., flipping a coin AND rolling a dice).
*   **Compound Event:** An event involving more than one object or the repetition of an experiment.

**Example:**
If you flip a coin and roll a dice, one possible result is getting Heads on the coin and a $2$ on the dice. This is written as the combined outcome: **H2**.

---

#### 3. Tools for Listing Outcomes
To ensure all possible outcomes of a combined event are identified, we use two primary tools:
1.  **Tree Diagrams:** Used to map out the branches of possibilities for each stage of the experiment.
2.  **Two-way Tables:** Used to cross-reference outcomes from two different events.

### Probability: Two-Way Tables

#### Concept: Two-Way Tables (Contingency Tables)
A two-way table is a method used to display all possible combined outcomes of two independent events. It functions similarly to a tree diagram, where the outcomes of one event are listed in the rows and the outcomes of the second event are listed in the columns. Each cell in the table represents a unique combined outcome.

**Example: Tossing a coin twice**

| | H | T |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **H** | H, H | H, T |
| **T** | T, H | T, T |

## Understanding Weather Forecasts
Weather forecasts are calculated by the South African Weather Service by analyzing historical data. They identify days in their database with similar characteristics (such as temperature, pressure, and humidity) and determine the frequency of rainfall on those specific types of days.

### Definition: Database
A **database** is an organised collection of information, often collected over time, that is structured so that it can be easily accessed, updated, and managed. 
*   **Paper database example:** A telephone book.
*   **Computer database example:** Social media platforms (e.g., Facebook) storing user information and photos.

---

## Calculating Probability
Probability is calculated by dividing the number of favourable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes.

### Formula
$$\text{Probability} = \frac{\text{Number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}}$$

**Example:**
If there are 100 days in a database with similar weather conditions (the sample space), and it rained on 60 of those days (favourable outcomes), the probability of rain is:
$$\frac{60}{100} = 60\%$$

### Complementary Events
The total probability of all possible outcomes must equal $100\%$. 
*   If the probability of rain is $60\%$, the probability that it will **not** rain is calculated as:
$$100\% - 60\% = 40\%$$

*   A $50\%$ probability indicates an equal chance of an event occurring or not occurring.
*   Any probability greater than $50\%$ indicates that the event is more likely to occur than not.

### Key Concept: Law of Large Numbers
You should find that the relative frequency gets closer to the theoretical probability when you increase the number of trials (throws). Each individual result is caused by chance, but as the number of trials increases, the experimental results will eventually reflect the theoretical probability.

### Probability and Relative Frequency: Practical Investigation

#### Core Concepts
*   **Theoretical Probability:** The mathematical expectation of an event occurring. For a fair object (like a standard 6-sided die), the probability of any specific outcome is:
    $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{1}{\text{number of possible outcomes}}$$
*   **Relative Frequency (Experimental Probability):** The actual ratio of the number of times an event occurs to the total number of trials performed:
    $$\text{Relative Frequency} = \frac{\text{Number of times event occurred}}{\text{Total number of trials}}$$
*   **Law of Large Numbers:** As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency of an outcome will generally get closer to the theoretical probability.

---

# Probability and Fair Games

## Worked Example: 8-Sided Dice
Consider an 8-sided die with faces numbered 1 through 8.

**a) List the possible outcomes when throwing one of these dice.**
The set of possible outcomes is: $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}$

**b) What are the theoretical chances of throwing a "7" on one of these dice?**
The probability $P$ of an event is calculated as:
$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}}$$
$$P(7) = \frac{1}{8} = 0,125 = 12,5\%$$

**c) Is it more likely that you will get an even number on these dice than on normal 6-sided dice? Explain.**
No, the chance is the same. 
*   On an 8-sided die, there are 4 even numbers $\{2, 4, 6, 8\}$ out of 8 total outcomes: $P(\text{even}) = \frac{4}{8} = 0,5$.
*   On a 6-sided die, there are 3 even numbers $\{2, 4, 6\}$ out of 6 total outcomes: $P(\text{even}) = \frac{3}{6} = 0,5$.
Since both equal $50\%$, the likelihood is identical.

## 7.3 Fair and Unfair Games

### Definitions
*   **Fair Game:** A game where all players have an equal mathematical probability of winning.
*   **Unfair Game:** A game where one player has a higher mathematical probability of winning than the other(s).

### Activity 7-4: Inventing Games
In this activity, you are required to invent two games using a single 6-sided die.

**a) Designing a Fair Game:**
To make a game fair, ensure the winning conditions for Player A and Player B have the same probability.
*   *Example:* Player A wins if the die shows an odd number $\{1, 3, 5\}$. Player B wins if the die shows an even number $\{2, 4, 6\}$.
*   *Probability:* $P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = 0,5$; $P(B) = \frac{3}{6} = 0,5$.

**b) Designing an Unfair Game:**
To make a game unfair, ensure the winning conditions have unequal probabilities.
*   *Example:* Player A wins if the die shows a $6$. Player B wins if the die shows any other number $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$.
*   *Probability:* $P(A) = \frac{1}{6} \approx 16,7\%$; $P(B) = \frac{5}{6} \approx 83,3\%$.

### Probability: Two-Way Tables

# 7.5 Weather Predictions

## Understanding Weather Forecasting

Weather forecasting is the application of science and technology to predict the state of the atmosphere for a future time and a given location. Forecasters use historical data, current atmospheric conditions (such as temperature, pressure, and humidity), and mathematical models to generate predictions.

### Key Concepts

*   **Data Collection:** Forecasters monitor current weather characteristics.
*   **Pattern Recognition:** By comparing current data to accumulated historical data, forecasters identify trends.
*   **Probability:** Weather predictions are rarely absolute certainties. They are expressed as probabilities based on past occurrences under similar conditions.

*   **Task:** Explain how forecasters use past data.
*   **Solution:** Forecasters analyze variables like temperature, pressure, and humidity, comparing them to historical patterns to estimate future outcomes.

**5. Interpreting Probability**
