## 1. Closing Transfers

### 1.1 Introduction
After completing the accounting cycle up to the Trial Balance, a business must prepare Financial Statements to determine the profit or loss for the year. 

*   **Financial Statements:** Comprise the Income Statement and the Balance Sheet.
*   **Financial Period:** A pre-determined 12-month period used for accounting purposes.

### 1.2 The Closing Transfer Process
Income and expense accounts are **temporary accounts**. They must be closed off at the end of the financial year to reset their balances for the new period.

#### 1.2.1 Steps in the Closing Transfer Process
1.  **Transfer Debtors' Allowances:** Transfer the Debtors' Allowances account to the Sales account to calculate **Net Sales**.
2.  **Transfer to Trading Account:** Transfer the Sales account and the Cost of Sales account to the Trading account.
3.  **Determine Gross Profit:** Balance the Trading account. Transfer the resulting Gross Profit to the Profit and Loss account.
4.  **Transfer Income and Expenses:** Transfer all remaining income and expense accounts to the Profit and Loss account.
5.  **Determine Net Profit:** Balance the Profit and Loss account to determine the Net Profit.
6.  **Transfer to Capital:** Transfer the balance of the Profit and Loss account to the Capital account.
7.  **Close Drawings:** Close the Drawings account by transferring it to the Capital account.
8.  **Balance Capital:** Balance the Capital account.
9.  **Post-closing Trial Balance:** Prepare a Post-closing trial balance to ensure the books are balanced for the start of the new year.

## 1. The Closing Process
At the end of the financial year, nominal accounts (income and expenses) must be closed off to determine the net profit or loss. This is done by transferring balances to the **Trading Account** and the **Profit and Loss Account**.

### Trading Account
The Trading Account is used to calculate the **Gross Profit**.
*   **Debit side:** Cost of Sales.
*   **Credit side:** Sales (net of returns).
*   **Balance:** The difference is transferred to the Profit and Loss Account as Gross Profit.

### Profit and Loss Account
The Profit and Loss Account is used to calculate the **Net Profit**.
*   **Debit side:** All operating expenses.
*   **Credit side:** Gross Profit (from Trading Account) and all other income.
*   **Balance:** The difference is transferred to the Capital account.

## 3. Year-End Adjustments
Adjustments are made at the end of the financial year to ensure that financial statements comply with the matching principle: only income earned and expenses incurred during the specific financial period are recorded.

### Purpose of Adjustments
*   To accurately determine the profit for a specific period.
*   To comply with **Generally Accepted Accounting Practice (GAAP)**.
*   Adjustments are initially recorded in the **General Journal**.

### Steps in Doing Year-End Adjustments
1.  **Prepare journal entries** for the adjustments.
2.  **Journalise** all closing transfers.
3.  **Post** entries to the General Ledger.
4.  Prepare **Final Accounts** (Trading Account and Profit and Loss Account).
5.  Prepare **Financial Statements** (Statement of Comprehensive Income and Statement of Financial Position).

## 2.2 Depreciation

### Historical Cost Principle
According to the **Historical cost principle** of GAAP, land and buildings must be recorded at their original purchase price in the financial statements, regardless of any re-valued market amount.

### Definition of Depreciation
Depreciation is an imputed expense representing the periodical cost related to the use of vehicles and equipment. It is based on the **Matching principle** of GAAP, which ensures that the cost of an asset is allocated over the periods in which it generates revenue.

### Factors Influencing Depreciation
When calculating the depreciation percentage, the following factors must be considered:
*   The purpose for which the fixed asset is used.
*   Technological changes (obsolescence).
*   Wear and tear (ageing).

### Double Entry for Depreciation
To record the annual depreciation expense:
*   **Debit:** Depreciation (Expense)
*   **Credit:** Accumulated depreciation on vehicles/equipment (Negative asset)

### Key Distinction
| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Depreciation** | The amount written off for the **current year** only. |
| **Accumulated depreciation** | The total amount written off for the **current year plus all previous years**. |

## Depreciation
Depreciation is the allocation of the cost of a tangible asset over its useful life.

### Worked Example
*   **Balance for accumulated depreciation (1 January 2008):** R23 400
*   **Depreciation written off (31 December 2008):** R3 100
*   **Accumulated depreciation (31 December 2008):** R23 400 + R3 100 = R26 500

---

### Methods of Calculating Depreciation

1.  **Fixed Amount Method** (also known as Percentage on cost price or Straight-line method):
    *   A fixed amount of depreciation is written off annually.
    *   The depreciation expense remains the same every year.

2.  **Diminished Balance Method** (also known as Book value, Carrying value, or Declining method):
    *   The depreciation amount decreases from year to year.
    *   It is calculated based on the carrying value of the asset.

**Formula for Carrying Value:**
$$\text{Carrying value} = \text{Cost price} - \text{Accumulated depreciation}$$

---

### Important Considerations
*   **Consistency:** You cannot change the depreciation method every year. The choice of method depends on the nature of the asset and its expected lifespan.
*   **Residual Value:** The value of an asset can never be reflected as R0 in the financial statements. The final value is shown as R1 in the Balance Sheet.
*   **Pro-rata Depreciation:** If an asset is purchased during a financial period, depreciation is calculated only for the number of months the asset was in the possession of the business.
*   **Installation Costs:** Costs incurred to install an asset (e.g., computer setup) are capitalized and form part of the purchase price of the equipment.

---

### Asset Register
An asset register is a document kept by the business for all tangible assets.

*   **Purpose:** It provides detailed information regarding each specific asset.
*   **Example:** If a business owns four vehicles, a separate record is kept for each vehicle in the asset register.
*   **General Ledger:** Despite having individual records in the asset register, only one "Vehicles" account is maintained in the General Ledger.
*   **Valuation:** The amount recorded in the General Ledger for vehicles must always be at the original **cost price**.

## 2.3 Accrued Income (Income Receivable)

**Definition:** Income that has been earned during the accounting period but has not yet been received by the end of that period.

**GAAP Principle:** Matching Principle (Income must be recorded in the period it is earned, regardless of when the cash is received).

### Elements of Accrued Income
Accrued income consists of two distinct elements:
1.  **Asset element:** Recorded in the Balance Sheet section.
2.  **Income element:** Recorded in the Income Statement section.

### Important Information for Adjustments
*   The date of the financial year-end.
*   The balance of the specific income account as it appears in the pre-adjustment trial balance.

---

### Worked Example: Rent Income
**Scenario:** On 28 February 2011, the accumulated balance in the Rent Income account in the general ledger of MN Traders totaled $R55\,000$. The adjustment indicates that rent has been received for eleven months only.

#### Analysis of Information
To determine the monthly rent and the outstanding amount:
$$\frac{55\,000}{11} = R5\,000 \text{ per month}$$

Since the rent for the 12th month is outstanding, the amount of $R5\,000$ must be brought into account for the current financial year as **accrued income**.

**Effect on Accounting Equation:**
*   **Assets:** Increase (Accrued Income)
*   **Equity:** Increase (Rent Income)

## 2.5 Prepaid Expenses

Prepaid expenses are expenses paid for in the current accounting period that relate to the next accounting period.

*   **GAAP Principle:** Matching Principle.
*   **Elements:**
    *   **Asset element:** Recorded in the Balance Sheet section.
    *   **Expense element:** Recorded in the Nominal section.

### Important Information Required
*   The date of the financial year-end.
*   The balance of the expense account as shown in the pre-adjustment trial balance.

---

### Worked Example: Insurance
**Scenario:** On 28 February 2011, the accumulated total in the Insurance account for MN Traders was $R16\ 800$. This total includes an amount of $R2\ 400$ paid on 1 September 2010 for a one-year period (1 September 2010 to 31 August 2011).

#### Analysis of Information
1.  **Calculate monthly cost:**
    $$\frac{R2\ 400}{12} = R200 \text{ per month}$$
2.  **Calculate prepaid period:**
    From March 2011 to 31 August 2011 = $6 \text{ months}$
3.  **Calculate prepaid amount:**
    $$6 \text{ months} \times R200 = R1\ 200 \text{ (prepaid amount)}$$

#### Accounting Impact
*   **Asset account:** Prepaid expenses (increases).
*   **Expense account:** Insurance (decreases).
*   **Accounting Equation:** Assets increase, Equity increases.

## 2.7 Consumable Stores on Hand

### Concept Definition
Businesses purchase various consumable items to conduct daily operations. While these are recorded as **expenses** at the time of purchase, any items remaining unused at the end of the financial year are not considered expenses. Instead, they are classified as **temporary assets**.

**Common examples include:**
* Stationery
* Cleaning material
* Fuel
* Packing material

### The Two Elements of Consumable Stores
When accounting for consumable stores at year-end, two accounts are affected:
1. **Asset Account:** Recorded in the Balance Sheet section.
2. **Expense Account:** Recorded in the Nominal section.

---

### Worked Example: MN Traders
**Scenario:** On 28 February 2011, the totals in the General Ledger for MN Traders were:
* Packing material: $R12\,400$
* Stationery: $R15\,100$

**Adjustment:** A physical count on 28 February 2011 revealed the following items were still on hand:
* Packing material: $R1\,400$
* Stationery: $R2\,300$

#### Analysis of the Adjustment
To determine the actual expense for the year, we subtract the items on hand from the ledger totals:

| Item | Ledger Total ($R$) | Less: On Hand ($R$) | Actual Expense ($R$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Packing material | $12\,400$ | $1\,400$ | $11\,000$ |
| Stationery | $15\,100$ | $2\,300$ | $12\,800$ |

#### Accounting Impact
* **Asset Account:** Consumable stores on hand (Total value of items remaining).
* **Expense Account:** Packing material and Stationery (The value of items actually consumed).
* **Effect on Accounting Equation:** 
    * Assets increase (due to the creation of the "Consumable stores on hand" asset).
    * Equity increases (due to the reduction of expenses).

## 2.8 Trading Stock Deficit
### The Continuous / Perpetual Inventory System
A stock count may reveal that there is less stock than reflected in the trading stock account (a **deficit**) or more than the disclosed figure (a **surplus**). A journal adjustment entry is necessary to ensure that the trading stock account reflects the correct value of stock on hand.

**The two elements involved are:**
1. An expense account (**Trading stock deficit**)
2. An asset account (**Trading stock**)

**Effect on Accounting Equation:**
*   Equity decreases
*   Asset decreases

### Example: Trading Stock Deficit
On 28 February 2011, the trading stock account in the general ledger of MN Traders reflected a balance of $R34\ 500$. The physical stock count revealed the value of goods on hand to be $R32\ 200$.

**Calculation of Deficit:**
$$\text{Deficit} = \text{Balance per Ledger} - \text{Physical Stock Count}$$
$$\text{Deficit} = 34\ 500 - 32\ 200 = 2\ 300$$

## Key Concepts

### 1. Pre-adjustment Trial Balance
A list of all ledger account balances at the end of the financial year before any year-end adjustments (such as depreciation or accrued expenses) have been recorded.

### 2. Adjustments
These are entries made at the end of the financial period to ensure that the financial statements comply with the accrual principle. They update accounts to reflect the true financial position.

### 3. Post-adjustment Trial Balance
The final list of account balances after all adjustments have been posted to the general ledger. This is used to prepare the final Financial Statements (Statement of Financial Position and Statement of Comprehensive Income).

## Mathematical Notes

*   **Interest Calculations:** For items like the Fixed Deposit and Loan, interest is calculated using the formula:
    $$Interest = Principal \times Rate \times Time$$
    Where the rate is expressed as a percentage (e.g., $12\% = \frac{12}{100}$).
*   **Balancing:** Ensure that for every entry, the total debits equal the total credits:
    $$\sum \text{Debits} = \sum \text{Credits}$$
