# Topic 2: Analysis of Financial Statements

## 1. Introduction to Concepts

The analysis and interpretation of financial statements is an evaluation process used to assess the current and previous financial positions and results of an enterprise. A **ratio** is the mathematical relationship between two different figures, expressed in simple terms. To be useful, ratios must express meaningful relationships and be compared against benchmarks or comparative figures.

The primary aims of analyzing and interpreting financial statements are to determine:

### 1.1 Profitability
Profitability measures the success of a business by comparing the profit made against the amount sold or invested. It is also a key indicator used to assess whether the business is experiencing cost control problems.

### 1.2 Return
Return determines if the investors (owners) are receiving a reasonable reward on the capital they have invested in the business.

### 1.3 Solvency
Solvency measures the ability of the business to meet its financial obligations on time and its capacity to pay off long-term debts.

## Mathematical Representation of Ratios

In accounting, ratios are expressed as a relationship between two variables, $A$ and $B$. This is generally represented as:

$$Ratio = \frac{A}{B}$$

Where:
*   $A$ represents the numerator (e.g., Net Profit)
*   $B$ represents the denominator (e.g., Sales or Capital)

Depending on the specific ratio, the result may be expressed as:
1.  **A percentage:** $\frac{A}{B} \times 100$
2.  **A ratio (e.g., 2:1):** $\frac{A}{B}$
3.  **A rate (e.g., times per year):** $\frac{A}{B}$

## Calculating Gross Profit Percentages

Gross profit can be expressed as a percentage of either **Sales (Turnover)** or **Cost of Sales**.

### 1. Gross Profit as a Percentage of Sales
To calculate the gross profit percentage based on sales, use the following formula:

$$\frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Sales}} \times \frac{100}{1} = \text{Percentage}$$

**Example:**
If Gross Profit is R750 and Sales are R3 750:
$$\frac{750}{3 750} \times \frac{100}{1} = 20\%$$

### 2. Gross Profit as a Percentage of Cost of Sales
To calculate the gross profit percentage based on cost of sales, use the following formula:

$$\frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Cost of Sales}} \times \frac{100}{1} = \text{Percentage}$$

**Example:**
If Gross Profit is R750 and Cost of Sales is R3 000:
$$\frac{750}{3 000} \times \frac{100}{1} = 25\%$$

---

## Key Concept: Relationship between Mark-up and Margin
A profit mark-up of **20% on sales** is equivalent to a profit mark-up of **25% on cost of sales**. If a business maintains this mark-up consistently, these percentages will remain constant in the final Income Statement.

### Worked Example: Income Statement Analysis
Consider the following financial data for a business:

| Item | Amount (R) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Sales (240 articles) | 900 000 |
| Less: Cost of sales | (720 000) |

#### Analysis Calculations:

**Gross profit expressed as a percentage of sales:**
$$\frac{180 000}{900 000} \times \frac{100}{1} = 20\%$$

**Gross profit expressed as a percentage of Cost of sales:**
$$\frac{180 000}{720 000} \times \frac{100}{1} = 25\%$$

## Financial Ratios

### a) Percentage Gross Profit on Turnover
This ratio expresses the gross profit of a financial year as a percentage of the net sales (turnover). If a business maintains a constant mark-up policy, this percentage should remain consistent.

**Formula:**
$$\frac{\text{Gross profit}}{\text{Turnover}} \times \frac{100}{1}$$

**Reasons for variation in this ratio:**
*   Theft of inventory.
*   Incorrect sales calculations.
*   Trade discounts allowed to customers.

### b) Percentage Gross Profit on Cost of Sales
This ratio determines the effectiveness of the business's purchasing policy and inventory storage management. A decrease in this percentage compared to the previous year may indicate theft in the warehouse.

**Formula:**
$$\frac{\text{Gross profit}}{\text{Cost of sales}} \times \frac{100}{1}$$

### c) Percentage Operating Profit on Turnover
This ratio represents the operating profit as a percentage of turnover. It accounts for income and expenses before interest is considered. It is used to evaluate how effectively business expenses are being managed; a lower percentage suggests better expense management.

**Formula:**
$$\frac{\text{Operating profit}}{\text{Turnover}} \times \frac{100}{1}$$

## Profitability Ratios (Continued)

### d) Net profit on turnover
This ratio represents the net profit for a financial period as a percentage of sales.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Net profit on turnover} = \frac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Turnover}} \times 100$$

*   **Analysis:** This percentage should ideally be $\frac{1}{3}$ of the percentage gross profit on turnover.
*   **Interpretation:** If the percentage differs significantly from the gross profit on turnover, it indicates that expenses are too high. ### e) Total expenses as a percentage of turnover
This ratio shows the portion of sales used to cover operating expenses.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Total expenses as a percentage of turnover} = \frac{\text{Operating expenses}}{\text{Turnover}} \times 100$$

*   **Analysis:** This relates directly to cost control.
*   **Interpretation:** Compare this number to the previous year. The percentage should ideally remain the same or decrease. A lower percentage indicates that expenses are being managed well.

## 3.2 Liquidity

Liquidity refers to the ability of a business to meet its short-term obligations (current liabilities) using available funds (current assets that can be converted to cash).

### a) Current ratio
The current ratio measures the extent to which current assets cover current liabilities.

**Formula:**
$$\text{Current ratio} = \text{Current assets} : \text{Current liabilities}$$

*   **Calculation:** The current assets are divided by the current liabilities to express the ratio as $x : 1$.
*   **Interpretation:** The higher the ratio, the greater the business's ability to meet its short-term obligations. 
*   **Example:** If the ratio is $2 : 1$, it means there are current assets of $R2$ available for every $R1$ of current liabilities.

---

# Term 3 Topic 2: Analysis of Financial Statements

## b) Acid Test Ratio
The acid test ratio is a measure of liquidity that excludes inventories, as these cannot always be immediately converted to cash (they may be dated, stolen, or broken).

### Formula
$$\text{Acid test ratio} = (\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventories}) : \text{Current liabilities}$$

*   **Note:** Inventories include both Trading stock and consumable stores on hand. Both must be deducted from current assets.
*   **Interpretation:** The ratio is expressed as $x : 1$. A business is considered liquid if the ratio is greater than $1$ (e.g., $1,2 : 1$). This means for every $R1$ of current liability, there is $R1,20$ available in liquid assets.

---

## 3.3 Solvency of the Business
Solvency refers to the ability of a business to meet its long-term financial obligations.

### Formula
$$\text{Solvency ratio} = \text{Total assets} : \text{Total liabilities}$$

### Component Definitions
*   $$\text{Total assets} = \text{Tangible assets} + \text{Fixed deposit} + \text{Current assets}$$
*   $$\text{Total liabilities} = \text{Non-current liabilities} + \text{Current liabilities}$$

### Status Indicators
*   **Solvent:** $\text{Total assets} > \text{Total liabilities}$
*   **Insolvent:** $\text{Total assets} < \text{Total liabilities}$

*   **Interpretation:** The ratio is expressed as $x : 1$. A business is regarded as solvent if the ratio is not lower than $1 : 1$.

---

## 3.4 Risk
The debt/equity ratio is used to assess the financial risk of the business.

### Formula
$$\text{Debt/equity ratio} = \text{Non-current liabilities} : \text{Owners' equity}$$

### Component Definitions
*   $$\text{Owners' equity} = \text{Capital balances of partners} + \text{Current account balances of partners}$$
*   **Note:** If a partner's current account has a debit balance, subtract that amount from the total.

*   **Interpretation:** This ratio indicates how the business is financed (i.e., the proportion of owner-provided capital versus borrowed money). Borrowed money carries a cost to the business.

## Borrowed Capital and Financial Risk
When a business borrows money, it must pay interest. Relying heavily on borrowed capital increases financial risk. If profits decline, the business must still repay the loan and interest, which creates financial strain.

### Debt to Equity Ratio
The debt to equity ratio indicates the extent to which a business is financed by borrowed capital versus owner's equity. It helps stakeholders assess the level of risk the business has taken on.

#### Favourability of Borrowed Capital
*   **Acceptable Ratio:** $1:1$ (For every R1 invested by owners, there is R1 of borrowed funds).
*   **Low-Risk (Favourable Gearing):** A ratio such as $0,7:1$ suggests the business relies more on equity. This usually results in a good credit rating.
*   **High-Risk (Negative Gearing):** A ratio such as $1,3:1$ suggests the business relies heavily on borrowed funds. This may result in a poor credit rating.
*   **Lending Criteria:** Financial providers (banks) generally consider lending to a business if the ratio is between $0,5:1$ and $1:1$.

## 1.5 Percentage Return on Average Owners' Equity

### Formula
To calculate the percentage return on average owners' equity, use the following formula:

$$\text{Return on Average Equity} = \frac{\text{Net Profit after Tax}}{\text{Average Owners' Equity}} \times 100$$

$$\text{Average Owners' Equity} = \frac{\text{Opening Balance} + \text{Closing Balance}}{2}$$

When commenting on this ratio, consider the following:
* **Comparison:** Compare the result against the previous year's performance or the industry average.
* **Investment Viability:** A higher percentage indicates that the business is generating a better return for the owners on the capital they have invested.
* **Risk:** If the return is lower than the interest rate on alternative investments (like a fixed deposit), the business may not be performing efficiently.

# Cost Accounting: Fixed and Variable Costs

## Core Concepts

### 1. Fixed Costs
Fixed costs are expenses that remain constant regardless of the level of production or the number of items produced. Even if production output increases or decreases, these costs do not change.

### 2. Variable Costs
Variable costs are expenses that change in direct proportion to the number of items produced. As production increases, total variable costs increase; as production decreases, total variable costs decrease.

---

## Worked Example: Profit Calculation

To calculate the profit of a production run, use the following formula:

$$\text{Profit} = \text{Selling Price (SP)} - \text{Variable Costs (VC)} - \text{Fixed Costs (FC)}$$

**Scenario:**
*   Number of units produced: $100$
*   Variable cost per unit: $R99$
*   Fixed costs: $R1\,750$
*   Selling price per unit: $R150$

**Calculation:**
1.  **Total Variable Cost:** $100 \text{ units} \times R99 = R9\,900$
2.  **Total Selling Price (SP):** $100 \text{ units} \times R150 = R15\,000$
3.  **Profit:**
    $$\text{Profit} = R15\,000 - R9\,900 - R1\,750$$
    $$\text{Profit} = R3\,350$$
