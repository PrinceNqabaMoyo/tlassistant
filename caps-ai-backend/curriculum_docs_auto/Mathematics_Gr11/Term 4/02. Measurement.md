# 9 Measurement of perimeter and area

## 9.1 Introduction and key concepts

Knowing how to calculate the perimeter and area of an object is useful in many contexts, particularly when we need to know how much of a material is required for a task and how much it is going to cost. Some examples of this are calculating the area of a wall to ensure we buy the correct quantity of paint, or calculating the perimeter of a vegetable garden so we know how much fencing to order.

In this chapter we will learn how to:

* find the perimeter and area of rectangles, squares, triangles and circles by direct measurement and estimation.
* accurately calculate perimeter and area of rectangles, squares, triangles and circles using formulae.
* solve real-life problems and tasks relating to perimeter, area and cost.

> **DEFINITION: Formulae**
> 
> The plural of formula. One formula, many formulae.

---

## 9.2 Measuring perimeter

> **DEFINITION: Perimeter**
> 
> The total length of the outside of a shape or the continuous line forming the boundary of a closed geometric figure. Perimeter is measured in $\text{mm}$, $\text{cm}$, $\text{m}$ or $\text{km}$.

In this section we will find the perimeter of rectangles, squares and triangles by measuring them directly, and the perimeter of a circle by estimation. We will not be using any formulae just yet.

> **DEFINITION: Rectangle**
> 
> A flat two-dimensional shape, with both pairs of opposite sides equal in length and all adjacent sides at right angles ($90^\circ$) to each other.

---

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**DEFINITION: Square**

A flat, two dimensional shape with all four sides equal in length, and all adjacent sides at right-angles ($90^{\circ}$) to each other.

## Estimation and direct measurement of perimeter

**EMG5S**

To measure the perimeter of a rectangle, a square or a triangle, we simply measure the length of each side using a ruler and add up the sides to get the perimeter.

To measure the perimeter of a circle, we need to use a piece of string: we can place the string along the outline of the circle, marking off how much string it took to go around the circle once. Then we measure that length of string on a ruler to estimate the perimeter of the circle.

The perimeter of a circle is the same as the **circumference** of the circle.

**DEFINITION: Circumference**

The perimeter of a circle or the distance around the curved edge of a circle. Circumference is measured in $\text{mm}$; $\text{cm}$; $\text{m}$ or $\text{km}$.

# Using formulae to calculate perimeter

We have learnt how to directly measure perimeter and circumference, but in some cases (like measuring the circumference of a circle using a piece of string) we have seen that it is difficult to be accurate.

There are simple formulae we can use to accurately calculate perimeter and circumference. In order to use these formulae, we need to know certain dimensions of the shape we want to measure.

## Perimeter Formulae Table

| Shape | Perimeter formula | Diagram |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Rectangle | $2 \times \text{length} + 2 \times \text{width}$ | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> |
| Square | $4 \times \text{length} \text{ or } 4 \times \text{side}$ | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> |
| Triangle | $\text{length } 1 + \text{length } 2 + \text{length } 3$ | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> |
| Circle | $\pi \times (2 \times \text{radius}) \text{ and/or } \pi \times \text{diameter}$ | <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram --> |

> **NOTE:**  
> You will always be given the above formulae in your assessments.

> **DEFINITION:** *Radius*  
> The radius ($r$) of a circle is a straight line drawn from the centre to the extreme edge of the circle, or the length of the line from the centre of the circle to any point on its edge.

---

## DEFINITIONS

### Diameter
The diameter ($d$) of a circle is a straight line drawn from one edge of the circle to the other, that passes through the centre of the circle.

$$\text{diameter} = 2 \times \text{radius}$$

### Pi
Pi ($\pi$) is a special symbol we use when calculating perimeter and area of circles. The value of $\pi$ is $3{,}141592645\dots$ continuing to infinity. For all of our calculations however, we will use the approximate value of $\pi = 3{,}142$.

### **SOLUTION**

1. a) 
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Radius} &= 50\text{ mm} = 5\text{ cm} \\
\text{Circumference} &= \pi \times (2 \times \text{radius}) \\
&= \pi \times (2 \times 5\text{ cm}) \\
&= 3,142 \times 10\text{ cm} \\
&= 31,42\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}$$

---

b) $\text{Diameter} = 40\text{ cm}$

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Circumference} &= \pi \times \text{diameter} \\
&= \pi \times 40\text{ cm} \\
&= 3,142 \times 40\text{ cm} \\
&= 125,68\text{ cm} \\
&\approx 125,7\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}$$

c) We know the total circumference of the large, outer circle is $125,7\text{ cm}$.

The perimeter of half of the larger circle is simply the total circumference $\div 2$:

$$\frac{125,7\text{ cm}}{2} = 62,85\text{ cm}$$

d) $\text{Width of yellow area} = \text{radius of large circle} - \text{radius of small circle}$

$$\begin{aligned}
&= \frac{40}{2}\text{ cm} - 5\text{ cm} \\
&= 20\text{ cm} - 5\text{ cm} \\
&= 15\text{ cm}
\end{aligned}$$

---

2. We can see that this complex shape is actually just a square and a triangle. We only measure the outer three sides of the square (which are equal in length), and the outer two sides of the triangle. So:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Perimeter} &= 3 \text{ equal (outer) sides of square} + 2 \text{ outer sides of triangle} \\
&= (3 \times 300\text{ mm}) + 50\text{ cm} + 0,4\text{ m}
\end{aligned}$$

First we change all the units to metres and then we calculate the sum of these sides:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Perimeter} &= (3 \times 0,3\text{ m}) + 0,5\text{ m} + 0,4\text{ m} \\
&= 0,9\text{ m} + 0,9\text{ m} \\
&= 1,8\text{ m}
\end{aligned}$$

---

3. This shape is not as obvious a combination of regular shapes like in the previous question, but we can still easily calculate the perimeter by looking carefully at the dimensions given.

First we change all units to metres, and then we calculate the sum of the sides:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Perimeter} &= 3\text{ m} + 2\text{ m} + 4\text{ m} + 300\text{ cm} + (4\text{ m} + 3\text{ m}) + (300\text{ cm} + 2\text{ m}) \\
&= 3\text{ m} + 4\text{ m} + 4\text{ m} + 3\text{ m} + (4\text{ m} + 3\text{ m}) + (3\text{ m} + 2\text{ m}) \\
&= 26\text{ m}
\end{aligned}$$

# 9.3 Measuring area

EMG5V

> **DEFINITION: Area**
> 
> The size of a surface or plane, or the amount of space inside the boundary of a flat (2 dimensional) object such as a square, triangle or circle. Area is measured in $\text{mm}^2$; $\text{cm}^2$; $\text{m}^2$ or $\text{km}^2$.

## Estimating area

EMG5W

We can estimate area without using any formulae. One way of doing this is to place a square grid over the area we wish to estimate. If we know the size of the squares, we can then count up how many squares are filled by the area we wish to measure. As we shall see, for some shapes, this method is easy to use and accurate (we would get the same answer for our area as we would using a formula). For other shapes it becomes harder to estimate area accurately however, because they don’t fit neatly into a square grid.

---

**QUESTION**

### QUESTION

Using the grid below, estimate the area of the triangular garden. Each square in the grid is $1 \text{ cm}$ by $1 \text{ cm}$.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### SOLUTION

* First, count the number of whole blocks that fit inside the triangle.
  There are $18$ blocks that are completely inside the perimeter of the triangle.

* Next, count the number of half-blocks that fit inside the triangle, and divide the total by $2$.
  There are $4$ half-blocks covered by the triangle. This is equivalent to $2$ whole blocks.

* Now we count the remaining blocks and estimate how many whole blocks they would make up.
  There are $9$ blocks left that are approximately equivalent to $4\frac{1}{2}$ whole blocks.

# Lesson Revision Notes: 9.3. Measuring Area

## Core Concepts

When calculating the area of geometric shapes:
* **Using formulas** is more accurate and simpler than estimating using a grid, especially for triangles and circles.
* **Complex shapes** can be measured by breaking them down into smaller, simpler shapes, finding the area of each individual shape, and then adding them together.

---

## Formulas and Worked Examples

### 1. Area of a Rectangle
$$\text{Area} = \text{length} \times \text{width}$$

**Example:**
$$\text{Area} = 5\text{ cm} \times 6\text{ cm} = 30\text{ cm}^2$$

---

### 2. Area of a Triangle (b)
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{perpendicular height}$$

**Example (Triangular Garden with base $12\text{ cm}$ and height $4\text{ cm}$):**
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 4\text{ cm} \times 12\text{ cm} = 24\text{ cm}^2$$

---

### 3. Area of a Circle (c)
$$\text{Area} = \pi \times \text{radius}^2$$

**Example (Circular Pond with radius $r = 1\text{ cm}$, using $\pi \approx 3,142$):**
$$\text{Area} = 3,142 \times (1\text{ cm})^2$$
$$\text{Area} = 3,142 \times 1\text{ cm}^2$$
$$\text{Area} = 3,142\text{ cm}^2$$

---

## Activities and Analysis

### Question
Compare your answers to those you got when estimating the area of the triangular garden and circular pond using a grid. Are they different? If so, why?

### Solution & Explanation
* **Rectangular Patio:** The answer using the formula is the same as the grid estimate because the patio fitted neatly into the square grid without needing to guess partial blocks.
* **Triangular Garden:** The grid estimation gave $24,5\text{ cm}^2$, whereas the formula gives $24\text{ cm}^2$. The estimate is larger due to counting partial blocks on the grid.
* **Circular Fish Pond:** The grid estimation gave $3\text{ cm}^2$, whereas the formula gives $3,142\text{ cm}^2$. The estimate is smaller because curved edges make it difficult to accurately count fractional grid squares.

> **Conclusion:** Using formulas to calculate area is both more accurate and simpler than estimating using a grid.

# Worked example 10: Using formulae to calculate area

## QUESTION

1. Your Maths Literacy classroom gets new tables, shaped as shown below.

   <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

   a) Using the appropriate formulae, calculate the area of each table, in $\text{m}^2$.
   b) If each table cost $\text{R } 615,00$ and ten tables were bought, calculate how much the tables cost per $\text{m}^2$. (Hint: calculate the total cost of the tables and their total area first).

2. For your birthday, a friend gives you a rare, lucky coin that has a square cut out of the middle as shown in the photo and diagram below:

   <!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

   a) You measure the diameter of the circle to be $3\text{ cm}$, and the length of one side of the square to be $0,9\text{ cm}$. Calculate the area of the coin in $\text{cm}^2$. Round your answer to one decimal place.
   b) If the coin is worth $\text{R } 3,58\text{ per cm}^2$, calculate its value.

---

## SOLUTION

1. We can see that the table is made up of two identical triangles, and one rectangle.

   a) 
   * The formula for the area of a triangle is $\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$. So the area of one of our triangles is:
     
     $$\frac{1}{2} \times 500\text{ mm} \times 70\text{ cm}$$
     
     $$= \frac{1}{2} \times 0,5\text{ m} \times 0,7\text{ m} \text{ (change the units to be metres)}$$
     
     $$= 0,175\text{ m}^2$$

---

* The formula for the area of a rectangle is $\text{length} \times \text{height}$, so the area of the middle rectangle is:
  $0,9\text{ m} \times 70\text{ cm}$
  $= 0,9\text{ m} \times 0,7\text{ m}$ (change the units to be metres)
  $= 0,63\text{ m}^2$

* Now we simply add the three areas together:
  $\text{Area triangle} + \text{area rectangle} + \text{area triangle}$
  $= 0,175\text{ m}^2 + 0,63\text{ m}^2 + 0,175\text{ m}^2$
  $= 0,98\text{ m}^2$

b) 10 tables will cost $\text{R } 615 \times 10 = \text{R } 6\,150$.
10 tables will have a total area of $0,98\text{ m}^2 \times 10 = 9,80\text{ m}^2$

$$\frac{\text{R } 6\,150}{9,80\text{ m}^2} = \text{R } 627,55$$

So the tables cost $\text{R } 627,55$ per square metre.

---

2. a) To calculate the area of the coin, we need to calculate the area of the circle, and then subtract from this the area of the square cutout.
   * The formula for the area of a circle is $\pi \times \text{radius}^2$.
     We know the diameter is $3\text{ cm}$, therefore the radius is $\frac{3\text{ cm}}{2} = 1,5\text{ cm}$
     Therefore the area of the circle is:
     $\pi \times (1,5\text{ cm})^2$
     $= 3,142 \times 2,25\text{ cm}^2$
     $= 7,0695\text{ cm}^2$
     (Remember, we shouldn't round to one decimal point while we are still busy with our calculations! We should only round our final answer.)
   * The formula for the area of a square is $\text{side} \times \text{side} = (\text{side})^2$
     Therefore the area of the square is:
     $(0,9\text{ cm})^2 = 0,81\text{ cm}^2$
   * We now subtract the area of the cutout square from the area of the circle:
     $7,0695\text{ cm}^2 - 0,81\text{ cm}^2 = 6,2595\text{ cm}^2$
     So the area of the coin is $6,2595\text{ cm}^2 \approx 6,3\text{ cm}^2$.

b) $6,2595\text{ cm}^2 \times \text{R } 3,58 = \text{R } 22,40901 \approx \text{R } 22,41$.

9. As a decorative feature, Jan builds a round window into the attic of his house.

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

a) Jan needs to paint the triangular wall around the window. What is the area of the triangular piece of wall in $\text{m}^2$? (Use the formula: $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$)

b) Assume it takes $1$ litre of paint to cover $0,5\text{ m}^2$ of wall. How many litres of paint will Jan need to buy?

c) If the hardware store only sells paint in $2$ litre tins, how many tins will Jan need to buy?

**Solution:**

a) $\text{Area of triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2}(2,63\text{ m})(2,1\text{ m}) = 2,76\text{ m}^2$

b) $2,76\text{ m}^2 \div 0,5\text{ m}^2 = 5,52\text{ litres of paint}$

c) $5,52 \div 2 = 2,76\text{ tins}$. He cannot buy $0,76$ of a tin, so he will have to buy $3$ tins.
