In this topic, learners will learn about **Financial Literacy**. They will look at:
- Posting to the Debtors Ledger and General Ledger
- The effect of credit transactions on the accounting equation

## Review of the Debtors Ledger Sources

Review the Debtors Ledger and emphasise where we get the information from:

| Document / Journal | Source Document | Entry in Ledger Column | Effect on Balance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **DJ** (Debtors Journal) | Invoice | Entry in the debit column | Add amount to the balance |
| **CRJ** (Cash Receipts Journal) | Receipt | Entry in the credit column | Subtract amount from the balance |

---

## Settlement of Debts

Debtors can settle their accounts in three ways:

- **Settle accounts on the first of the month:** For example, on 1 April, a debtor pays the previous month's account in full.
- **Settle accounts in full (up to date):** A debtor pays the full amount that he or she owes up until that day in full.
- **Settle accounts in part:** A debtor pays only a part of his or her account.

## Calculating Profit Percentage

Learners must calculate the profit percentage using the following formulas and steps:

$$\text{Profit} = \text{Sales} - \text{Cost of sales} \quad \text{(use the balance amounts)}$$

$$\text{Profit} = \text{R}41\ 400 - \text{R}34\ 500 = \text{R}6\ 900$$

$$\frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Cost Price}} \times 100 = \frac{6900}{34500} \times 100 = 20\%$$

The cost of sales for 3 July is calculated as follows:

$$2\ 880 \times \frac{100}{120} = \text{R}2\ 400$$

# 1. Credit Sales and Cash Sales

## Cash Sales
* Money is given immediately for items purchased. 
* Items are immediately exchanged during cash (This is a cash transaction).

## Credit Sales
* Items received immediately, but the money will be paid at a later stage with more interest.
* Businesses sell mainly on credit to increase their sales and cash flow in the business is improved.

# Process of someone becoming a debtor

* First, a person must complete an application form.
* The application form consists of the following:
  * Full names of the applicant
  * ID number
  * Marital status (e.g., single, married, divorced, or widowed)
  * Address or place of residence
  * Salary or wage
  * References of other credit stores (any other store where the person has an account)
  * The occupation of the person applying for the account, etc.

---

# Salary Advice and Credit Evaluation in Business

Some stores will require salary advice to assess whether an applicant is able to pay for the goods they purchase on account. 

## Key Reasons for Checking Salary Advice

1. **Affordability of the applicant:** Determines whether the customer can pay for the goods purchased. A business will not want a debtor that cannot pay them back.
2. **Proof of employment:** If someone is not genuinely employed, they will not be able to pay the money back.
3. **Debit order payments through the bank:** Used to check if someone is a good payer and if they pay on a regular basis. Businesses want a debtor that pays on time and in the correct amounts.
4. **Credit Bureau checks:** Conducted through the ID number on the salary slip. The Credit Bureau has all the information on any form of debt that is linked to your ID number—whether it is a car repayment, a loan from a bank, or store accounts—and this information will reflect on their systems.

# National Credit Act

* **The National Credit Act, Act 34 of 2005 (NCA)** was created to *protect both the consumers and businesses* and became fully operational in South Africa on 1 June 2007.

## The purpose of National Credit Act

* Educate consumers on the responsibilities of borrowing
* Ensure that money that is borrowed or lent out can be paid back.
* To protect consumers who are unfairly treated by NCA
* Provide debt counselling processes to consumers who cannot find a solution to repay their debts

---

# Revision Notes: The National Credit Act

## 1. What the National Credit Act Can Do For You

* Credit is beneficial when used responsibly.
* It provides extra purchasing power to acquire items that might otherwise be out of financial reach.
* The risk arises when debt spirals out of control quickly.
* **The key principle:** Manage your finances carefully and only borrow what you can afford to repay.

---

## 2. Credit-Worthiness

* Before a business grants credit to a consumer, it must determine if the person is **credit-worthy** (i.e., whether the individual has the financial ability and stability to pay back debts).
* To assess this, businesses examine the person's **credit record** to evaluate how previous debts were managed and repaid.
* If the business is satisfied with the assessment, both parties will enter into a formal credit agreement.

---

# Revision Notes: Credit Agreements with Debtors

## Core Concepts

A credit agreement between a business and a debtor establishes the formal terms and conditions under which credit is extended. 

Such an agreement specifies the following key elements:

* **Credit limit:** The maximum monetary amount the debtor is permitted to owe the business on any specific date.
* **Payment terms:** The agreed-upon timeframe and conditions under which the debtor must settle their account.
* **Incentives or rewards:** Discounts or benefits offered to the debtor as an encouragement for early payment.
* **Penalties:** Additional charges, such as interest, applied to the debtor's account in the event of late payment.

# The Invoice Contains the Following:

An invoice is a source document issued by a seller to a buyer, detailing the products or services provided, quantities, and agreed prices. 

According to accounting standards, a standard invoice contains the following key elements:

* Letterhead of the seller (including business name, address, contact details, and registration/tax numbers).
* Name and address of the buyer.
* Date on which the transaction took place.
* Details of the goods (i.e., description, quantities, catalog numbers, etc.).
* Prices (unit prices and total prices per item).
* Total amount of the debt.
* Value Added Tax (VAT), where applicable in the South African context.
* Terms of payment (e.g., 30 days, COD).
* Trade and cash discount allowed (if applicable).
* The abbreviation **E&OE** (Errors and Omissions Excepted), which protects the business against clerical errors.
* Special instructions if there are any (e.g., delivery instructions or banking details).

# What is a debtor?

* A **debtor** is a **Current Asset**. *He owes the enterprise money.*
* An enterprise (business) **sells goods/services to him on credit**.
* The enterprise issues the Debtor with an invoice.
* The **SOURCE DOCUMENT** is the **DUPLICATE INVOICE** (This is a duplicate invoice because the customer will always get the original invoice and the business completes the journal from the duplicate invoice, therefore it is a duplicate invoice for the business and an original invoice for the customer.)
* These transactions are recorded in the **Debtors Journal (DJ)**.
* When the Debtor settles his account or makes a part payment, the enterprise issues the debtor with a receipt (original).
* The source document, once payment is received, is the **duplicate receipt**.
* This receipt of money is recorded in the **Cash Receipts Journal (CRJ)**.

---

# The Debtors Journal (DJ)

* All transactions during which an enterprise **_SELLS ON CREDIT_** will be entered in the first book of entry, the debtors journal.
* So all goods sold on credit will be recorded in the debtors journal.
* When an enterprise issues invoices to various customers, the invoices are numbered chronologically and it is these numbers that you use for recording purposes in the Journal. (The invoices will be in chronological order meaning if we start with invoice number 501 the next invoice will be 502, 503, 504 etc.)

# Debtors Allowance Journal (DAJ)

## Concepts & Structure

* In the debtors' allowance journal, we record transactions where debtors send goods back.
* **Debtors send goods back for the following reasons:**
  * Wrong color, wrong size
  * The product is broken
  * They are not satisfied with the product
  * Or if a product is overpriced, an allowance will be given. *(The amount which is overcharged with will be written back on the debtor's account. For example, if you paid R300 for a t-shirt and it should only cost R200, the R100 that was overcharged will be credited to your account. You will now be in credit with the store.)*

* **Source Document:** When a debtor returns goods, we issue a **CREDIT NOTE**. Therefore, the source document for the DAJ is a **DUPLICATE CREDIT NOTE**. *(Remember: the customer will always get the original invoice/credit note and the business will use the duplicate to complete the journal)*.
* It is called a credit note because the customer's account must now be **credited** (decreased with that amount).

# Effect on the accounting equation

## Effect of credit sales on the accounting equation:

* When we sell goods on credit, we do not get any money, the client who bought goods owe our business money.
* Someone or another business enterprise that owes money to the business is called a **Debtor**.
* When we sell goods, we use the term **Sales** regardless of whether the goods were sold for cash or on credit. The difference between cash and credit sales are the accounts affected.
* When we sell goods for cash money is received therefore the accounts affected will be

---

## Bank and Sales

When we sell goods on credit, we do not get any money therefore an account is opened called **Debtors control**. Therefore the accounts affected will be:

* **Sales** $\longrightarrow$ Income $\longrightarrow$ Owner's Equity increases $\longrightarrow$ Credited
* **Debtors control** $\longrightarrow$ an asset $\longrightarrow$ assets Increase $\longrightarrow$ Debited

Remember every time you sell goods your trading stock account decreases, therefore a double entry is made for the cost of the goods sold.

The accounts affected will be:

* **Trading stock** $\longrightarrow$ an asset $\longrightarrow$ assets decrease $\longrightarrow$ Credited
* **Cost of sales** $\longrightarrow$ an expense $\longrightarrow$ owner's equity decreases $\longrightarrow$ debited

### WHAT IS A DEBTOR?

* A **debtor** is a **Current Asset**. He owes the enterprise money.
* An enterprise sells goods or services to him on credit.
* The enterprise issues the Debtor with an invoice.
* The **SOURCE DOCUMENT** is the **DUPLICATE INVOICE**.
* These transactions are recorded in the **Debtors Journal (DJ)**.
* When the Debtor settles his account or makes a part payment, the enterprise issues the debtor with a receipt (original).
* The source document, once payment is received, is the **duplicate receipt**.
* This receipt of money is recorded in the **Cash Receipts Journal (CRJ)**.

---

### DEBTORS JOURNAL

> All transactions during which an enterprise **SELLS ON CREDIT** will be entered in the first book of entry called the **DEBTORS JOURNAL**.

When an enterprise issues invoices to various customers, the invoices are numbered chronologically, and it is these numbers that you use for recording purposes in the Journal.

---

### WHAT IS THE MEANING OF CREDIT?

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

> **NOTE:** When you sell and provide goods or services, but you allow the customer time to pay for this at a later date. When you buy and receive goods or services, but you are allowed time to pay for this at a later date.

---

### WHAT IS A CREDIT SALE?

When your business sells goods or provides a service to a customer who does not pay immediately, but pays at a later date. 

The enterprise provides the customer (individual or business) with a credit invoice, which shows the amount which he has bought for. The customer is the **DEBTOR**. They now owe the enterprise money. The Debtor accepts the responsibility for paying the debt within a specific period. Because some debtors do not pay their accounts, many firms make provision for bad debts as they may have to write off debt (money owed to them).

#### THE SOURCE DOCUMENT FOR A CREDIT SALE IS THE "DUPLICATE CREDIT INVOICE ISSUED."

The enterprise keeps the duplicate invoice and gives the original to the customer.

# INVOICING

## THE INVOICE

An **INVOICE** is **documentary proof** of the transaction that takes place when goods or services have been purchased or sold.

* When an enterprise **SELLS** goods or provides a service, the **source document** is the **DUPLICATE INVOICE**. The **ORIGINAL** invoice is given to the customer.
* When an enterprise **BUYS** goods or services, the **source document** is the **ORIGINAL INVOICE**. The **DUPLICATE** is held by the supplier or service provider.

---

## THE TAX INVOICE

A **"TAX INVOICE"** is issued by an enterprise if it is a **registered VAT (Value Added Tax) vendor**. 

* Any person who carries on an enterprise and whose total value of taxable supplies (taxable turnover) exceeds, or is likely to exceed $R1\ 000\ 000$ ($R1\text{ mil}$) in any $12$-months period, must register for VAT.
* It may be advantageous for a person to register voluntarily if you provide goods or services to other vendors so as to allow the purchasing vendor to claim the VAT incurred on the supply.
* If you supply mainly to non-vendors (people not registered with VAT) then it would be beneficial not to register for VAT voluntarily. He is called a **VAT VENDOR**.
* VAT is charged on all goods and services provided. Certain goods and services are either **STANDARD RATED** or **ZERO RATED**.

If your business is registered as a VAT vendor:
* the VAT registration number and
* the VAT amount must be displayed on the TAX INVOICE at all times.

## LESSON ONE: In this lesson we introduce the concept of selling on credit and some of the theory that surrounds this.

Up until now we have only dealt with businesses that deal in cash, as a result you have only had to record cash transactions in the Cash Receipts and Cash Payments Journals. In this lesson we introduce businesses that sell their products for cash, as well as on **credit**.

Selling items on credit is simply defined as a ‘buy now, pay later’ situation, something that is quite common in South Africa. There are many retailers in South Africa that offer a credit option as well as a cash one.

---

### How Credit works:

Before someone is granted a credit facility or an account, their credit history and financial status is first assessed. This is to determine:
1. should they be granted credit and
2. if so, how much?

Once granted credit, the customer may then buy goods without having to pay cash immediately for them. The retailer simply records how much the customer, now referred to as a **debtor**, owes them.

The debtor is required to repay what is now owed to the retailer according to an agreed set of conditions or terms. From the point of view of the business, it is best if the money owed is collected as quickly as possible, so their cash flow is not negatively affected. **Interest** can be added to the amount owed, this is one way of encouraging the debtors to pay quickly, another is to send regular reminders using modern communication methods such as SMS and email.

# LESSON Two: The Accounting Cycle

In this lesson we look at the accounting cycle, focussing on the different stages in the cycle as well as how they relate to each other.

---

## Introduction to the Accounting Cycle

Accounting is a process through which we keep a written record of each and every transaction the business makes during a consecutive twelve month period called a **financial year**. 

Each transaction is recorded in the aptly named **books of first entry**, with the ultimate goal of summarizing all of the information into two one-page documents:
* The Income Statement
* The Balance Sheet

---

## The Financial Year

The diagram below is a linear representation of a Financial Year. Many businesses choose to use 1 March to 28 February as their financial year.

| 1 March 2012 | The Financial Year | 28 February 2013 |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| | All transactions that take place during these consecutive twelve months are recorded. | The summaries, the Income Statement and Balance Sheet are prepared right at the end of the year and include all the information from the rest of the year. |

---

## A Step-by-Step Guide to the Accounting Cycle

### Step One: The Transaction and Source Document

Source documents are the link between transactions and the accounting system. All transactions are recorded on source documents, and these source documents are then used to prepare the accounting journals (the books of first entry).

* **The source documents for the CRJ are the:**
  * Receipt
  * Cash register roll

* **The Source document for the CPJ is the:**
  * Cheque counterfoil

* **The Source document for credit transactions is:**
  * An Invoice

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

---

### Step Two: The Journals (Books of First Entry)

The journals are known as the books of first entry as they record transactions in the accounting system for the first time. Similar transactions are grouped together with the aim of organizing and summarizing the information.

---

## Step Three: The Ledger

The **General Ledger** is a further summary of the information contained within the journals. Information from the journals is posted or transferred to the General Ledger. This action brings together information kept separate in the various journals, for example, the increase and decrease in the Bank Account.

### The General Ledger is divided up into two sections:

1. **Balance Sheet Section:**
   * This includes Capital, Drawings, all asset accounts, and all liability accounts.
2. **The Nominal Section:**
   * This includes all of the Income accounts and the Expense accounts.

---

### Subsidiary Ledgers

In addition to the General Ledger, we also have subsidiary ledgers:
* **Debtors' Ledger**
* **Creditors' Ledger**

In these ledgers, each debtor and creditor will have their own account. Each individual transaction with the debtor or creditor is shown. Thus, by looking at the debtor's or creditor's ledger account, you can see how much is owed to or by the other party.

---

## Step Four: The Trial Balance

The drawing up of the Trial Balance follows on from the completion of the General Ledger. 

* **Structure:** The Trial Balance is divided up into the same two sections as the General Ledger: **The Balance Sheet Section** and **The Nominal Section**.
* **Purpose:** It is a further summary of the General Ledger where each account is represented as a one-line entry, with either a Debit or a Credit Balance or Total. It is a one-page summary of the entire financial year of the business.

---

## Step Five: The Financial Statements

Although it is a one-page summary, the Trial Balance is not a usable accounting statement. It is no indication of the financial performance of the business, nor does it present the financial position of the business. For this purpose, we have the **Financial Statements**.

---

1. The Balance Sheet

This Statement uses the Balance Sheet section of the Trial Balance to show the financial position of the business on a particular date, usually the last day of the financial year. The Balance Sheet is divided up into two sections, Assets and Owner's Equity and Liabilities. It is called a Balance Sheet, as these two sections must balance (be equal). The assets section shows us what assets the business owns, while the Owner's Equity and Liabilities Section shows us where the business got the money from to buy the assets.

The Balance Sheet is a reflection of the Accounting Equation:
$$\text{ASSETS} = \text{OWNER'S EQUITY} + \text{LIABILITIES}$$

2. The Income Statement

The Income Statement is prepared using the Nominal Section of the Trial Balance. In the Income Statement all income accounts are added together to calculate total income. Likewise, all expense accounts are added together to calculate total expenses. The total expenses amount is subtracted from the total income amount in order to calculate Net Profit for the year.

The following is a summary of the Income Statement:
$$\text{Total Income} - \text{Total Expenses} = \text{Net Profit}$$

# Revision Notes: The Double Entry of the Sales and Cost of Sales Columns

## 1. Core Concepts and Procedures

### The Double Entry of the Sales Column
* **Account Type:** The Sales account in the General Ledger is an **income account**.
* **Rule of Debit and Credit:** Income accounts increase on the **credit** side.
* **Application:** When goods are sold on credit, sales increase and are therefore **credited**.
* **Contra Account:** The corresponding account that is debited is the asset **Debtors' Control**.
* **Understanding Debtors' Control:** This asset represents the money owed to the business by customers. The more money that is owed, the larger the asset Debtors' Control grows.

### The Double Entry of the Cost of Sales Column
* **Account Type & Rule:** Handled similarly to the Cost of Sales column in the Cash Receipts Journal.
* **Trading Stock (Asset):** Because Trading Stock is being used to earn income, the business has less Trading Stock left. Therefore, we **credit** Trading Stock to decrease it.
* **Cost of Sales (Expense):** The asset is being converted into an expense. Expenses increase on the **debit** side, so we **debit** the **Cost of Sales** account.

## Activity Five: Cash and Credit Sales

### Core Concepts & Procedures

The objective of this exercise is to help you differentiate between **cash sales** and **credit sales** (sales on account). 

#### Clues to Identify Transaction Types:
1. **Wording of the transaction:** Phrases like *"for cash"* indicate a cash transaction, while *"on account"*, *"on credit"*, or naming a specific debtor indicates a credit transaction.
2. **Source Documents:** 
   * **Invoice:** Issued when goods are sold on credit.
   * **Cash Register Roll / Cash Invoice / Receipt:** Issued or generated when goods are sold for cash.

#### Mark-up Calculations:
When a business sells goods, the selling price usually includes a profit margin added to the cost price (mark-up). 
If the mark-up is given as a percentage on cost (e.g., $25\%$):

$$\text{Selling Price} = \text{Cost Price} \times \left(1 + \frac{\text{Mark-up \%}}{100}\right)$$

$$\text{Cost Price} = \text{Selling Price} \div \left(1 + \frac{\text{Mark-up \%}}{100}\right)$$

For a $25\%$ mark-up on cost:
$$\text{Cost Price} = \text{Selling Price} \times \frac{100}{125}$$
$$\text{Sales} = \text{Cost Price} \times \frac{125}{100}$$

## Incorporating Credit Sales into the Accounting System

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

**Note:** Remember – we are the ones doing the selling! So it is from our point of view as the business.

Let's begin by comparing a credit sale to a cash sale:

| | Cash Sale | Credit Sale |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Description** | We sell goods to a customer, the customer receives goods, and in return we (the retailer) receive money (payment). | We sell goods to a customer, the customer receives goods. We do not receive payment, however the customer agrees to pay us at some point in the future. The customer now owes us money, and is referred to as a **debtor**. |
| **Double entry 1** | **Dr** Bank<br>**Cr** Sales | Money is received as a result of the income sales. | **Dr** Debtors control<br>**Cr** Sales | In this case, the business is generating an income from sales, but no money is received. Instead, the promise of money in the future is received. |
| **Double entry 2** | **Dr** Cost of sales<br>**Cr** Trading Inventory | **SAME** | This records the loss of the asset trading inventory as it is used to generate and income. | **Dr** Cost of sales<br>**Cr** Trading Inventory | **SAME** | This records the loss of the asset trading inventory as it is used to generate and income. |
| **Difference** | As we can see the only difference is that money is not received in a credit transaction. Therefore the bank account is replaced with the **Debtors Control** account. This asset account represents money that will be received in the near future. | | |

## LESSON Four: Recording Receipts from Debtors

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

### Key Concepts & Structure

In accounting, every transaction is recorded in a specific journal where similar transactions are grouped together:
* **Cash Receipts Journal (CRJ):** All transactions that involve the receipt of cash are recorded here. 
* **Receipt from Debtors:** When money is received from a debtor, a receipt is issued, the transaction is recorded in the CRJ, and it is later posted to the General Ledger.

#### Ways Debtors Pay Their Accounts:
1. **Part-payment:** The debtor pays a portion of the amount owed (e.g., Debtor A owes $\text{R}1\ 900$ and pays an amount of $\text{R}1\ 000$ in part payment).
2. **Full settlement:** The debtor pays their outstanding balance in full (e.g., Debtor B owes $\text{R}850$, and the business receives an amount in full settlement of this debt). The term *'full settlement'* indicates the exact amount paid equals the debt due.

# LESSON Six

> **In this lesson we learn about buying on credit and how you record credit purchases in the Creditors' Journal**

> _'Running into debt isn't so bad. It's running into creditors that really hurts'_
> 
> — unknown

<!-- DIAGRAM_PLACEHOLDER: diagram -->

In the same way that a business sells goods on credit to debtors, businesses can also buy goods on credit. We call the business that we buy from on credit a **creditor**. This can seem a little confusing!

### Let's take another look:

We sell on Credit to a Debtor, BUT, We buy on Credit from a Creditor.

**Take note:** both of the above are **credit transactions**.

> **Words and terms:**
> 
> **Credit transactions:** This refers to any transaction that does not involve cash.

When we buy on credit we enter into an agreement where we agree to pay the supplier (now called a creditor), at a future date. (Usually between 30 and 90 days.) Therefore it is necessary for us to keep a record of what we have purchased on credit, and from whom. We also need to keep a record of how much and when we pay our creditors. This is where accounting is useful! Properly kept accounting records will tell us how much we owe each individual creditor, as well as how much we owe in total (which is necessary for **cash flow planning**).

> **Words and terms:**
> 
> **Cash flow planning:** This is the monitoring of money being received and money being paid, with the intention of making sure that there is always enough cash on hand in the business.

### Source documents:

Once again, the source document is an invoice (this is the source document for all credit transactions). However, when the business sold on credit, the invoice that we used was a duplicate of the original invoice, a carbon copy. The customer must legally always keep the original invoice. NOW, when the business buys on credit, they are the customer! Hence the business gets to keep the original invoice!

## Key Observations and Concepts

* **Comparison to the Cash Payments Journal:** The only real difference is that the **Bank** column is replaced with the **Creditors’ Control** column. This is because money is not being paid immediately for the items purchased, and so the Bank account is not affected.
* **Posting to the Ledger:** The Creditors’ Control column is posted to the **liability** account, **Creditors’ Control**, in the General Ledger.

* **Double Entry Principle:** Just like in the Cash Payments Journal, each transaction is recorded in two columns in the Creditors’ Journal: the **Creditors’ Control** column (representing **credits**) and **one other column** (representing **debits**).

# Lesson Seven: Recording Payments Made to Creditors in the Cash Payments Journal

## Introduction
This lesson covers how to record payments made to creditors in the **Cash Payments Journal (CPJ)**. 

You should already be familiar with recording entries in the Cash Payments Journal. This journal records payments made by a business using a cheque as a means of payment. 
* **Source Document:** The source document for this transaction is the **cheque counterfoil**, which is the small part of the cheque that remains in the cheque book when a cheque is used.
* **Function of the Cheque Counterfoil:** Like all source documents, it records all of the relevant information from the transaction.

---

## The Monthly Statement
Once a month, creditors normally send a statement of account to their debtors. This statement informs the debtor how much they need to pay, and by when.

### Information Normally Found on a Statement:
* The **opening balance** (amount owing from the previous month).
* **Transactions for the month** (including both purchases and returns).
* Any **payments** that may have been made during the month.
* How much the debtor needs to pay, as well as any information relating to **discounts** or **additional interest**.

---

# Lesson Eight: Posting to the General ledger from the Creditors journal and CPJ

## 1. Posting from the Creditors Journal to the General Ledger

In this example we focus on posting from the Creditors’ Journal to the General Ledger. It is important for you to note that this process is similar to the posting of the Cash Payments Journal to the General Ledger. This is due to the fact that they both record purchases. The difference being that the Creditors’ Journal records purchases on credit and not for cash.

### Posting Rule for the Creditors' Journal:
**Credit the Creditors' Control column and debit everything else.**

### The rule explained:
The total of the Creditors' Control column is posted to the credit side of the Creditors account, and all other columns and sundry entries are posted to the debit side of their specific accounts in the General Ledger.

## Key Accounting Concepts

### 1. Debtors Control
* **Classification:** Current (short-term) asset.
* **Definition:** It is an asset as it represents money that will be received in the near future (like a bank, which is also a 'store' of money).

### 2. Creditors Control and Loan: ABSA
* **Classification:** Liabilities.
* **Definition:** Both of these represent money that we owe to other businesses. They differ regarding their time frame:
  * **Creditors Control:** Represents money that we owe suppliers in the short term (**current liability**).
  * **Loan:** A **Long-Term** or **Non-current liability**.

> **Important Note:** It is important to separate these so that we can plan our cash flow—we need to have money available to pay the current liabilities!
