## Introduction

### Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **phylogenetic tree** | a diagram showing the evolutionary relationships between ancestral groups or organisms and their descendants |
| **phylum** | a taxonomic rank below **kingdom** and above **class** |
| **taxonomist** | biologists that identify and group organisms according to their characteristics |

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life on Earth. There are approximately 1,5 million recorded animal species on Earth. Animals are sorted according to their similarities and differences (Figure 1). All animals belong to the Kingdom Animalia. This kingdom is further divided into phyla (singular: phylum) which are based on differences in their basic body plan.

## Key Features of Body Plans

A body plan is structural characteristics of an organism that separates it from other organisms or groups of organisms. Important features of body plans include:
* body symmetry and cephalisation
* tissue layers
* number of gut openings
* the presence of a body cavity

### Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **cephalisation** | the presence of a definite head that contains sense organs in animals; first seen in the phylum Platyhelminthes |
| **sessile** | organisms that are immobile and attached to one place for life, e.g. sponges and barnacles |
| **gut** | portions of the alimentary canal |

## Body Symmetry and Cephalisation

An organism is symmetrical when it can be cut into two equal and identical halves through one or more plane. Multicellular organisms can be asymmetrical, radially symmetrical or bilaterally symmetrical. 

Animals that have bilateral symmetry are usually more advanced and have cephalisation. Cephalisation is when most of the sense organs, feeding appendages and the brain are near the anterior part of the body.

Animals with bilateral symmetry are usually active and are constantly moving around in search of food, mates and interacting with their environments.

## Tissue layers

The first tissue layers formed in the embryo are called germ layers. The germ layers differentiate into different organs.

---

## Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **diploblastic** | Having a body wall that is composed of two layers: the endoderm and ectoderm |
| **triploblastic** | Any organism that develops from a three-layered embryo; ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm |
| **coelom** | A fluid-filled cavity that lies between the ectoderm and endoderm and is found in triploblastic organisms |
| **hydrostatic force** | A force exerted by a liquid, usually water, increased with constriction and gravity |
| **peristalsis** | An automatic wave of muscle contraction and relaxation that moves food in one direction through the digestive tract |

## Primary Germ Layers

The primary germ layer consists of the ectoderm (outer layer) and the endoderm (inner layer).
* The ectoderm will develop into the skin or epithelium and the nervous system of the animal.
* The endoderm will form the digestive system.
* Animals that only have two germ layers (ecto- and endoderm) are called diploblastic animals (Figure 5).
* Diploblastic animals do not form complex organs and are more primitive animals.

*Figure 5: Tissue layers in embryonic development; ecto (outside), endo (inside), meso (middle)*

## Secondary Germ Layers

The mesoderm is a secondary germ layer which develops between the endoderm and the ectoderm.

| :--- | :--- |

# Coelom (body cavity)

A coelom is a body cavity that develops inside the mesoderm tissue layer in more advanced animals. Animals without a coelom are said to be **acoelomate**.

## Acoelomate

An acoelomate animal does not have a body cavity or coelom (Figure 8A).

Acoelomate animals may be either diploblastic or triploblastic. Acoelomate animals are usually smaller and are less mobile that coelomates.

## Coelomate

Coelomate animals have a body cavity or coelom in their mesodermal tissue layer (Figure 8B). However, some triploblastic organisms have a body cavity that is not surrounded by mesoderm. This cavity is called a **pseudocoel** (pseudo- means false or not real) (Figure 8C).

## Advantages of a Coelom

* Allows more complex organs to develop such as digestive organs, muscular system, blood system etc.
* Allows the creation of a hydrostatic force to be generated for movement in soft-bodied animals
* It separates the endoderm and ectoderm from each other with a cavity which allows the layers to move independently of each other. This allows **peristalsis** to occur
* In some organisms, the coelomic fluid (fluid found in the coelom) helps to transport nutrients and waste in the body

> **Note:** An animal must be triploblastic before it can be a coelomate because the coelom develops into the mesoderm.

## Animal phyla

Only six of the approximately 33 animal phyla will be discussed in this chapter. We shall use the body plans discussed in the previous section to describe the different phyla. The phyla will be discussed in order from the simplest to the more advanced.

### Key terminology

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **invertebrates** | organisms without a backbone |
| **vertebrates** | animals that have a backbone or spinal column |
| **spicule** | a minute (very small) sharp-pointed object or structure that is typically present in large numbers, found in sponges |
| **nematocyst** | a specialized cell in the tentacles of a jellyfish or other coelenterate, containing a barbed or venomous coiled thread that can be projected in self-defense or to capture prey |
| **mesoglea** | the tissue in jellyfish that functions as a hydro-static skeleton |
| **acellular** | not consisting of, or not containing cells |
| **haemocoel** | the body cavity of most invertebrates containing circulatory fluid |
| **exoskeleton** | a thick, rigid outer covering that protects and supports bodies and provides places for muscles to attach in animals, e.g. arthropods |
| **ecdysis (moulting)** | the process of casting off the outer cuticle in arthropods |

## Phylum Porifera

* **Habitat:** Aquatic (live in water).
* **Body Plan:** Asymmetrical with **no cephalisation**.
* **Level of Organisation:** Function at a cellular level.
* **Coelom Status:** Acoelomate.
* **Gut:** No openings to the gut.
* **Feeding:** Sessile organisms that feed by filtering out floating particles from the water column.
* **Support:** The body is made up of millions of spicules which protect and support the sponge.

## Phylum Cnidaria

* **Habitat:** Aquatic, mostly marine, but some live in freshwater habitats.
* **Body Plan:** Radially symmetrical with **no cephalisation**.
* **Tissue Layers:** Diploblastic, which means they have a cellular ectoderm and a cellular endoderm, separated by an acellular jelly-like layer called the **mesoglea**.
* **Coelom Status:** Acoelomate.
* **Gut:** One opening to the gut that acts as both the mouth and the anus; the mouth often has tentacles that help catch prey.
* **Body Forms:** Cnidarians occur in two different body forms:
  * A sessile **polyp** phase.
  * A free-swimming **medusa** phase.

> **Note:** Cnidarians have stinging organelles in their cells called **nematocysts** that they use for catching their prey and for protection. Cells that contain nematocysts are called **cnidocytes** or **nematocytes**.

## Phylum Platyhelminthes (Flatworms)

### Core Characteristics
* **Habitat:** Most are internal parasites, while some are aquatic and free-living.
* **Body Symmetry:** **Bilaterally symmetrical** with **cephalization** (a definite anterior, posterior, dorsal, and ventral side).
* **Body Shape:** **Dorsoventrally flattened**, meaning they appear squashed from the dorsal to the ventral side.
* **Nervous System & Cephalization:** Sense organs and nerve tissue are concentrated in the anterior region of the body (cephalization), allowing them to detect what lies ahead—aids in feeding and avoiding danger.
* **Tissue Layers:** **Triploblastic**, which allows them to develop distinct tissues and organs (e.g., nervous tissue and reproductive organs).
* **Body Cavity:** **Acoelomate** (lacking a coelom), and therefore they have **no circulatory system**.
* **Digestive System:** Only **one opening to the gut**. The digestive cavity branches around the body to transfer nutrients directly around the body.

## Phylum Annelida (Segmented Worms)

### Core Characteristics
* **Habitat:** Aquatic (freshwater and marine) and terrestrial habitats.
* **Body Symmetry:** **Bilaterally symmetrical** with **cephalization**.
* **Tissue Layers:** **Triploblastic**.
* **Body Cavity:** **Coelomate**—the coelom is a fluid-filled cavity that functions as a **hydrostatic skeleton** for movement.
* **Body Structure:** **Segmented**, meaning their bodies consist of repeating segments called **metameres**.

# Phylum Arthropoda

* **Habitat:** Aquatic (freshwater and marine) (Figure 15) and terrestrial.
* **Symmetry:** Bilaterally symmetrical with **cephalisation** (development of a head region).
* **Germ layers:** Triploblastic (three primary germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm).
* **Coelom:** Coelomate – the coelom is filled with a fluid, which acts like blood, called a **haemocoel**. Arthropods therefore have an **open circulatory system**.
* **Segmentation:** More advanced segmentation of body parts so that each segment has a particular form and function (e.g., abdomen, thorax, and head).
* **Appendages:** All arthropods have **jointed appendages** that are used for movement and feeding.
* **Gut:** Two openings to the gut (a through-gut).
* **Exoskeleton:** A waterproof **exoskeleton** made of chitin.

### The Exoskeleton:
* Protects the arthropod from drying out.
* Prevents diffusion of gases across its waterproof surface; arthropods have therefore developed specialised gaseous exchange organs such as gills and lungs.
* Does not grow as the arthropod grows and it must be shed regularly (**ecdysis**) and regrown; the arthropod is vulnerable during regrowth of the exoskeleton because it is weaker and requires a lot of energy.

# Phylum Chordata

* **Habitat:** Aquatic (freshwater and marine) and terrestrial.
* **Symmetry:** Bilaterally symmetrical with cephalisation.
* **Germ layers:** Triploblastic.
* **Coelom:** Coelomate.
* **Segmentation:** Segmented body.
* **Gut:** Two openings to the gut (through-gut).

# Revision Notes: Vertebrates and Circulatory Systems

## 1. Characteristics of Vertebrates

Vertebrates (and chordates) share several key anatomical and physiological features during their development or lifetime:

*   **Hollow Dorsal Tubular Nerve Cord:** Forms the spinal column, which often expands at the anterior end to form the brain (e.g., humans).
*   **Pharyngeal Gill Slits:** Structures that develop initially and may persist as gills or disappear in terrestrial chordates at adulthood.
*   **Post-Anal Tail:** A muscular tail extending posterior to the anus.
*   **Thermal Regulation:** Chordates can be classified by how they regulate body temperature:
    *   **Ectothermic:** Body temperature is regulated by the external environment.
    *   **Endothermic:** Body temperature is regulated internally by metabolic reactions (e.g., humans and birds).

---

## 2. Circulatory Systems

Animals utilize different types of circulatory systems to transport nutrients, gases, and wastes throughout their bodies.

### Comparison of Circulatory Systems

| Feature | Open Circulatory System | Closed Circulatory System |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Transport Fluid** | Haemolymph (similar to blood) | Blood |
| **Vessel Containment** | Fluid does not stay entirely inside vessels; flows into the haemocoel (body cavity). | Blood is entirely contained within a continuous network of blood vessels. |
| **Fluid Movement** | Diffuses from sinuses around organs back into the heart. | Pumped continuously through closed vessels (including small branch vessels in organs). |
| **Typical Example** | Insects | Mammals |

# Surface Area to Volume

The surface area to volume ratio refers to the surface area of an organism in relation to its volume.

* Larger organisms have less surface area per volume than smaller organisms. This is important to understand when comparing the size of an organism (volume) and their gaseous exchange surfaces (surface area).
* The surface area to volume ratio will also affect the rate of heat gain or loss from the environment.
* Larger animals usually require specialised breathing structures (e.g. lungs) to increase their gaseous exchange surface area.
* Smaller organisms are able to use their body’s surface area to bring gases into the body (e.g. Platyhelminthes, Annelida).

# Decomposition

**Decomposition** is the process that decays or breaks down organic molecules from dead organisms into simpler organic molecules that are released into the environment and reused in nutrient cycles.

* **Invertebrates** (worms, beetles, etc.) break down complex organic molecules (**detritus**), such as leaf litter, into simpler molecules.
* **Microscopic decomposers** (i.e. bacteria and fungi) can further break down the organic matter into **humus**.
* **Humus** is the organic part of soils which greatly improves the quality of soils for plants.

---

# Soil aeration

Invertebrates like earthworms, burrow in the soil and make tunnels through the soil (Figure 20). These tunnels allow gases to move through and aerate the soil. The activities of earthworms are important because:

* Their tunnels accelerate the decomposition of nutrients to be reused for plant growth
* The community structure of the habitat is dependent on soil nutrients and plant growth
* Their tunnels improve drainage of the soil
* The earthworms act as pumps when they move through the tunnels by pushing and pulling air around their tunnels
* Their tunnels loosen the soil and allow plant roots to penetrate deeper into the soil
